This feature is not available in Modern Warfare 4, but it's possible this means the operator skins added in the post-launch updates will remain thematically appropriate. Black Ops 7 did scale back the skins from Black Ops 6, but it still featured some colorful anime-style bundles and a RoboCop operator. Many players found the addition of the Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles and cel-shaded skins of animated characters to be off-putting, and this new filter allows Activision to find some middle ground to appease a split playerbase. However, it does risk muting the appeal of buying a skin--a key part of CoD's business--if players know they won't be able to use it while playing with everyone they encounter. In Control Resonant, It’s The Tiniest Details That Continue To Matter MostNEW

Control is one of my favorite games ever. Six years after release, one of the things that sticks with me the most is a pencil.  Within your first few seconds of playing, you happen upon your first memo. It's located at a security checkpoint and outlines a number of items that are not permitted inside the government building you've just entered. These seem expected enough: laptops, weapons, various smart devices, and so on. The final two list items sound much stranger: one being No. 2 pencils.  It sounds almost like a prank. Certainly, I did not expect any payoff for those words, which are located at the end of one of the many memos that populate The Oldest House, the setting of the game. Here is a giant building in the middle of New York, housing a clandestine government agency responsible for managing the supernatural, and a pencil is a problem for these people? So it was a treat many hours later when I reflected on that seemingly throwaway bullet point, after developing an understanding of the way archetypal items can transform into dangerous objects, and why No. 2 pencils would thus be banned by the Federal Bureau of Control. The fact that the explanation requires you to piece these things together, rather than being overtly explained, makes it that much more satisfying. The Oldest House is full of weird stuff, so it would be easy to forget all about that tidbit from the very beginning of the game, or for it to indeed have been nothing more than a joke. And yet it's precisely that connection back to such a small detail that makes me adore Control so much. The world-building is accomplished not just through overt lore dumps or cutscenes, but through small details that are interspersed so subtly that you could easily miss them.  Fortunately, Control Resonant also delivers in this regard. I was reminded of that No. 2 pencil memo a few hours into the game when Zoe, your FBC handler, threatens a fellow FBC agent with "fridge duty." On its own, this again sounds like a joke--given the years-long lockdown of The Oldest House, the prospect of having to clean out the fridges inside sounds both tedious and disgusting. Secret government agents being stuck on cleaning duty sounds like a silly threat. Good goof, Zoe. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f1LEHPr8e_o Having been trained by Control to catalog every last detail in my mind, I kept playing, wondering if that would lead to some payoff. In discussing this with my colleague Mark Delaney (who scored Resonant a 9 in our review), he made me realize I wasn't thinking widely enough--fridge duty clearly refers back to the first game. A side quest called Fridge Duty involves an Altered Item, a supernatural fridge, that must be stared at--without blinking--in order to keep it pacified; you get to see the gruesome aftermath when someone fails in the task. So, Zoe dangling the prospect of fridge duty is actually more of a serious threat than you'd otherwise realize. Does this matter at all in the grand scheme of things? Will you better understand protagonist Dylan Faden's redemption story, what happened to my precious Dr. Darling, or how this all expands on the Remedy Connected Universe? Not in the slightest. And yet, it's this callback, more than most combat encounters or larger plot beats, that I expect I will remember years from now, and that's no slight against the game's very enjoyable action or story. By encouraging you to actively engage with every detail you come across, Remedy is able to build a deeply rich world that gives you as much as you're willing to put into it. These details don't make or break the experience, and you'll certainly be able to understand what's going on without them. But it's these morsels that I find so tantalizing, and I love the way you're rewarded for obsessing over any given thing--I'll never forget the "aha!" moment in Control when I pieced together the true nature of the NSC, ostensibly a power plant for The Oldest House, which--like everything in the building--is more nuanced and more sinister than it first appears.. Despite being my most-anticipated game of the year, I'm progressing relatively slowly through Control Resonant, exploring every nook and analyzing every document. It's going to take me a while to see through the full story that I'm eager to see, and yet I wouldn't have it any other way.

After this last session with Control, I want to propose the following: Every single-player game with combat (barring some exceptions) should have this Immortality toggle. Turning on Immortality allowed me to experience Control fresh, without the burden of banging my head against difficult opponents or bosses. It remained exhilarating to be knee-deep in Control’s combat, with Remedy’s signature destruction and dazzling array of abilities. By the end of the game, main character Jesse Faden had turned into a powerful superhero: She flies, psychically controls enemies, and turns any object into a telekinetically throwable weapon. In a recent blog post, lead gameplay designer Sergey Mohov detailed how Remedy opted to offer individual toggles and adjustable modifiers, including Immortality, instead of global difficulty options such as "easy," "medium," and so on. What does having Immortality toggled on look like in play? In Control, Jesse could still take hits and lose health, but it would never reach zero, and thus never forcing you to die and restart from a checkpoint. Being on the brink of death darkens the screen, muting the sound and restricting your ability to react efficiently. All you’re doing is saving me from reloading a nearby checkpoint and suffering through a loading screen and possibly unskippable dialogue and cutscene. But even when Control Resonant stumbles (and at times frustrates), its world and story drew me back in, further cementing it as one of my favorite universes in gaming.Seven years after FBC director Jesse Faden sealed the Hiss – along with herself, her allies, and many FBC employees – within the bureau’s headquarters, the Oldest House, a new, more devastating paranatural occurrence has bled beyond its walls. Manhattan has been distorted into a mind-bending version of itself that threatens the very fabric of reality. That the Hiss has escaped into the city and possessed many citizens who weren’t lucky enough to evacuate doesn’t help. Jesse has mysteriously left the FBC, leaving her supernaturally gifted and long-imprisoned brother, Dylan, to pick up the pieces. Thankfully, for a guy who spent years in a coma, he does a great job walking in his sister’s sizable shoes. After he played the role of the possessed and tortured damsel in distress in Control, I’m impressed by how likable Dylan is in Resonant. His awkward, socially inept earnestness and drive to atone for past sins make him a softer and sympathetic lead – a nice juxtaposition of his power that causes many to vilify him as a monster – and I especially love his dynamic with his charismatic FBC handler Zoe De Veras. The duo’s entertaining choice-driven banter adds weight, levity, and humor to a wild story. I also enjoyed being reacquainted with returning favorites such as obsessed scientist Emily Pope and altered item supervisor Frederick Langston. I always looked forward to receiving a new message from someone or visiting them to learn details about the seven-year lockdown, Jesse’s hypothetical fate, and the evolving state of New York.Cool moments, including surprising and even heartbreaking revelations, plus a few left-field mechanical and visual twists, prop up an overall strong narrative. Fascinating, often meaty sidequests also make good use of Resonant’s premise: ordinary citizens experiencing the paranatural for the first time. I was delightfully surprised by the variety of stories and gameplay in missions involving singing along with a malevolent karaoke machine, escaping a taxi-filled pocket dimension by solving “spot the difference” visual puzzles, or chasing down an FBC employee’s temperamental supernatural pet. Exploring Manhattan’s open hubs for longer mission strings, such as uncovering a secret organization's activities and completing abandoned FBC experiments, made me excited to bust into every identically designed laundromat, apartment building, and cafe. Unfortunately, these quests are accompanied by lesser, repetitive tasks like escorting trapped rangers or fending off enemy waves to protect assets. In a game with such well-crafted missions, these paper-thin tasks feel out of place and like obligatory additions to pad out the world. Thankfully, you can ignore these lesser jobs to little consequence, but you shouldn’t overlook every optional diversion, and not just because they’re largely good.  Control Resonant doesn’t politely suggest you complete its quests; it sternly demands compliance. Doing so strengthens you enough to survive the often overwhelming character-action-style melee combat encounters. Dylan wields a transforming weapon called the Aberrant, a fun upgradable tool that can morph into a one-handed axe, dual sword-like weapons, or a two-handed scythe. Each is entertaining in its own way and is accompanied by less fun, semi-useful sidearms, like a heavy axe or a weirdly sluggish whip. While the side weapons don’t feel as good or as powerful to handle, combo finishers add nice exclamation points to the otherwise basic attack combos. I like rocking foes with a barrage of reinforced punches, slicing them with a hovering spinning blade, or knocking them back using a giant glaive-like weapon. The unexpected star of combat may be Dylan’s swift perfect dodge, which always feels great to execute and chain into an attack. Combat begins basic and repetitive but becomes more mechanically interesting and strategic as you unlock new supernatural abilities. Igniting foes with explosive fire attacks, poisoning them using rapid-fire mold spores, summoning abstract entities to fight alongside Dylan, or simply force-pushing enemies into hazards and off ledges helps spice up the basic attack strings. Mastering these tools matters because Control Resonant can be an infuriatingly challenging game. Enemies often level alongside you, meaning you rarely overpower them. Unfortunately, this often means chipping away at sturdy health bars in large, wave-based battles that usually last longer than necessary. I’m also lukewarm on Resonant’s system of killing big enemies to win fights, as smaller foes respawn non-stop until their larger counterparts fall. While it adds enjoyable intensity at times, it can also make more challenging battles feel like lengthy wars of attrition. As powerful as Dylan is, I wish Control Resonant’s balancing eased up more often so I could fully experience the power fantasy it tries to present.Boss battles against disturbingly distorted humans called Resonants offer entertaining, more evenly designed tests. I also appreciate how Remedy shook up encounters by introducing enemy types besides just variations of the Hiss, such as mold monsters and another faction I won’t spoil. While battles still eventually fall into repetitive territory due to how often you fight bands of baddies roaming Manhattan, at least there’s a deeper enemy pool to wade through. Despite being set in an open Manhattan, its structure resembles the Oldest House in the first game in that you must learn abilities and gain keys to access areas and expedite travel. The path to an objective isn’t always obvious, requiring careful observation to find obscure openings, but fun abilities like warping to rooftops or hovering across large distances make getting around a blast. Mind-bending imagery makes Manhattan feel like a surrealist painting in some spots and a sci-fi novel’s cover art in others, adding nice visual variety beyond standard cityscapes. However, navigating visually trippier areas that you can approach from multiple angles can be a tricky, headache-inducing proposition.Despite my annoyances, they always faded as I bolted toward the next story mission or mysterious question mark on the map. Depending on how much you engage with Control Resonant’s offerings, it will either be an oppressive action game with great narrative beats and presentation or just a pretty tough action game with all the worldbuilding fixins. Remedy mostly nails its first attempt at stylish action, but the misses are apparent and disappointing. The remaining package has me eager to uncover every remaining secret even after rolling credits. As someone most excited to be awash in Control’s lore again, I’m glad that aspect of this sequel resonates most effectively. Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
