Super Smash Bros. Was The Best Thing To Ever Happen To Fire EmblemNEW
Booting up Fire Emblem: Fortune’s Weave had me feeling nostalgic. Fire Emblem as it is now would’ve been surreal to six-year-old me, who only knew “Marth and Roy from Smash.” Back then, Fire Emblem wa...
Booting up Fire Emblem: Fortune’s Weave had me feeling nostalgic. Fire Emblem as it is now would’ve been surreal to six-year-old me, who only knew “Marth and Roy from Smash.” Back then, Fire Emblem was still a niche series in the west and many of us had no idea who they were. Little did I know, these swordsmen would just be two of many Fire Emblem heroes to join the fighting game series, and Fire Emblem games would reach mainstream popularity. I discovered Fire Emblem in the early 2000s when I first started playing Super Smash Bros. Melee on our clunky big-screen TV in the basement. It was my favorite game to play with my cousins outside of having them watch me play single-player games like Spyro: Enter the Dragonfly and The Legend of Zelda: Wind Waker. We played Melee for hours that summer of 2001, eventually unlocking Marth, the blue-haired prince from the 1990's Fire Emblem: Shadow Dragon and the Blade of Light. After that, we unlocked Roy, the protagonist of 2002's Fire Emblem: The Binding Blade.  Marth and Roy do have voicelines, but they are all in Japanese. I mostly remember just the grunting sounds they would make from swinging around their swords, anyway. Maybe it’s because of that that the Smash fandom invented personalities for both fighters in fanfics and comics until their games were released in the west and set things straight. Link also showed up in many of these creations, maybe because he’s the only other Super Smash swordsman from that era.  Most of the fan-made stories that I remember from that time came from Fanfiction.net and Deviantart. CallistoHime, the author of fanfics like Time Cannot Erase, admits that she filled in the blanks because she had little to go off of besides Marth’s Smash Bros. profile. One artist I followed back then, Katie Tiedrich, also made a comic about the gap between her interpretation of Marth and the real thing. These are just a few examples from what was a relatively small community in the mid-to-late 2000s. Nintendo Magazine cites Marth and Roy’s inclusion in Smash Bros. as a driving reason to release the Fire Emblem games in the west, starting with Fire Emblem: The Binding Blade, where Roy was the protagonist. In an Iwata Asks interview, Fire Emblem producer Tohru Narihiro even thanked Masahiro Sakurai for his work on Smash Bros. because of the fighting game franchise's impact on the international popularity of the previously niche RPG series.“Thanks to your work, the reputations of Marth and Ike have grown beyond our expectations,” he said (2008's Super Smash Bros. Brawl had already come out when he made this comment, and 2005's Fire Emblem: Path of Radiance protagonist Ike was included in the roster instead of Roy). Since the addition of Marth and Roy in Melee, adding new Fire Emblem characters to Smash has become somewhat of a tradition for the fighting game series. As mentioned earlier, Ike joined the roster in Brawl. Then, 2014's Smash 4 not only saw Roy return to the roster, but it also added Fire Emblem Awakening's Lucina and Robin. 2018's Ultimate really reflects how popular Fire Emblem was becoming, including all aforementioned characters and then also adding Awakening's Chrom, Fates' Corrin, and Three Houses' Byleth.  As Fire Emblem's presence in the Smash roster has grown, so has the popularity (and quality!) of the games. The first Fire Emblem to be localized for international audiences, The Blazing Blade, released in 2003. Several years (and Fire Emblem releases) later, Fire Emblem: Shadow Dragon would come out in 2009, and the fanbase outside Japan finally got to see Marth’s backstory. Rather than the moody prince we imagined, Marth is a kindhearted leader who is looking for allies to assist him in taking back his kingdom from an evil dragon.  At the time, I was less enthusiastic about learning about Marth and more locked into Shadow Dragon's gameplay of training units and strategically placing them on the map during battle. Marth was the catalyst for me to pick up my first Fire Emblem game, but the gameplay itself is what I fell in love with. It still wasn’t good enough to rocket the series into critical acclaim, but thanks to the growing popularity of Fire Emblem, we steadily got more and more games in the series.  Looking back, Shadow Dragon isn’t even what I would consider “one of the good ones” when it comes to Fire Emblem. The five Fire Emblem games that came out between Melee and Shadow Dragon--The Binding Blade, The Blazing Blade, The Sacred Stones, Path of Radiance, and Radiant Dawn--didn’t garner much attention from the mainstream. The shift from niche to noteworthy happened when Fire Emblem: Awakening introduced a fully fleshed out support system and life-sim gameplay that hooked me on its characters.  Then, Fire Emblem: Three Houses further improved this formula with a branching narrative that actually felt cohesive and morally complicated--so much so that players still fight about which of the titular three houses was “right” years later. Fire Emblem: Fortune’s Weave refines this formula even farther, with a way to easily swap between the stories of its four protagonists.  This isn't to say that all of the games have been hits since Awakening. I wanted to love Fates, which also featured a branching narrative with DLC branches that you needed to buy, but the conflict was black and white without much room for moral interpretation and Corrin was pacifistic to a fault. FE3H was essentially everything I wanted from Fates but didn’t get. However, at this point, Fire Emblem sells itself. In recent years, Fire Emblem: Three Houses was given the Player’s Voice Award at The Game Awards 2020. Fire Emblem: Fortune’s Weave is equally if not more ambitious, earning a 9/10 in GameSpot's review and an 89 on Metacritic. It’s now common to find players who have discovered the series through the sheer push of its own popularity rather than piggybacking off the advertising of a popular fighting game.  It’s been over 25 years since Marth and Roy debuted in Melee and, even now, watching how much Fire Emblem blew up over it is an overwhelming feeling.

Helen Chiang, the veteran Xbox staffer who got promoted in July and left the company amid mysterious circumstances just recently, has shared her side of the story--but her response doesn't close the book on this saga. In a LinkedIn post titled "Clarity is Kindess," which itself is a reference to her former boss (and Xbox CEO) Asha Sharma's much-ridiculed and cringey mantra, said it's "not accurate" that she was "fired for a policy violation" as multiple reports said. Chiang said she will not "turn this into a play-by-play" and reveal the specifics, but she wanted to speak out to dispel rumors. "What has been said about my departure, and what has been left unsaid, has created a picture of me that I hardly recognize." Xbox is undergoing a "reset" currently. She went on to "set the record straight" by claiming she did not leak confidential company information to the press as some had speculated. "I have never communicated with any journalist outside the limited, company-authorized interviews I’ve done as part of my job," she said, adding that this includes having no contact with any journalist who reached out to her before today to ask for her side of the story. Chiang went on to say she did not leave Xbox to join a competitor. "You don’t spend two decades somewhere without developing a deep loyalty to the people, teams, and businesses you’ve helped build," she said "Xbox was never just another stop in my career. My end game was always to keep building here," she said.   That may all be true, but it's also unquestionable that people regularly spend lengthy periods of their career at one company, in high-level positions, and then move to other companies. Third, Chiang said she "did not engage in discrimination, bullying, harassment, insider trading, embezzlement, or other fraudulent activity." "I stand by my record: both the results I delivered and the way I have treated people throughout my career. I won’t let speculation define my character or erase that record," she said. Chiang added that she did not leave Xbox because she was "passed over for a bigger role." She said that since having children, she became more "intentional about how I define ambition and success." She said she has purposefully not sought out bigger roles or jobs with more responsibilities; she said she hadn't interviewed for a job since 2011. "My reputation and results paved the way for the opportunities that followed. I went where the company believed I was needed and where I could have the greatest impact," she said, adding that she did not ask to become Xbox's first-ever COO. "I believe in fairness, accountability, and holding ourselves to the standards we expect of others. Those standards only mean something when they’re applied consistently," she said. Chiang said she has "serious concerns" about how those standards were applied in her case, and she said those concerns "deserve to be taken seriously." "I share this because I don’t want my departure reduced to an inaccurate story about incompetence, ambition for the next big job, or a Nelson-level scandal. My story is about staying true to your core values, especially when it is hard," she said. "That’s the legacy I hope to leave behind." Chiang's statement doesn't spell out the specific nature surrounding her departure from Xbox, so many will naturally want more answers. Those could come in time, as Chiang said this is "not the whole story." Another former Xbox executive, Shannon Loftis, commented on Chiang's post to say she is "Team Helen all the way." Loftis said she wonders who may have leaked the "false information" about Chiang and what their motivations may have been. In any event, she says it shows a "loss of moral character and 'clarity' in leadership at Xbox." "A real leadership team would have relentlessly pursued the 'leaker' and taken appropriate action because 'confidential personnel matters' actually are bases for defamation lawsuits," she said. "It's a clownshow up in there and I feel terrible for anyone that still has to deal with it." A spokesperson for Microsoft told Windows Central that the company does not “discuss individual personnel matters.” According to a source speaking to Eurogamer, Chiang was “terminated for policy violations.” The Verge’s Tom Warren backed this up, saying Chiang was cut loose due to a “policy violation,” but the exact nature of that is unknown. In July, Sharma announced that Chiang would become Xbox’s first-ever COO, with “end-to-end” responsibility for the financial future of Xbox’s content, hardware, platform, and services. Chiang had worked at Microsoft for 20 years, in that time playing a key role in the creation of Xbox Live and later shepherding Mojang and the lucrative Minecraft franchise. As COO, Chiang was expected to “bring our businesses together under one operating model, making sure we make clear investment decisions, learn from our successes and failures, and hold ourselves accountable for results,” Sharma said. For her part, Sharma said she will have an update to share on the COO position at Xbox at some point, but she didn’t divulge more. The Xbox business was “not healthy” when Sharma took over, she said, and Chiang no doubt had a challenging road ahead of her. As for now, however, people can only speculate as to the real reason she left Xbox.

Virtue And A Sledgehammer Marcus Stewart This Devolver Digital-published game has a simple but head-turning premise. You’re a profoundly angry woman who returns to her small hometown armed with nothing but a sledgehammer, which she uses to smash everything in sight. A big part of Virtue and a Sledgehammer’s allure is its fascinating and well-written tale about the protagonist’s strained relationships with her sister and mother. Titanium Court On Switch 2 Marcus Stewart This bizarre indie blends fun match-3 puzzle-solving with tower defense gameplay wrapped around a funny but surreal story. The match-3 gameplay involves sliding tiles to match geographic elements like castles, trees, and rivers to collect resources that feed into the tower defense portion. Depending on how the board looks when the puzzle portion ends, you send troops (sustained by the resources you collected) to attack/defend against rival castles and gather more items; board positioning determines how well this can go. The two mechanics feed into each other to create a fun loop that grows in complexity as new mechanics are introduced. Did I mention the game is also very weird? The silly tale centers on a medieval court designating you the chosen savior to rescue their lost queen (and many other tasks), and the dumb tone adds personality to a sometimes dry genre. Titanium Court charmed and impressed us when it launched on PC earlier this year, and now console and mobile players get their chance to hold court. Call of Duty is a household name for first-person shooters, and Activision has released yearly titles for over two decades. Some entries of the franchise were obscure and platform-specific games, but most Call of Duty titles have been major fall releases across multiple platforms. The first few Call of Duty games focused on World War II and authenticity as a military shooter, but over the years the franchise has grown in complexity and often leaned away from a mil-sim design. Call of Duty has evolved with third game modes, robots, spaceships, jetpacks, and sometimes mind-bending fictional campaigns that steer more toward sci-fi and fantasy than the game's initial military roots. The first Call of Duty game was developed by Infinity Ward, but today's current games are a collaborative effort across multiple Call of Duty studios: Treyarch, Raven Software, Infinity Ward, Sledgehammer Games, Beenox, Demonware, High Moon Studios, and more. Below we list all of the Call of Duty games in order of release. Call of Duty The very first Call of Duty was originally a PC game released in 2003, but an enhanced port titled Call of Duty: Classic was later developed by a separate studio and released worldwide in November 2009. Developed by Infinity Ward, Call of Duty put players in the shoes of three infantry soldiers in World War II. This single-player campaign was squad-based, pairing the player with AI-teammates for missions featuring the American, British, and Soviet forces. Call of Duty 2: Big Red One was available for GameCube, Xbox One, and PlayStation 2. This was another first-person military shooter set during World War II, but it differed from the previous Call of Duty campaigns because Big Red One only featured one protagonist, with the storyline focusing on a single soldier from the American 1st Infantry Division. This division is historically known as the "Big Red One" and became the most recognized U.S. Army formation of World War II. The campaign missions covered the division's part in North Africa, the invasion of Sicily, storming the beaches of Normandy on D-Day, and eventually the push into Germany. Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare was an award-winning title, and it featured a single-player campaign that allowed players to bounce between perspectives of the U.S. Marines and the British S.A.S. in a modern war story against a global threat of a rogue enemy faction.
