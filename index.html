Despite the neon lights, futuristic technology, and the promise of a brighter future, the entire cyberpunk genre lives on the fact that, in reality, no one should want to live in this world. Games like Cyberpunk 2077 don’t just give you awesome enhancements; they also tackle identity and a corporate-run city that controls nearly every aspect of anyone unfortunate enough to live here. Nivalis Nights doesn’t remove those traits, but instead, it gives you the perspective of someone normal. Best of all, this slice-of-life simulation game is at a discount just after launching, meaning now’s the best time to give it a try. You start your life in Nivalis in your apartment, before suddenly being offered the chance to own your own noodle bar by the previous owner, who is a little secretive about why he’s giving up his livelihood. After that, you’re tasked with getting some stock in for the shop--thanks for leaving it empty, old owner--and going from a lowly nobody to a nobody who runs a pretty decent restaurant. You’re not V, trying to make a name for yourself in Nivalis like a legend from Night City. Instead, it’s all about making friends (or enemies) with vendors, restaurant owners, and other businesses in the area. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lZJHgkSGY2E Despite a curfew being in place, a serial killer prowling the neon-drenched streets, and the fact that you--or another business owner--can become a saboteur to take down rival companies, the overall experience is rather cozy. Rain patters against the sidewalk as you take in the sights, get called ugly by someone livestreaming (but who, when confronted, says she’s not livestreaming), and inevitably steal meal ideas from other restaurant owners. It’s like if Stardew Valley grew up and had a baby with Cyberpunk 2077, but the former removed some of the latter’s more irredeemable qualities. Sure, it may not be as comforting as some of the best cozy games, but it has the same feeling at its core. The visual style pulls you in, with bright colors popping despite the somewhat low-poly look of Nivalis Nights’ graphics. Turning your initial business from a zero-review place into something that deserves a spot in Meridian Market is engaging and challenging, and yet it’s just dipping your toes into Nivalis’ waters. Nivalis Nights just launched, but if you don’t want to pay full price, you can buy it for $22.49 / £19.79 at Fanatical, which gets you a Steam key for less than the listed price. Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: S-Game Developer: S-Game Release: October 29, 2026 I played Phantom Blade Zero at Gamescom in Cologne, Germany, back in August, which, at the speed of the internet, means it feels like I had my hands on the controller about five years ago. I also made sure to take thorough notes because I knew it would be some time before I could write about it.For my Phantom Blade Zero play session two months ago, developer S-Game Studio offered up the opportunity to basically play from the beginning of the game, encouraging a long play session. I played through the intro and initial tutorials, meeting protagonist Soul, and learned some of his backstory.  A major selling point for Phantom Blade Zero since its reveal has been its use of motion capture for its animation. When our own Wesley LeBlanc visited the studio in China last year, they put him in the motion capture rig to showcase how important the motion capture process was to the game’s development. My admittedly dismissive reaction to this selling point has always been… all modern triple-A games use motion capture for gameplay and cutscenes. I have seen video game performers like Troy Baker dressed in grey leotards more than I have seen him dressed in clothes whenever behind-the-scenes footage of game development is shared. What’s the big deal?I am not fully removed from that camp, if I am being honest, but I admit that getting my hands on Phantom Blade Zero and controlling the movement and action did highlight the overall smoothness of playing what is essentially a Chinese martial arts film. It really does feel like you’re playing a game that borrows the beauty of human movement showcased in movies like Ang Lee’s Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon. Acrobatic dance applied to the art of swinging a sword. After completing an opening combat encounter, it is revealed that Soul suffered a heart wound and a healer cryptically named The Flesh Sculptor gave him a replacement: a tainted heart that will only let him live for 66 days. There is no QTE, however, to save the horses as the carriage careens off a cliff and crashes in a valley. When Soul comes to following the crash, I am surprised to see the dead horses hanging from the trees. A gruesome image now etched onto the map screen presumably serves as a memorable visual reminder of this location, but I can’t help but feel bad for the poor animals. You push up on the control stick and wait. If you’re lucky, sometimes you can press the sprint button to make your character climb a little faster, or slide down. That’s been the best-case scenario for decades. Soul, however, bounds up ladders like Spider-Man sprinting up the side of a building. Echo Weaver Review - Discovery Joy And Challenging PlatformingNEW
Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Akupara Games Developer: Moonlight Kids Release: October 8, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ Like most games born of Metroid, Echo Weaver’s inspiration is obvious. You explore and fill out a large, continuous map in a mysterious location and your abilities expand as you progress. Its other presumed inspiration, however, Outer Wilds, only makes itself apparent as you dig deeper into the unique structure of the game. You are stuck in a time loop in Echo Weaver, and it dictates what you can do and what you know about your suite of abilities. You don’t unlock new traversal moves and expanded weapons as much as you remember you literally had them all along. It makes the reward of discovery powerful in Echo Weaver, but it also creates frustrating barriers to progress. As the titular Weaver, the character basically knows as much as the player holding the controller. You have a boomerang-like glaive you throw primarily to solve puzzles, and though it can be used to attack enemies, you won’t encounter very many. You also quickly learn that every few minutes, a vague calamity strikes, and you are forced back in time to start over. You keep your map progress and obviously everything that you’ve learned, and that’s more than enough to make death or resets not feel too unfair.What really sets Echo Weaver apart is that many of your most useful abilities are not ones that you unlock, but rather ones you learn exist at some point along the way. For example, you can set your glaive on fire from the moment you start the game, but you won’t learn how until you’ve made significant progress. Mechanically, this is basically how every Metroid-inspired game works: Make enough progress and you will be able to do a new thing. But learning the often complicated procedures necessary for these later abilities that you could always do makes the reveal all the more rewarding. You weren’t given the ability to set your glaive on fire – you figured it out yourself. And that’s a good feeling.This all plays into the time loop structure, too. The shortcuts needed to get to certain areas so you have more time to explore them were there all along – you just didn’t know how to see them. It’s why Echo Weaver feels more like a complicated puzzle box than a search action game where you’re killing the same creatures over and over, and I appreciate how that sets it apart from other games in the genre. When you’re figuring things out and making good progress, it feels like you’re working in tandem with the time loop. But on the occasions when you’re not solving the puzzles you need, which is frequent, it can be frustrating. Running out of time when you’re trying to explore or just figure one last thing out becomes a burden, and the best course of action is often to just reset the time loop (essentially killing yourself), which feels like giving up in a moment that you don’t want to.The worst moments of the time loop coming to a close are when you’re engaged in a complicated platforming gauntlet. Echo Weaver’s greatest shortcoming is that its jumping doesn’t feel great. Leaping is solid for general exploration, but when you’re trying to make it through thickets of thorns and endless pits that steal your health (and time), the action struggles to keep up with the demand. And when you’ve finally made it through and time loops just before you hit that checkpoint, it can be incredibly disheartening. Though resetting and trying again is built into the game’s design so it doesn’t become too much of a burden, I did hit a handful of distracting bugs primarily related to areas not loading properly when I fast-traveled to them.Echo Weaver’s unique approach to the Metroid structure is admirable, unique, and well executed. The sense of discovery is fantastic, and though I realistically probably won’t immediately play the game again, the idea that I could start with knowledge of basically all of the abilities to rip through the game at high speed is an enticing one. Developer Moonlight Kids puts a lot of trust in the player to figure out how it all works, and the reward is palpable. That trust leads to some frustrated, aimless wandering, and the platforming challenges occasionally venture into feeling unfair, but by the end you will feel like a genius. That’s a good feeling to leave a game with. Score: 7.75 About Game Informer's review system
