All Hail Blood Gulch, Halo’s Greatest Multiplayer MapNEW
After 25 years, Halo can be remembered for many things. It's among the best launch games of all time, which was pivotal in making Xbox a serious console contender. Its campaign was so brilliantly desi...
After 25 years, Halo can be remembered for many things. It's among the best launch games of all time, which was pivotal in making Xbox a serious console contender. Its campaign was so brilliantly designed, from set pieces like The Silent Cartographer to the distinct and believable AI behaviors, that it was one of the few games at the time that compelled me to replay on its hardest difficulty. But perhaps what has made it so meaningful and memorable over the years--which is sadly absent from the recent Halo: Campaign Evolved--is its competitive multiplayer. And I can't really think about Halo multiplayer without thinking of Blood Gulch, especially when it was essentially the only map I would ever play with friends. Multiplayer sessions fixated on just one map was a bit of a common occurrence among my friends, given the countless sleepovers that involved GoldenEye on the N64 and doing nothing but having shootouts in the toilets of Facility. In comparison, Halo took a while to make an imprint for us, considering that I had to wait until the following Christmas after its launch before I could get an Xbox. I was also the only person among my friends to own Microsoft's console, but Halo's influence would spread like The Flood when I introduced it to my friend Josh. We had gone to different schools in sixth form but stayed in touch as I was going to church back then, so it became a ritual that after evening service, he would come around to my place to work through Halo's campaign in co-op. By the end of its explosive final mission, he was converted and got his own Xbox. Halo: Combat Evolved - Blood Gulch It was also at this point where we were able to meaningfully experience Halo's multiplayer. After all, shooting at each other in 1v1 matches was a bit one-note, but Josh also had younger brothers, so suddenly I was going over to his house with spare controllers so we could play four-player splits-screen just like the N64 days. This was when we got our introduction to Blood Gulch, a name I had only been able to read about for months, but would become permanently etched from thereon. On the face of it, Blood Gulch is the antithesis of a typical FPS map, its canyon design giving too much space in a genre that's often more fast-paced, with fewer tactical corners to poke around. However, the open field was also what made it viable for vehicular action, letting you rampage with your pal on a Warthog or run someone over with a Ghost. But when thinking of its semi-symmetrical layout with a base at each end, it also brings to mind a large football field, which also leads me to Capture the Flag. Halo may not have invented this mode, but as a console gamer first, it was my real introduction to it, and felt transformative. While in the days of GoldenEye we would quite happily run around shooting each other, now we actually had a concrete goal to work towardr as a team, which also didn't feel completely different from the attack and defense of five-a-side football. Halo: Combat Evolved - Blood Gulch Even so, there's only so much potential you can wring out of 2v2 CTF, with one side haplessly carrying the flag while the other tries to fight off the opposing Spartans, and we occasionally made things harder on ourselves by turning off vehicles.  The bigger game changer was discovering the System Link Cable that allowed us to connect multiple Xbox consoles together, which was how I got my first taste of a LAN party. I could also count on Josh to evangelize on Halo's behalf so that we ended up roping in enough folks from church to get together for 4v4 matches (this was still only half of its full potential, as it was actually possible to connect up to four Xbox systems for 16-player multiplayer). It was via this method that we really got to appreciate the balanced yet asymmetrical design of Blood Gulch, such as how the Red team has access to a ridge that's a perfect sniping spot, while the Blue team is closer to a tunnel system, though that doesn't prevent each team from taking advantage of either route. The base would be at one moment a quiet place to shoot banter with another defending teammate before exploding into tight shootouts when someone tried to make a scramble for the flag. I've played many more team-based FPS games over the years too, but few ever have as as memorable a moments ascatching the glint of a sniper hiding behind Sniper's Rock and then trying to sneak your way up to take them out first, the foolhardy derring-do of stealing a flag while praying your invisibility power-up lasts, or having everyone pile into a Scorpion tank and going in loud.  Halo: Combat Evolved - Blood Gulch Given the setup required, I would be lying if I said I got to experience Halo via System Link more than a handful of times. After all, the first time we managed to organize this event was the summer before I went away to university, where I was back to being the only person in my immediate circle who was gaming, let alone an Xbox owner. Of course, the year Halo 2 came out and I was back for the holidays, we naturally wrangled people together again to link up and load up its Blood Gulch remake, Coagulation, taking to the new additions like energy swords and Banshees even if they might have upset its predecessor's balance. It's the seemingly all-night-long sessions at Blood Gulch that are so burned into my memory. I don't think I'm alone in that, given the map's popularity over the years, having not only been remade by Bungie in subsequent entries, but even by the community the moment the Forge update was added to Halo Infinite. In later years, its beloved status has found it being remade in other shooters, including CS:GO and Fortnite. But beyond the genre, its cultdom was arguably cemented by the web series Red vs. Blue, its creator Burnie Burns perfectly tapping into its desolate desert as a space for contemplating existentialist themes on almost the same level as a Samuel Beckett play.  But for all the ways that Halo has evolved over the years, for me it was Halo: The Master Chief Collection getting a System Link update in 2018 that was the most meaningful. Over that time, I had stopped going to church and even spent many years a lapsed gamer, but once the habit had kicked back in, I had also gotten back in touch with Josh. While we had caught up with various online multiplayer games, such as Destiny and Overwatch, the opportunity to relive Halo LAN parties 15 years later was something we just had to seize. Halo: Combat Evolved - Blood Gulch Of course, we had all gotten older with more responsibilities and busy schedules, but there was something special about how despite the different paths we'd taken, Halo somehow brought us back together for one night, albeit with each team huddling around a larger and sharper monitor than the CRT TVs we made do with back in the day. And despite Halo MCC including the new Bloodlines map--a remake of Coagulation--when it came to setting up a Capture the Flag session, there was no question that Blood Gulch was our unanimous pick as a pure nostalgia hit. Halo may feel like a shadow of its former self these days, its recent campaign remake feeling more like a pristine eulogy than a path toward the future, which will now be under the direction of Activision. But no matter how bleak or uncertain that future looks, at least we'll always have Blood Gulch.

Xbox is celebrating its 25-year anniversary today, November 15, 2026. Below, we take a look back at Microsoft's great, and infamously buggy, second console. In the mid to late aughts, the Xbox 360 reigned supreme. It felt like the center of the gaming universe, as the best place to play multiplatform hits like Modern Warfare and Grand Theft Auto, and the only way to play exclusive blockbusters like Halo 3. All of my high school friends were on Xbox Live every night, and online gaming became a ritual to me. My clearest, purest memories of gaming come from this era, and the Xbox 360 remains one of my favorite consoles of all time. It’s too bad that I burned through so many of them. The Xbox 360 was as infamous for hardware failures as it was famous for its amazing software library. It’s nearly impossible to look back at the 360 without also recalling the Red Ring of Death, the ominous three red lights surrounding the power button that indicates a general hardware error. This red ring was a plague on the initial Xbox 360 model, and the shipping and repairs for affected consoles cost Microsoft a little over a billion dollars. The Xbox 360 platform as a whole had an amazing lifespan--the first Xbox 360 consoles themselves, though, did not. Halo 3 Somehow, I had completely avoided the dreaded red ring with my original 360 model, but it wasn’t spared of all technical issues. One night, I plopped my Halo 3 disc in the tray, only to be met with a suspicious clicking-like noise. Instead of the Halo 3 icon appearing on my dashboard, a generic “Open Tray” message appeared in its place. Troubleshooting was a maddening process--all of my discs were returning the same message, and Google searches were largely unhelpful. Despite the functioning software, my 360 refused to read any of my physical games. It quickly became clear that my 360 was a lemon. I removed the hard drive, enveloped my 360 in bubble wrap, stuffed it in a box, and left it with UPS. The ordeal cost me $90, as my Xbox was out of warranty. My precious Xbox 360 and the digital space that my friends occupied on a nightly basis became completely out of reach. About two weeks later, my replacement 360 came home. Being able to jump back into Xbox Live felt like rejoining society, and the countless hours of Halo, Call of Duty, and Left 4 Dead resumed.  Left 4 Dead Months later, the clicking noise returned, and I was back to square one. Back to UPS for me. It felt like cruel irony that my consoles never received the ominous red ring, yet fatal errors still caught up with them anyway. My third Xbox 360 fared well, I recall. Then, after leaks and rumors, Microsoft announced and shadow-dropped the slim Xbox 360. For me, and presumably many others who went through similar hardware struggles, this announcement was a godsend. The Xbox 360 was a fantastic platform with limitless entertainment, but it was unfortunately trapped in a sickened body. This new, sleeker model was a total overhaul of its internals and form factor, and it was incapable of red-ringing (mainly because it literally had no red LEDs). You can bet that I was quick to trade in my original model 360 for this newfangled, glossy Xbox. It only took nearly half a decade, but Microsoft finally fixed its biggest problem. The Xbox 360 platform overcame its flawed engineering, and the hardware itself was finally able to keep up with the amazing software support and bustling online community that had built up despite all of the technical issues. I don’t remember any issues with my slim Xbox 360 in the years that followed (except accidentally hitting that touch-sensitive power button every once in a while), and that version of the Xbox 360 is how I choose to remember it today. Gears of War Arguably, no platform since then has had the omnipresence of the Xbox 360, at least in the West. Despite the popularity and sales success of the PlayStation 4 and the Nintendo Switch, it felt as if the Xbox 360 dominated attention and broke the containment of gaming communities into the mainstream in ways other platforms couldn’t. It was a transformative platform, defining what an online service infrastructure should look like for gaming, and housing so many influential titles like Gears of War, Mass Effect, The Elder Scrolls 4, Call of Duty: Modern Warfare, BioShock, and Grand Theft Auto 4. For me, the Xbox 360 was a source of joy, and the time of the platform’s peak was when gaming became a central pillar of my life. But with all of its successes, the 360’s prolific technical fiascos are forever intertwined with the platform’s history. They say you have to break a few eggs to make an omelette--I apparently had to break at least two Xboxes before I could truly have fun.

After years of development, multiple delays, and what can only be assumed to be one of the biggest production budgets ever, Rockstar's Grand Theft Auto 6 launches on November 19. The game is poised to "blot out the sun," some experts believe, and its release looms so large that other gaming companies are shifting dates for their own games to stay out of the blast radius.  Francis Lawrence, the director of the new Hunger Games movie Sunrise on the Reaping, which comes out a day after GTA 6, has said he hopes people can find the time to play the game and watch his movie. In short, GTA 6 is seen by some as a black hole that will suck up time, attention, and money from the broader gaming economy--and even workplaces, as people dive into the 80-hour game they've been waiting a decade to play.  To help make sense of, and contextualize, the enormity of GTA 6's looming launch, GameSpot caught up with Bart Spiegel of the global professional services and research company PricewaterhouseCoopers, or PwC. He told GameSpot that GTA 6 is "something much bigger than a traditional entertainment launch," and is more akin to an event like the Olympics. He believes GTA 6 is a "rare cultural moment" that "commands extraordinary attention and creates economic activity well beyond the event itself." Jason pumping some iron in GTA 6. Spiegel said he foresees potential shifts beyond gaming, in places like TV viewership, music consumption, social media engagement, and even travel. "In an increasingly fragmented entertainment landscape, it's rare for a single release to command this much attention across so many industries simultaneously. That's what makes this moment so significant," he said. An economics professor recently claimed that about 1.5 million people plan to "call out sick" to play GTA 6, and this could come at a "cost" of $1 billion to the US economy. Spiegel said people should remember that GTA 6 isn't anything new or special as it relates to taking time off work. "People regularly plan time off around major cultural and entertainment events, and this is another example of consumers prioritizing an experience they're excited about," he said. That being said, Spiegel remarked that PwC has observed estimates suggesting time-off requests near GTA 6's release could be 9% to 17% higher than normal. "But what's more interesting to me is what happens outside the workplace. If millions of people are spending additional hours gaming, they're potentially spending less time watching television, going out to restaurants, or engaging in other activities," he said. "And unlike a sporting event that lasts a few hours or a few weeks, this experience could keep people engaged for months. So the broader economic implications may extend well beyond a single day of missed work," he added. "Huge net positive" GTA 6 is expected to be an enormous success commercially--it's reportedly already sold 11 million units through pre-purchases, and that could rise to 25 million at launch. That would work out to $2 billion in revenue, which would cover the game's entire development budget and most of its marketing spend, Sensor Tower said. But many are also saying that GTA 6 represents the last of a dying breed, and that other publishers might learn all the wrong lessons from its success. Spiegel said GTA 6 is going to be a "huge net positive for the gaming industry as a whole," in part because its success will reiterate to the broader entertainment and media landscape that games are culturally significant. At the same time, Spiegel said he doesn't foresee a world in which other publishers try to replicate a blockbuster of this magnitude.  "Very few organizations have the capital, expertise, and intellectual property required to support investments on this scale. These are extraordinary events, not necessarily repeatable business models," Spiegel said.  If GTA 6 is the huge success it's projected to be, it could positively uplift the broader gaming ecosystem in a lot of ways, Spiegel continued.  "It could attract new audiences, renew investor interest, and create opportunities across advertising, payments, technology, and content creation. The real lesson isn't that everyone needs to build the next billion-dollar game. It's that gaming is an increasingly powerful part of the entertainment economy, with opportunities that extend well beyond the games themselves," he said. Jason escaping the law in GTA 6. Some companies, like Ubisoft, see GTA 6 not as something to be strictly afraid of. CEO Yves Guillemot says, that's because the rising tide that is GTA 6 could help drive more people to gaming overall. In fact, Ubisoft is putting out a big new update for one of its biggest games, Rainbow Six Siege, right around GTA 6's launch. Mat Piscatella of Circana reminded people that not everyone wants to play GTA 6, and he pushed back against the theory that it's a bad business idea to launch a game near GTA 6. "The fear of many teams to put their game in the same window as GTA is completely overblown. GTA is going to be a massive game. Of course it is. It’s going to be a huge cultural event, but not every one of the 49 million people who own a PS5 or an Xbox Series in the US is going to be interested in that," he told GamesBeat. Could GTA 6 lead the push to more in-game ads? Spiegel went on to say that one additional follow-on effect from GTA 6 could be something that gamers might balk at but publishers and investors might salivate over. He said that if GTA 6 succeeds in the way people expect it to, and draw people into its world for tens--or even hundreds--of hours, that could demonstrate to the wider market that it's time for gaming, as an industry, to do more with advertising.  "When millions of people are spending substantial amounts of time in interactive entertainment environments, it's natural for advertisers and investors to consider how they might participate," he said.  Advertising for mobile and social games is already commonplace, but it's less present in the PC and console gaming market. There are examples, of course, as companies have already undertaken efforts to put ads into gaming with mixed results. Gamers quickly revolted when EA put ads in a UFC game, prompting EA to pull them quickly to help quell the backlash. Despite that, EA recently announced the formation of a new EA Advertising division that will seek to put ads "directly into gameplay." That announcement didn't go over well, either, as fans pushed back against the idea of having to see ads in yet another place in their life. Other examples have been received more positively, or at least ambivalently, with a trucking company buying ad space in the American Truck Simulator series to help recruit staff.  Lucia and Jason in GTA 6. As Spiegel sees it, though, if executed correctly, advertising could be a big boon for gaming. "What's particularly interesting is that the opportunity extends beyond placing advertisements inside games. There's an entire ecosystem of digital advertising infrastructure, technology, and services that could benefit as gaming continues to grow. More investment During the COVID years, the gaming industry saw massive revenue and player growth as people, forced to stay inside due to stay-at-home orders, turned to games to pass the time, have fun, and connect with friends. At the same time, interest rates were historically low. The cost to borrow money was so attractive that publishers ramped up significantly.  The COVID-era boost to gaming did not last, though, and the industry is now challenged, to say the least, with numerous developers cutting staff, cancelling projects, and scaling back in other ways. If GTA 6 is indeed a massive success, it could trigger renewed interest in gaming as an investment vehicle, Spiegel believes, but not necessarily exclusively for funding new games and studios. "I absolutely think a release of this magnitude will get the attention of investors, corporate executives, and private equity firms. "For business leaders, the question is whether they're competing with this cultural moment for consumer attention or finding ways to participate in it.
