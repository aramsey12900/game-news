The Metroidvania Is Decades Old, But One Year In Particular Is The Undisputed KingNEW
The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of th...
The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of the popular genre. This is Metroidvania Week. Metroidvanias have been around for decades, with numerous high points over those years. Super Metroid–which set the template for the beloved (and much-iterated-upon) formula–hit the SNES in 1994. Castlevania: Symphony of the Night–which established a bold, non-linear direction for the series and helped pioneer the genre–followed in 1997. Metroid Prime–which proved this kind of game could work in 3D–touched down on the GameCube in 2002. And Hollow Knight: Silksong–which put metroidvanias at the center of gaming culture for one of the only times in their history–hit in late 2025.  Though all of those titles have been and will be beacons for developers who came after, they were lonely lights. There were no other games released in 1994 that could join forces with Super Metroid; nothing to help Symphony of the Night make like its famous castle and turn the gaming world upside down. When metroidvanias had a moment in 2002, it was entirely driven by the Metroid series itself, as the GBA title Metroid Fusion launched the same day as Prime. Metroid Prime Remastered That wasn't the case in 2018, the best year in the genre's history. It was a year that stretched the boundaries of what a metroidvania could look like and play like, and how it could be built. There were original outings, new games in established series, and one very important port that brought a modern classic to a wider audience. The year's metroidvanias took inspiration from other genres (souls-likes, roguelikes, character action) and even mediums (pinball). Not all of these games worked, critically or commercially, but the overwhelming variety on display made it the best and easiest time ever for new players to try the genre out.  Just take a look at this release calendar: January 23, 2018 - Iconoclasts February 6, 2018 - Dandara April 10, 2018 - Super Daryl Deluxe May 29, 2018 - Yoku's Island Express June 12, 2018 - Hollow Knight (Switch) July 30 - La Mulana 2 July 31, 2018 - Chasm August 7, 2018 - Dead Cells August 14, 2018 - Death's Gambit August 21, 2018 - Guacamelee 2 August 30, 2018 - The Messenger September 25, 2018 - Timespinner December 4, 2018 - Monster Boy and the Cursed Kingdom From the first month of the year to the last, fans were regularly fed with new, exciting takes on the old formula. Players who turn to games for an engaging story were rewarded right off the bat with Iconoclasts. It was an unusually narrative-driven take on a genre that, by and large, had solely prioritized exploration up to that point, diving into themes like religion and authoritarianism. And, equally importantly, it let you hit stuff with a big wrench.  Dead Cells Dandara took inspiration from Dark Souls, with corpse runs and punishing difficulty. But it was most notable for its unusual control scheme. Instead of walking and running through the levels, you controlled the protagonist's movements with angled leaps. Gravity had no power here: Dandara could shoot from the ceiling, stand on the walls, heal up on the floor, and survive fights by leaping between all three.  Yoku's Island Express messed with metroidvania movement even further, dropping players into a map that was a sprawling collection of pinball tables. Yoku, a dung beetle tethered to a conveniently ball-shaped pebble, begins the game by taking over as the island of Mokumana's mailman (er, mailbug). To deliver Yoku's packages, the player needs to skillfully use the controller's right and left triggers, which correspond to color-coded flippers and paddles built into the map. It's a one-of-one game, with a fusion that feels intuitive but has never been repeated. Chasm and Dead Cells also experimented with mixing and matching, bringing roguelike randomness to the metroidvania in unique ways. Dead Cells implemented procedural generation in the more straightforward way, taking a page from Spelunky, and asking players to make their way through a series of biomes whose map layouts changed each time you started a new run, building unique and powerful builds as they went. Yoku's Island Express Chasm was different. Bit Kid, Inc.'s action-adventure game generated a map at the beginning of a playthrough, and that map remained set until you rolled credits. It was still using procedural map generation, but to witness the array of possible changes, you would need to play the game more than once. It was a roguelike on a grander scale, where a "run" didn't take 30 minutes; it took 30 hours. Dead Cells was more successful, spawning years of updates and expansions. But eight years later, Chasm remains a fascinating evolutionary dead-end.  The metroidvania genre having such a strong showing was possible, in part, thanks to 2018 being an unusually sparse year for big triple-A releases. Now, everyone remembers 2018 as the year that gave us Red Dead Redemption 2, God of War, and Spider-Man. And those games were a lot of fun. But GoW hit in April, Spidey swung in to save the day in September, and Arthur Morgan hitched his horse in October. The rest of the year was pretty quiet, and summer was especially barren. That dearth of big releases made space for indie metroidvanias to break out and, thanks to the Switch launching the previous year, we suddenly had the perfect console to play them on. Hollow Knight's port to Switch on June 12 was an important step on its journey from popular, but niche, title to a franchise that could push indie competitors around the calendar like GTA 6. And August was absolutely packed. Dead Cells exited early access to widespread acclaim on August 7, Death's Gambit attempted to replicate Dark Souls in 2D on August 14, Guacamelee! 2 punched out with outstanding kinetic fights on August 2, and, on August 30, The Messenger pulled off an incredibly cool genre twist, as linear Ninja Gaiden-inspired platforming gave way to non-linear metroidvania exploration. (And that's not counting La Mulana 2 and Chasm, which barely missed the window with July 30 and 31 release dates).  Hollow Knight Your favorite metroidvania might not have been released in 2018. It probably wasn’t. But one standout game isn't what makes a moment. For a genre (or a scene or a medium), a moment happens when many creators are working, together and separately, to make new, exciting, boundary-pushing work. It's what made 2018 a great time to be a metroidvania fan, and it's what makes 1986's release of the original Metroid so exciting in retrospect. Nintendo laid the building blocks for a genre, and Konami built upon it to create a truly special formula that is still popular decades later.

Clever management of the stagger bar against tough enemies, especially the wind-wielding boss of the dungeon I faced, was the difference between a victory fanfare and a full party wipeout. Chipping away at the bar meant constantly balancing who was attacking, defending, healing, and using items to keep the magic flowing.Final Fantasy Resonance falls into an interesting spot, as Square Enix's trend of revitalizing former mobile games into standalone console releases continues. There are some upgrades and quality-of-life changes built up over the narrative framework and building blocks of Brave Exvius, with plenty of series iconography draped over the top; a towering sundae of RPG. The cherry on top, though, is a solid new RPG built on both new and old. Dungeons & Dragons: Long Rest Tavern Hands-On Preview – A Cozier InitiativeNEW
...
Platform: PC Publisher: Dotemu Developer: Homo Ludens Dungeons & Dragons is often played from the perspective of the daring adventurer, rolling dice to slay monsters, topple tyrannical rulers, and loot perilous areas. Small roles like the shop or innkeeper are typically left to the Dungeon Master to occupy, but a new video game flips the script and lets the players experience the fantasy world from behind the counter for a change. It’s a role reversal that has a ton of potential.In the aptly named Dungeons & Dragons: The Long Rest Tavern, players assume the role of a customizable innkeeper running a burgeoning business in Faerûn. Despite this angle, your character still takes on one of seven classes: Bard, Wizard, Fighter, Druid, Barbarian, Rogue, or Cleric. How the unique traits of these classes come into play wasn’t immediately evident during my 45-minute hands-on demo, but I imagine it's more than a mere label. I begin my business venture by building my tavern. Intuitive tools make laying out my floor plan a breeze; by simply clicking and dragging on a grid, I quickly lay out and construct a large dining room, a closet for storing inventory, and a couple of bedrooms. Decorations include various floor and wall patterns and textures, different doors and windows, and furniture like tables. To add an extra comforting touch, I add a blazing fireplace for good measure. The first and most important element of any tavern is a bar, which serves as the centerpiece of the business.Once I build and place the bar, I must then hire my first employees: a bartender and a server. Hiring someone involves weighing stats such as pouring speed for bartenders and walk speed for servers. The faster these tasks are performed, the quicker you can flip tables, meaning the more customers you’ll serve during a shift, which always begins at night. During the day, you’ll explore outside to expand or improve the tavern or place orders to ship new inventory to keep the business well-stocked, among other tasks. You’ll even be able to send adventurers on quests to retrieve resources for you. Each shift sees parties of adventurers and other clientele arrive to drink the night away. As the manager, you need to ensure there are open, clean tables for patrons to occupy, and that there’s enough booze on tap to keep mugs full. I began with a basic ale, but later unlocked a recipe for higher-quality beer brewed using gathered ingredients. Running out of beer mid-shift means turning away disappointed customers and losing money. Once a party leaves, I quickly clean the tables to make them ready for the next group.  If your tavern has rooms with beds, some adventurers will stay the night, letting you earn more money from them. At the end of each shift, you earn Renown based on how much money you earned, overall customer satisfaction, and the total number of visitors that night. As your Renown level rises, you’ll attract higher-level customers with deeper pockets.I had a good, relaxing time playing through a few early shifts and improving my tavern during my demo. The Long Rest Tavern checks the basic boxes for a good shop management sim, but it will take a longer play session for its deeper, more D&D-centric systems to reveal themselves. Once they do, I hope they further separate the game from the myriad of similar titles out there. But even if they don’t, I suspect even a by-the-numbers cozy management sim with D&D trappings will more than satisfy avid tabletop fans. This is a killer concept, though, so here’s hoping it leverages the license to the absolute fullest.
