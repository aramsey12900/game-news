Star Wars Zero Company Is XCOM Meets Star Wars In The Best Way PossibleNEW

Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Electronic Arts, Lucasfilm Games Developer: Bit Reactor, Respawn Entertainment Release: August 27, 2026 Rating: Teen I still remember where I was when I heard that Firaxis was making a Marvel game. While the game I dreamt of – XCOM with the Marvel license – ended up differing pretty substantially from what we ultimately got with Marvel's Midnight Suns, I still thoroughly enjoyed the unique deckbuilding and social mechanics layered on top of the turn-based tactics I had come to love about Firaxis. However, with many of the developers who created XCOM: Enemy Unknown, XCOM 2, and Marvel's Midnight Suns departing Firaxis to form Bit Reactor, and that studio announcing its debut game to be a Star Wars title, it was safe to say I was excited at the prospects of the end result. And now that I've spent upwards of 10 hours exploring the Galaxy Far, Far Away in this turn-based tactics experience, I'm finally getting the licensed tactics game that hits many of the same notes that made me fall in love with XCOM back in 2012.Back to Square ZeroThe Maryland-based Bit Reactor was founded in 2021 by several former members of Civilization and XCOM developer Firaxis Games. Co-founder and director Greg Foertsch, who spent 22 years at Firaxis, was joined by several other former Firaxis developers, as well as team members from other studios with diverse backgrounds, which Foertsch says brings new spins and perspectives to that core group's way of thinking. However, having that foundational group of Firaxis employees helped Bit Reactor hit the ground running, even though the studio was started during the COVID-19 remote work period."We were starting this up while people were remote, but we haven't just shipped one game together... a lot of us have shipped four or five games together, and have been together for decades, not just a year or two," Foertsch says. "We're a weird company in that we're a completely independent startup, and we have days where, from a business side, we're a startup, but we've got the soul of a much older team, and we've got a shorthand with each other. We have trust. All those things are so important to building a game that if you don't have them, they create hurdles. We were, out of the gate, able to sidestep some of the early startup stuff because of our experience together and, most importantly, our trust in each other." Bit Reactor was always going to make a turn-based tactics game for its debut title. However, it was the late Vince Zampella, former head of Respawn Entertainment, who pushed Bit Reactor to create a Star Wars game. "I wanted to work with existing IP to begin with; it's a hurdle that's taken care of for you, and you can focus on the game part of it and the studio part of it," Foertsch says. "Vince ended up calling me and said, 'Hey, can you talk to me about what you want to make?' And I did, and I can't say enough about Vince having the vision and conviction to let us pursue this. [...] I was pitching a few other places before I talked to Vince, and then once I talked to Vince, I steered all of my attention toward Vince and Respawn because I felt that ideologically, we aligned. Vince believed in developers, and it's not just about where your funding comes from, it's about making sure that the people you're working with understand game and understand that there's a point where they don't look good, and understand that there's a point where, 'Wow, why did I ever go on a journey to make this thing?' But that if you have faith in the process, eventually you come out with something great, and you just have to stick with it. So that became pretty clear to me that we matched up well."Once that match was made, Electronic Arts signed on to publish the game, with Respawn helping Bit Reactor with development. Respawn's Star Wars Jedi franchise takes place between the Prequel Trilogy and the Original Trilogy, telling the story of a Jedi taking on the Empire following the execution of Order 66. Bit Reactor decided to go a different route, telling a story during a well-trodden, but ripe part of Star Wars history: the Clone Wars. Despite having a long-running animated series devoted to explaining the events of the Clone Wars, Foertsch knew it was the right time period to tell the story he wants to tell. It turned out Lucasfilm agreed. "The fact that there is so much stuff surrounding the Clone Wars allows us to put our story in that Clone Wars bubble, and it can kind of exist inside of this thing," Foertsch says. "And so the edges of the Clone Wars kind of reach in and touch it a little bit, but you've got this little bubble that the player is in, and they're playing a story that's new and fresh and different, yet it's got all the familiar stuff that we can pull in. So, if you're a big Star Wars fan, you're going to have lots of little things where you're like, 'Wow, that's a deep cut! That's really cool!' If you're not, you'll learn about some of those things. It's a way to position us to tell a really good story with enough familiar elements available to bind it."As someone who loves both XCOM and Star Wars, I couldn't wait to dive into the pre-release preview build, which gave me unfettered access to the first several missions of the game, leading up to a climactic battle that gives a big nod to that broader Clone Wars era. That conclusion served as the exclamation point for my time with Star Wars Zero Company, but it was far from the only highlight.Telling You The OddsStar Wars Zero Company casts you as Hawks, the fully customizable leader of Zero Company, a ragtag group of mercenaries who start off working jobs for the Republic before slowly unraveling a mystery surrounding a fanatical cult. This cult, The Infinite Coil, is led by Kundri Fathom, who infects all members and initiates with a plague that improves their physical strength seemingly at the cost of their mind and free will. After the first couple of hours, The Infinite Coil establishes itself as insidious, capable, and relentless. Not only that, but it aligns itself with the Separatists and its army of battle droids.At the start of the story, Hawks is on a mission with Zero Company, which consists of a Rodian named Aurelio, a Clone Trooper named Trick, and a retired Felshi boxer called Kabb Uppercut.  By the end of my time playing, I also added a Jedi and a Mandalorian bounty hunter. Each of these characters has an authored personality and signature look, which might lead you to think they're irreplaceable. That would be a mistake. As you navigate the turn-based tactical combat, which makes up the bulk of the Star Wars Zero Company experience, there are various ways your play impacts the story and future battles. If you do everything great, leaning on your teammates and relying on assists, your characters will level up, earn focus points to upgrade their abilities, and even develop stat-boosting bonds that make your squad even more formidable. That happened the vast majority of the time. However, it wasn't always cause for celebration. Star Wars Zero Company carries forward the common convention of permadeath, so while I was consistently recruiting new members of Zero Company, it wasn't always due to expansion. In fact, this may be telling on myself for some of the boneheaded moves I made, but by the time I reached the end of my demo, only Hawks and one other Zero Company member from the start of the story remained alive. Sure, I could have reset the mission and tried again, but that feels disingenuous to the way it's intended to be played. Not only that, but I appreciate the emergent stories that come out of just pushing forward in the game no matter how things go."The narrative team did an awesome job on that," Foertsch says. "We wanted to make sure that the characters provided some archetypes for people to latch to and are familiar with, but weren't so heavy that you couldn't attach – like, they didn't bring too much backstory. Trying to balance that amount of archetype and character and backstory that you bring with your ability to still be able to latch on to these authored heroes as much as the ones that you custom-make was a real big goal for us. I think we've threaded it pretty well for people."Outside of combat, you can use your intel points to undergo menu-based operations that earn you credits, regional reputation, new tactical missions, and other rewards. You can use your credits to upgrade your base with new modules, upgrade your squadmates with new skills and equipment, send teammates to the medbay for healing, and even purchase new weapons on the Black Market.There are also gameplay areas where the camera abandons its typical isometric perspective in favor of swinging behind Hawks as he navigates various Star Wars environments. Often, these sequences are quick or inconsequential, but they do add a small touch of gameplay diversity and a heap of immersion you can't get from a bird's-eye view. This is especially true in your base, which you explore in the third-person perspective. Walking around the base, talking with teammates, and interacting with different base modules directly gave me Mass Effect vibes, which perfectly complements the kind of story Bit Reactor hopes to tell. My only major concern from my time with Star Wars Zero Company is the seeming lack of mission variety. While the maps are all thoughtfully crafted, the missions often boil down to eliminating all enemies or reaching a point of interest before evacuating your squad while more enemies spawn in. While those are tried-and-true tactics classics, I was more excited when missions with more variety popped up. One of those had me rescue a downed ally, then blast our way out of the sticky situation alongside the rescued operative. Another broke up the tactical gameplay with a brief espionage sequence in which Hawks donned an officer's outfit and infiltrated a restricted area to gather intelligence.Fascinatingly, despite playing on my desktop PC, I only used keyboard and mouse for half of my time playing. I picked up my Xbox controller just to quickly see how it feels and never went back. That may seem sacrilegious for longtime tactics players, but for Bit Reactor, it was by design. "We've considered controller just as much as we've considered keyboard and mouse," Foertsch says.Star Wars Zero Company thoroughly impressed me from the time I started. I cherished each well-executed plan. I cursed my squadmates' missed shots despite the 90-percent chance of hitting. I gripped my controller tight as the Coil and battle droids descended upon my injured team from multiple angles. I mourned those I lost along the way. I celebrated those who survived and leveled up. But the most telling aspect of my time with Bit Reactor's debut game is that even after spending more than 10 hours with these characters, I can't wait to reassume the role of Hawks and lead Zero Company to another hard-fought victory.A Familiar HopeI went into my demo expecting an experience that might lightly scratch the XCOM itch I perpetually have. I didn't expect Star Wars Zero Company to scratch it with the intensity of a lightsaber. I came away from my time with Bit Reactor's leap into the Star Wars galaxy not only excited to see where the story goes and who might show up, but also feeling incredibly compelled to jump back in for more tactical missions. The gameplay loop, the tactical combat, and the impactful choice and risk/reward mechanics all amount to a formula for something potentially special. Add in the Star Wars dressing, and the nostalgia factor amps the anticipation up even more. As Foertsch looks ahead to the launch of Star Wars Zero Company in just two weeks, he understands that the gaming world is perhaps more ready to embrace the tactics genre than it was in 2012 when Firaxis retooled the XCOM series. "Our mission is to challenge that space, the thought that these games are niche; they're not, and we're about to bring the biggest franchise in the world to it, and hope that people come along for the ride on it and can understand and accept it," Foertsch says. "A lot of the things that interfered with the genre coming over in the early 2000s are gone, and I think people are more open to, 'It's just a great game.' Final Fantasy Tactics is a great game. I don't think of it as a tactics game; it's just a great game. I think we're starting to see a gamer population that's more open to, 'It's a good game, cool story, cool mechanics, it's something different.'"And with an IP like Star Wars, Zero Company may just have a noteworthy leg up over other titles in the tactics space, a feeling further amplified by the fact that it's crafted by several masters of the genre. However, we won't know for sure how far-reaching it is until Star Wars: Zero Company arrives on PS5, Xbox Series X/S, and PC on August 27.

1666: Amsterdam – Hands-On With The Assassin's Creed Creator's Long-Awaited Passion ProjectNEW

Platform: PC Publisher: Panache Digital Games Developer: Panache Digital Games Release: August 25, 2026 Assassin’s Creed creator Patrice Désilets has moved mountains to bring his passion project 1666: Amsterdam to fruition. The witchcraft-focused action-adventure game was originally conceived over 15 years ago while Désilets was still at Ubisoft. Through a series of tumultuous events, the project has changed studios and been at the center of a years-long lawsuit over rights ownership between Ubisoft and Désilets. Now that 1666: Amsterdam will finally see the light of day, I traveled overseas to its namesake city to play an hour-long build Panache Digital Games prepared for media, which I do at the Amsterdam Dungeon, a local horror attraction that’s part Universal Halloween Horror Nights, part history lesson. Amid the muffled screams of guests enduring the dungeon’s creepy rooms and spirited live performers, it creates the perfect atmosphere to dive into Panache’s occult adventure.  The demo begins immediately after the free prologue chapter that launched alongside the game’s re-reveal at Summer Game Fest. I control Noa Brooklyn, a young crimson-clad witch who has just completed her initiation into the Zaindaris, a magical faction that raised her to become a Collector. Noa’s primary purpose is to identify and eliminate entities called Originals, powerful beings who can assume human form to live amongst us, sometimes for centuries. The Zaindaris granted the Originals their power long ago by bestowing Nux, an ancient magic, upon chosen individuals in an attempt to elevate humanity; instead, the Nux corrupted many of them over time, and they now pose a danger to the world. The Zaindaris have since been engaged in a generations-long cycle of training a Collector to reclaim the Originals’ power to restore balance. Now, it’s Noa’s turn. Noa’s journey won’t be the only time period players will experience. In a fitting twist from the mind behind Assassin’s Creed, the game prominently features a more contemporary setting. It’s New Year's Eve in 1999, and a young man named Aaron visits Amsterdam with his lover. Turns out his partner is secretly a Zaindaris. They engage in a sex ritual that somehow transports Aaron’s consciousness to 1666, where he awakens in the body of a cat during the night of Noa’s ritual; Noa chooses him as her familiar, or Union Cat. This begins a fascinating partnership between the steadfast Noa and reluctant Aaron as they must work to help one another. But there’s one more era at play. If you played the prologue, you met a woman named Clio, seemingly in the 2020s, investigating a letter from her father. Clio’s dad, it turns out, is Aaron, and his message recounting his and Noa’s adventure serves as the narration. Clio’s full role in 1666: Amsterdam is unclear, but given she was evidently conceived during the ritual that kicks off the game’s events, it’s clearly important.  After completing the ritual, the gathering is interrupted by witch-hunting militia. Chaos ensues, giving me the first sample of combat. The right shoulder trigger unleashes a basic combo attack, with Noa pummeling foes using her staff. Stunning them at the brink of death triggers a flashy final blow. A dodge allows Noa to evade attacks from the often four or five enemies surrounding her, and she consumes potions to heal. The melee combat doesn’t pack much satisfying impact in this in-development build, but it gets the point across. The enemies also tend to take turns attacking, which feels disappointingly dated and reminds me of dealing with mobs in the early Assassin’s Creed games. Defeating a group paves the way for another mob to take their place, with dramatic camera zooms displaying their shouts such as “Don’t move” and “Blaggard.” It’s a stylish touch, but one I grew tired of after consecutive encounters. I’d rather they just take their lickings without the prerequisite introduction.The only magic attack at my disposal lets me blow up objects such as barrels and crates. This proves much more satisfying, and can instantly kill or heavily damage nearby targets. However, Désilets later reveals in a separate hands-off demo other abilities Noa will gain. For example, she can unleash tendrils that ensnare and suspend foes, and temporarily petrify enemies, among other powers. Gathering Nux energy allows Noa to perform these feats, and they look fun to pull off. Noa also commands traversal abilities, such as the power to glide over water and warp across platforms to quickly get around a freely explorable Amsterdam that, while large, is denser than wide. This scale is partly due to Panache being a smaller team – 70 employees strong – and its goal of ensuring that every building has explorable interiors. This design choice plays into another key part of 1666: Amsterdam: investigating Originals. Finding them involves unearthing clues such as notes (often by scavenging homes and businesses), analyzing your surroundings for helpful details, and speaking with citizens to glean the required information: their appearance, seal, and original name. Investigations occur during the day. Once night falls, it’s time to confront the Original. Another surprise is that missions have a roguelike element. After accepting a contract to hunt an Original, players choose one of three powers from a randomized pool. These can be stat buffs such as increased health or offensive powers like the aforementioned Petrify. Panache gives players the freedom to investigate Originals as they see fit, with many avenues for gathering information discreetly or more chaotically. Sadly, I only get to watch Désilets tackle a full investigation, but finding clever ways to track down Originals looks to be the bulk of 1666: Amsterdam’s gameplay. I hope it’s fleshed out well enough to remain consistently engaging. To explore more safely, Noa can freely shift between her witch and civilian appearances. The latter draws less attention and makes citizens more amenable towards you. This matters, as an attention meter builds whenever you do questionable things, such as peeking through windows in front of others or openly performing magical acts. A filled meter invites the full wrath of the militia, forcing you to either fight your way to safety or flee to one of many safehouses Noa can retreat to in order to extinguish the heat. These safehouses also allow her to craft potions using recipes and ingredients found by searching through spaces. If the militia defeats Noa, she’ll be sent to prison, where you’ll have to escape. However, Panache is still figuring out this particular element and how severely it will punish multiple incarcerations. As for Noa’s partner Aaron, you can switch control to him at any time. As a cat, he can access places Noa can’t. Panache has created buildings that can be accessed in multiple ways. Aaron can leap up and around buildings to enter them, bypassing locked doors and opening them for Noa. Like everything else in this shaky development build, platforming was rough and occasionally unresponsive, but it reminded me of Stray’s deliberate yet graceful traversal. Aaron can also distract certain guards eager to pet him. On the flip side, those fearful of black cats are vulnerable to possession. Approaching a frightened person and hitting R2 unleashes a hiss, followed by a timed minigame where Aaron pounces upon them and scratches their face. This act allows Aaron to hijack their bodies, useful for exploring and letting Noa into restricted spaces. Aaron can also transmute Lux into Nux. I enjoyed the dynamic of switching between Noa and Aaron to figure out how to combine their unique talents to access tricky locations.  The game’s digital Amsterdam is impressively realized. Panache’s environmental level and art direction draw inspiration from 17th-century Dutch painter Johannes Vermeer (historically living during the game’s timeframe), famed for specializing in domestic interior scenes capturing everyday life of the period. 1666: Amsterdam's visual presentation has a painterly flourish that adds flair to the 3D models. When 1666: Amsterdam becomes available in Early Access on August 25, players can only explore the game’s early chapters, but Désilets calls it a “pretty big chunk” of content. He confirms that the game’s story outline, lore, and rules are complete, with Panache polishing and expanding dialogue and gameplay mechanics. Désilets emphasizes Panache’s eagerness to build the game with the community, and says to expect it to remain in Early Access for at least one year. As a self-professed “old timer,” Désilets recalls the days when a game printed to disc was complete and unchanging, flaws and all. He’s excited for players to witness 1666: Amsterdam’s development and, hopefully, provide valuable feedback. Désilets also confirms the game’s reported AI placeholder assets have been removed and no such content is present in the Early Access launch.My demo of 1666: Amsterdam was, as expected, rough around the edges, as an in-development build of a game launching in Early Access. The experience didn’t wow me from a polish standpoint, and I worry if the game will at least feel more stable and fun to play than the build I played when it arrives in a couple of weeks. But there are exciting gameplay and narrative ideas at play with Désilets leaning on some concepts from Assassin’s Creed but reworking them under a cool witchcraft premise. After over a decade of waiting and uncertainty, it’s tough not to feel happy for Désilets and even inspired by his persistence to will 1666: Amsterdam into existence. The project has a long road ahead, and although it could be off to a rocky start, here’s hoping it can eventually realize its enchanting potential.
