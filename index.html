Well, guess what: it’s back. Onimusha: Way of the Sword is the first new Onimusha game from Capcom in over two decades, and after covering it all month long for Game Informer’s cover story hub, I’m happy to report that the final game is absolutely worth your time and money. The combat is incredible – challenging, intricate, complex, and constantly building on itself. The characters and setting are good fun, too, and though it has more repetition in its combat arena design than I’d prefer, you still get to kill stuff with protagonist Musashi’s sword… and that’s always a great time. It’s out today, and you can read my Onimusha: Way of the Sword review while it’s downloading. Oh, and if you’re worried about difficulty, don’t be: Onimusha: Way of the Sword has an incredible easy mode. The Blood Of Dawnwalker Charles Harte I was optimistic about The Blood of Dawnwalker after reading my coworker Brian Shea's articles from his cover story trip, but I was still very surprised by how much I like the game. You play as Coen, a Dawnwalker (which means you're a vampire by night and a human by day) who has 30 days to save his family from an evil vampire lord. Each quest you complete advances the clock by a little, and you only have a limited amount of time before the game concludes, which means you have to pick and choose your missions carefully. What I didn't realize until playing, however, is that there's no main quest – you choose what feels important to you in your journey to save your loved ones. It's an incredibly empowering combination, and I really feel like my decisions matter when I focus on a quest to reforge an ancient sword instead of buddying up with some revolutionaries. I also enjoy the directional combat, the different mechanics in the night and daytimes, and the general atmosphere. Our reviewer Diego was a little less enthusiastic than I, so your mileage may vary, but if you're a fan of games with choice-based stories, I highly recommend The Blood of Dawnwalker.Game Informer's The Blood of Dawnwalker review Orbitals Charles Harte I like Orbitals, but I don't love it. It's an absolutely gorgeous game with visuals inspired by 80s anime and a wicked soundtrack to match. The characters are fun and charming, and even the menus are expressive and unique. The vibes are immaculate, but the actual gameplay is just okay. It's co-op only, and you'll spend its 6-hour runtime collaborating with a partner to solve puzzles and save your home space station. I just wasn't impressed with the game itself – it has some good ideas, but fails to meaningfully stand out the way similar co-op-only games do. I also don't love the story, but it's admittedly not much of a hurdle until the final moments. Despite those gripes, it's still such a joyous experience that I recommend it nonetheless. If the art style draws you in, you're in for a pretty good weekend with a friend. For my full thoughts, you can read my review here. So, would I recommend picking this game up for just its campaign? No, absolutely not. But if you’re a fan of asymmetrical horror games, or if you’ve never tried an IllFonic joint (they’re good fun, with or without friends to join you), Halloween: The Game is shaping up to be great. Crimson Moon Marcus Stewart Crimson Moon has Souls-inspired combat, but it isn’t a true Soulslike. It’s actually more of a roguelike thanks to its run-based blueprint, where you embark on missions as a nephlim (a human-angel hybrid) to slay a bevy of grotesque and dangerous demons. I’m not sure entirely why – I’m sure it benefits whatever version of Earth this is – but you’ll likely not care too much because of the solid combat, a more forgiving run-based format thanks to having multiple lives, and an impressive presentation for a $20 game. This is a charming and approachable platform adventure that can be played either solo or with a partner, in which you happily bounce and swing through jungle (and other) environments, collecting pick-ups, uncovering secrets, and mastering a surprisingly nuanced control scheme. The game is an outgrowth of a long-running Belgian comic book character, but rest assured, the taut and enjoyable gameplay of this particular project requires no special French connection to enjoy. Find the game on PS5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, or PC. Onimusha: Way of the Sword is finally out, and if you were considering picking it up – you should! It’s great, and you can read why in my review for Game Informer here. Now, if you’ve read any reviews for the game, or even previews leading up to its launch, you’ve likely heard about its difficulty, particularly its boss fights. While standard mob enemies have you feeling powerful, bosses absolutely put you in your place. There’s one boss I died more than a dozen times trying to defeat. But, if that scares you, don’t let it. Onimusha: Way of the Sword features the perfect easy mode for a game like this.  Capcom calls it Story (Storyline Focus) mode, a difficulty that lets you focus on the story and is recommended for action game beginners. That’s true in that, yes, it is a much easier difficulty mode compared to the Action (Combat Focus) mode that aims to test you at all times. Enemies do less damage, you do more damage to enemies, parry windows are wider, and other similar features are what tune the Story mode difficulty down, but the most interesting part of it is the Action Prompts feature. This is on by default in Story mode, and I am blown away by how transformative it is. Onimusha: Way of the Sword’s combat is intricate and complex. You must parry at the right time, complete Issen Breaks within even tighter timing windows, dodge at the right moment to avoid damage, deflect incoming attacks, and more. Capcom has created one of my favorite sword-based melee action games ever, and it’s all these moves that protagonist Miyamoto Musashi has in his kit that make it so fun.  However, I know what all these terms mean and how these timings work. If you don’t, well, there’s basically no hope of getting through Onimusha: Way of the Sword. Its later bosses demand near-perfection to win, and that means nailing every parry, deflection, and dodge. The game features tutorials but ultimately, it’s still on you to learn these timings and that can be difficult when you’re getting your face kicked in every time you miss one. The Story mode’s action prompts fix this entirely by showing you exactly the button to press and when to press it on-screen – it’s incredible! It’s such a simple feature, and though I’m blanking on it right now, surely other games have done this, right? Regardless, it’s exactly the kind of feature newcomers to this type of game need. It goes above and beyond the beginning tutorials and shows you, in real time, exactly what to press and when. This gives you all the satisfaction players who intimately know and love these types of games feel when they play Onimusha: Way of the Sword in its Action mode, for example. You can turn off the prompts too within the Story mode once you have a grasp on the timing, but you can also keep the prompts on from beginning to credits and beyond. And have no shame doing so – Onimusha: Way of the Sword is great, and you should do what you have to in order to reach the end (because its final hours rule). And hey, maybe after enough time learning the dance of swords that is Onimusha: Way of the Sword’s combat, you might try the Action mode and find success!  If you’re a newcomer, try Story mode because it will teach you everything you need to know about timing, parries, deflects, dodges, and more without feeling like you’re stuck in an extra-long tutorial.
