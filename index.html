In an official video, developer Bulkhead revealed September 10 as Wardogs' Steam early access release date, but also laid the rest of its cards on the table. Point by point, it outlined all the main reasons why potential players might not want to take a chance on the shooter, ranging from its lack of content, high early-access price, visual fidelity, and even the failure of Bulkhead's previous shooter, Battalion: 1944.  "Before the hype train gets out of control, we want to do something most AAA marketing teams would tell their dev teams to never do--give you 10 legitimate reasons why you should not buy Wardogs," Bulkhead said in the video. Those reasons are as follows: Lack of early-access content Early-access pricing Player count will decline Monetization Performance and visuals Shifting early-access priorities Battalion: 1944 VOIP Controller support Decisions and consequences https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ugkuP4a3xk4 Some of those reasons are fairly self explanatory, like lack of content during the early-access period and its price. Wardogs will initially cost $40 in early access, a steep price for some. The price will go up over the course of development and will eventually sell for $60 at the end of early access. "If the price sounds too high for an early-access game, please don't buy it yet," Bulkhead said. Other reasons require a bit more explanation. Decisions and consequences refers to Wardog's more milsim-style gameplay, where death requires a long trek back to the frontlines and rebuying lost equipment.  "If you're running in like you're playing Call of Duty or Battlefield, you will likely die to someone playing more cautiously," Bulkhead said. "If you're not willing to learn from those deaths, you're going to have a bad time."   While Wardogs doesn't yet have any monetization like a battle pass or paid skins, there will eventually be some way for Bulkhead to make more money and continue funding the game's development. However, that will likely be years from now, as Bulkhead said it won't monetize the game during early access. Additionally, Bulkhead won't sell weapon camos or in-game currency, with players needing to earn them through play. "It might seem strange to be talking about monetization years down the line, but we want you to be able to buy in knowing what you're buying into long term," Bulkhead said. One of the most hard-hitting reasons is Bulkhead's track record. The team previously released Battalion: 1944, a 5v5 Call of Duty-inspired shooter that died almost immediately on release, having only ever reached between 50-200 concurrent players, Bulkhead said. The studio eventually saved enough funds from work-for-hire projects to stay afloat, offered refunds to players, and refunded Battalion: 1944's Kickstarter backers, but it understands why some players may be hesitant. "It's these lessons and scars from Battalion that Wardogs has been built upon," Bulkhead said. "It's why we've been so cautious with our playtesting and why we've been so open with our marketing. To be really clear, we wouldn't have Wardogs without Battalion's failure, but if you still don't trust our studio because of Battalion: 1944, don't rush to buy Wardogs." So far, Bulkhead's list of reasons don't seem to be slowing down Wardogs' sales. It's currently climbing up Steam top-seller charts.

Onimusha: Way of the Sword is the latest game to grace the cover of Game Informer, and alongside my cover story, which subscribers can read right here, we’re highlighting many of the things we learned over the next couple of weeks. Already, our hub for the game is chock-full of exclusive features, interviews, and more, with videos on the way. One of my interviews while visiting Capcom’s HQ in Osaka, Japan, was with Way of the Sword animation team lead Hiroshi Yugen. We spoke about how the team crafted its own unique version of the historical Japanese samurai Miyamoto Musashi, among other topics. We also discussed Capcom’s RE Engine and how it held up for developing Way of the Sword, and learned in the process that the team had to consult engineers within the company to create a brand-new system for the game.  The RE Engine is not the Resident Evil Engine, despite RE typically being shorthand for the series and Resident Evil being Capcom’s most iconic series. The RE Engine, also known as the Reach for the Moon Engine, has been around for more than a decade. Resident Evil 7: Biohazard, which started a renaissance for Resident Evil games and arguably Capcom in general, was the first game to use it; since then, every Resident Evil game has been developed in it, as have other titles: 2023’s Street Fighter 6 and Exoprimal, 2024’s Dragon’s Dogma 2 and Kunitsu-Gami: Path of the Goddess, and last year’s Monster Hunter Wilds. Even this year’s Resident Evil Requiem, Monster Hunter Stories 3: Twisted Reflection, and Pragmata were developed in the RE Engine. Way of the Sword is the next game Capcom will release, developed using this engine, and I spoke to Yugen about how the engine held up, given that this Onimusha sequel features action much faster than the aforementioned games.“We didn’t actually have any limitations posed upon us when developing Onimusha using the RE Engine,” Yugen tells me. “On the complete opposite end of the scale, there was actually a new feature that hadn’t been really done in any other game.” That feature he’s referring to is the Dynamic Dismemberment System. It’s how Musashi can slice Genma enemies in two, slash through flesh that tears apart, and cut through arms, heads, and more. Way of the Sword reacts to that under the hood with visceral animations that create satisfying deaths for every single Genma in the game, assuming you deplete their stamina like a proper samurai and use Break Issen or one of Musashi’s other finishing moves.  “[This dismemberment] is not something that’s existed in any other title before, and it’s something that, on a technical level, needed to be added to the RE Engine [by Capcom’s engineers],” Yugen says. “Adding this feature was quite difficult because we wanted to combine it with the enemy deaths. In a lot of samurai dramas, they have a very exaggerated classic death where they are on screen for an extra five or six seconds and then slowly fall to the ground. To have that together with this dynamic dismemberment system on a technical level is quite tricky, so it’s something we really had to ask the programmers to introduce.” He adds that in the early stages of the project, the team attempted dismemberment, but this early iteration caused the enemy to freeze. “It wasn’t animated enough,” Yugen recalls saying. “But I was very adamant. I want them to be moving at the same time as having that effect.” If you haven’t seen the dynamic dismemberments in action yet, I highly recommend checking out the Way of the Sword demo that’s available right now. Complete a special move, like a Break Issen, and pay close attention to how the Genma’s body and flesh react to Musashi’s attack – it’s grody, but also amplifies the swordplay in a unique way.I’ll continue diving deeper into Capcom’s return to Onimusha as part of Game Informer’s month of Way of the Sword coverage. Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: Humble Games Developer: Chuhai Labs Release: August 13, 2026 (PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC), 2026 (Switch) Rating: Everyone 10+ Wild Blue Skies is not shy about the video game experience it is trying to emulate. You are Bowie, an anthropomorphic dog in a spaceship making your way through a series of levels, most on rails, some taking place in small open areas, to defeat the evil Grimclaw, an anthropomorphic tiger with a giant cyborg head. Developer Chuhai Labs and Vitei Backroom Inc. are transparently presenting their take on Star Fox 64 – a noble and welcome endeavor – but many shortcomings prevent it from reaching the nostalgic heights of its inspirational arcade action game. Mechanically, controlling your ship feels okay, but it dips into slog territory more often than I would like. The perspective of the reticle makes it hard to see what you’re firing at (and there is no first-person option), and hitting the occasional wall bounces you around like a pinball. It can be especially frustrating on the open-ended levels that are not on rails. Locking on and firing your standard lasers are also mapped to separate buttons, meaning you have to choose between the two actions, which is limiting. Also, the range to lock on to fire an extra powerful homing shot is too short. By the time I locked on to enemies, it was often too late, and the enemy had disappeared off-screen.The look of Wild Blue Skies is attractive, though, with bright colors and cool ship designs. I like the look of the main cast and their performances, along with the performances of all the bosses and secondary characters. Everyone’s characterization, however, leaves a lot on the table. The bosses all boast and yell with unfounded confidence, and I felt bad when teammates complained when I accidentally (allegedly) shot them. But even with the quality acting, everyone is undefined and generic. Dialogue could be swapped among the characters, I don’t think I would even notice.The levels offer some visual variety, but are also generic. Moving from a desert level to an ice level is a nice change of pace, but little has been done to make them feel novel. Also, many of the on-rails levels begin with characters pointing out the limited visibility, whether in a storm or from snow. Narratively, I like flying through a hurricane in a game like this, but when the elements keep making things hard to see, it feels like a story excuse for poor draw distance. When you do make it through the hailstorm, or the tornado, or the lava flows, the bosses at the end arguably take too long to defeat. Like its Star Fox 64 inspiration, there are incentives to replay the campaign to chase different challenges and see the alternate paths. Seeing the game to credits is completely doable, but I got frustrated by moments of unfair challenge. Health drops are infrequent, and deaths are often the result of running into a stray enemy, bouncing against walls, or getting overwhelmed by screen-filling danger that I didn’t feel equipped to avoid. It made many defeats feel like the game’s fault rather than my own, which is especially annoying when burning through lives sends you back to the beginning of the campaign.Bowie and pals’ adventure successfully impersonates the Star Fox 64 experience, but it’s an underwhelming impression. Nostalgic moments and satisfying action appear occasionally, but it pays homage to a fault and doesn’t find space to present its own personality. It has the unfortunate timing of releasing and being compared to this year’s Star Fox on Switch 2 – an excellent remake of Star Fox 64. But even if I did not have that game in my immediate memory, I would be hard-pressed to identify anywhere that it has improved on the genre with design lessons learned since 1997, or even risen to meet the ones that already existed even 30 years ago. Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: PlayStation Studios Developer: Arc System Works Release: August 6, 2026 Despite its origins as a comic book publisher, Marvel is fighting game royalty on par with the likes of Mortal Kombat and The King of Fighters. But after the disappointing Marvel vs. Capcom Infinite fell massively short of expectations, and with the release of Arc System Works' Dragon Ball FighterZ giving hyper tag-team fighting game fans a new home, Marvel's reign in the fighting game world felt at its end. Just shy of a decade since Infinite, however, Arc System Works delivers Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls, a fighter that is as much of an advancement to the genre as it is an homage to the Marvel vs. Capcom era.Marvel Tōkon is a 4v4 fighting game, but it’s no ordinary tag fighter. Each team shares a single health bar, but you can effectively play this as a 1v1 fighting game where your team's only purpose is to come in for assists. This helps retain the high-octane fighting fans are accustomed to without the need to memorize four entire movesets. Super and special moves are also assigned to simple button presses alongside more standard fighting game inputs, making it easier to pick up any fighter.Matches have a unique rhythm as they start with your main character and one assist, then you gradually gain access to your additional teammates. Not only does this style allow drastic momentum shifts, but it encourages you to be on the attack to gain control of your entire arsenal as soon as possible, making matches exhilarating at times.  While the hard-fought matches I played online aptly demonstrate the combo system’s depth, it’s still approachable. Despite my typical struggles with longer combos, Marvel Tōkon’s character-agnostic control uniformity helps keep the combat approachable for a wider audience. The characters are instead differentiated by unique traits and dedicated specials, which range from Doctor Doom creating barriers to Green Goblin summoning his glider.Arc System Works is known for over-the-top fighting and absolutely gorgeous visuals, and Marvel Tōkon sits alongside Guilty Gear Strive as one of the best-looking fighting games to date. Arc's signature 3D models that look like 2D drawings remain as impressive as they were back when we first saw Guilty Gear Xrd. Just as Dragon Ball FighterZ advanced on what Xrd did, Tōkon feels like another visual jump.A major part of the visual praise belongs to the character designs, which are all unique, fantastic, and tasteful. The anime-inspired style just works for this game; Iron Man, for example, has armor that looks ripped out of a Mecha anime, while Hulk’s exaggerated proportions and armor make him as huge and intimidating as he deserves to be. Story mode comes in the form of Episodes about teams in a worldwide tournament to take on a cosmic entity, The Champion, who has been travelling the universe and ending planets he deems unworthy. These narratives are told through visual novel scenes featuring the pre-set teams interspersed with fights. Each Episode takes around an hour to beat, and they’re all basic and predictable. Most stories follow the same format aside from a few differences in the Knights of Doom's path.  Episodes also don't serve as a tutorial in the same way story modes in fighting games sometimes do, with only very basic introductions to each character. Tōkon does have fairly in-depth training and tutorial options, but the character-specific tutorials are little more than some combo trials. They are useful, but don't really teach you the full mentality and gameplans behind each character. Online is standard for Arc games: lobbies, ranked and casual matches, plus a silly eight-player side mode called Arcadia Rumble. The netcode is great, with barely any noticeable issues when I've played, even over Wi-Fi. A fighting game's impact is difficult to determine at launch due to balancing updates and the community's approach to it. Given the package Arc has delivered here, though, Marvel Tōkon is a more than worthy successor to Marvel vs. Capcom and Dragon Ball FighterZ. I believe it has the potential to become a fighting game mainstay for years to come. Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
