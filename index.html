Stranger Than Heaven Is One Man's Story Of Biculturalism, Music, Fitting In, And Fighting, Told Over 50 YearsNEW

Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Sega Developer: Ryu Ga Gotoku Studios Release: January 15, 2027 Stranger Than Heaven is one of the early 2027 releases we're most looking forward to, thanks in large part to it being an ambitious title that tells a prequel story following the same man over the course of 50 years. Players control Makoto Daito, a half-American half-Japanese man stuck between two cultures, struggling to fully fit in with either. In 1915, he stows away on a San Francisco ship bound for Japan in an attempt to escape the prejudice he experienced in the United States as an Asian man. However, once he arrives in Japan, he doesn't quite find the culture of acceptance he expected. As he wanders the streets, pulling off jobs for Orpheus (portrayed by rapper Snoop Dogg), I hear the folks Kokura, where Makoto landed in Japan, jeering him behind his back. It's only one local, Tae Matsumoto, who seems to see Makoto for who he is. Makoto goes to the bar where Tae works, in an attempt to undermine a rival gang, but once he spends an evening with her, he confesses the entire thing before asking Tae out on a real date. Unfortunately for Makoto, Tae is the mistress of the Oki Clan, and when Oki finds out that Makoto is spending time with his mistress, he puts a hit on Makoto and steals Tae away. Makoto leads a siege against the ironworks mill where Oki has stolen Tae, resulting in the most climactic clash of my three hours with Stranger Than Heaven: a battle against the hulking Shimura. There, I really put my mastery of the unique combat system, which corresponds the shoulder buttons to your arms and the triggers to your legs for a more direct control of the combos you throw. Since Shimura uses a huge melee weapon in his second phase, I also used my weapon of choice: a knife. Using the nimbleness afforded by a lightweight weapon, I dodged his advances and finished him off to save Tae. But Kokura in 1915 is hardly the only setting of the game, as players will follow Makoto's exploits across five decades, visiting Kure in 1919, Osaka in 1943, Atami in 1951, and, finally, Shinjuku in 1965. I had a chance to free-roam explore both Kure and Osaka. I experienced the fighting arena of Kure, where you challenge increasingly difficult opponents (and can re-challenge defeated foes with special win conditions), and stumbled upon a masked serial killer who gave me the toughest fight of my life. I also found a composer, who can help craft songs based on sounds from which Makoto pulls inspiration; during my playthrough, Makoto found inspiration in everything from a crying baby in a house he walked by to the clang of his metallic melee weapon cracking against a thug's skull. In the Osaka sequence, the attitude toward Makoto has shifted dramatically; men and women alike run up to and greet him with bright smiles due to his work in organized crime and music showbiz. To that end, I use this gameplay section to live in that world. After negotiating with local business owners to buy ad space on their billboards, I have Makoto put on a show at a local theater. Here, I can choose the program, performers, and then take part in an interactive performance.I left my hands-on session extremely impressed by the many layers present in Stranger Than Heaven. Following my play sessions, I sat down with Stranger Than Heaven director Mikinobu Abe and lead actor Yu Shirota to discuss the themes and inspiration behind RGG Studio's new entry in the Yakuza/Like a Dragon universe.When you're getting to the point where you're conceptualizing your next game, what are the conversations like that led you to creating a prequel title to the Yakuza/Like a Dragon series with a unique premise like we see in Stranger Than Heaven?Mikinobu Abe, Director: In the beginning, there wasn't about, "Okay, let's make this game as something that's gonna connect to the Yakuza or Like a Dragon franchise." It was something more of we always wondered while making Yakuza and Like a Dragon games how would someone create such an organization, like a yakuza organization, and that could be an interesting narrative to focus on. In the Yakuza franchise, we have the Tojo Clan or the Omi Alliance, but we never actually told why or who would create such a thing. So, the starting point was from there, and then, as we were developing the narrative, we added all sorts of elements.One of the greatest ideas for the game is making the main character of mixed heritage of Japanese and American. I think that decision really opened up a whole new narrative to tell for the studio. So, from there, we did want to tell the beginning of such an organization, but it wasn't like we wanted to tell a prologue of the Yakuza or Like a Dragon series. We already had the mind of, like, "Okay, we want to make this as something brand new and like nothing that we have made before." And with the theme of biculturalism relating to Makoto Daito, why was telling the story of existing in that "in between" space where maybe you don't feel like you belong in either culture such an important story to tell with this game?MA: At the beginning, it wasn't a story about mixed heritage; it was just one Japanese guy who's trying to create an organization. New York Comic Con can be a strange place sometimes, but this year, Sega gave select media the opportunity to make it stranger (than heaven). We went hands-on with developer Ryu ga Gotoku's next major project, and while our extended look offered glimpses into the decades-spanning adventure, there's clearly still a lot more to see.  The three-hour demo was broken up into five parts. The first three were all set in 1915, first on a ship headed to Japan, and then in the harbor town of Kokura. These three sections introduced key characters, a major conflict in protagonist Makoto's early life, and a climactic battle in an ironworks factory.  The later two sections highlighted two other cities and eras: 1929's Kure and 1943's Osaka. Both sections were centered around the multiple open-world activities we'll find in each of Stranger Than Heaven's time periods, from minigames and short missions to the side content involving Showbiz show-planning.  There was a lot to take in during the demo, which reinforced the massive scope that director Minikobu Abe and the RGG Studio team are going for in the full game. After we played the game, we sat down with Abe to learn more about feedback received from previous demos, the inspiration behind Stranger Than Heaven's story, and more. Stranger Than Heaven seems to be shaping up to be a massive game, with an emotional story at its core. Based on what I played, it should be capable of standing out, rather than being lost in the shadow of RGG Studio's Like A Dragon series. A fish out of water Stranger Than Heaven tells the story of Makoto Daito, a half-American, half-Japanese man who finds himself torn between two cultures. In the prologue, he's chased by American policemen in San Francisco who use words like "oriental;" while in Japan, he's greeted with "keto," a derogatory slang word for non-Japanese people that literally translates to "hairy foreigner." While these moments are set over 100 years ago in 1915, Abe says that the overarching themes are very much still relevant today. "Questioning your identity is one big theme for a lot of people around the world, especially with the internet, and I think it really resonates with people," Abe said. "We thought making a character who's mixed race could spark a huge interest and a strong message, like it could give the courage for people to live their lives." Abe also mentioned that he knows firsthand the feeling of being considered an outsider, even in his home country. "I have a personal experience living abroad and then coming back to Japan; I'm not of mixed heritage, and yet people still treated me like a gaijin, which is a foreign outsider," Abe said. "[Telling this story] really resonated with me; I really wanted to tell this story as a director, and that was the entire starting point on how we got to this concept." Along with the themes associated with Makoto's mixed race, the Kokura sections of the demo also focused on the infancy of the yakuza, which was another key point of exploration for the team. The Like A Dragon series up until now has told stories of an established crime syndicate, but for Abe, his curiosity focused on where it began.  "While we were creating the Yakuza and Like a Dragon series, we always had this curiosity of who would create such an organization, or why they would create it, but we didn't have a great way of exploring it," Abe said. This theme led to the team deciding to tell the story over five decades, as they "started to feel like we need a huge time span to tell this epic story." Packing a punch My main concern coming into this demo, after having played it at Summer Game Fest back in June, was with the core combat system. Using the shoulder and trigger buttons on top of the controller to fight was awkward at times, with my attacks feeling slow and deliberate and difficult to combo together. The parry system was especially underwhelming, as the timing never felt consistent enough to wrap my head around.  Right from the start of this hands-on session, it was obvious that whatever work went into tweaking the fighting system between June and October is the right choice. This demo's mechanics felt much more refined and responsive compared to what I played at Summer Game Fest. My attacks were landing consistently, and I could hit combos with ease. Parries still gave me fits at times, but it felt like something I could improve on rather than an issue with the mechanic. The finishing blows felt powerful, especially with a weapon in my hands; landing a finisher on an opponent with the giant hammer was one of my favorite parts of the entire experience, and I did it a lot. Abe acknowledged that previous demos played a role in improving the combat system, but he revealed that the feedback wasn't considered negative by the team. "We tweaked a lot, but our takeaway from the feedback wasn't like, 'Okay, we might want to change our creative direction,'" Abe said. "Rather, as a whole, the core system that we believed would be fun and fit for this game was reinforced. It really gave us the motivation boost after Summer Game Fest that we are heading in the right direction." That reinforcement presented itself later in the demo, as a few of the more challenging enemies from back in June reappeared for another faceoff. The lone samurai showed up in both open-world sections of our demo, Kure and Osaka, and both times I defeated him on our first encounter.   The hulking bald man, meanwhile, turned out to be Shimura, the head of the ironworks that Makoto infiltrates in Kokura and a main partner of major antagonist Oki. Shimura emerged as the main boss of the Kokura section of the demo, and while he was still a tough fight, the combat improvements made it much more manageable to defeat him.  Speaking of the ironworks section, this part of the demo threw me an interesting curveball; once Shimura was defeated, I assumed a cutscene would play before I then faced off with Oki in the office above where I battled Shimura. Instead, Oki immediately started firing his gun at Makoto through the window, and I had to quickly run up the stairs and take Oki down before the cutscene played. It was small, but it was a defiance of my expectations that did not go unnoticed. "Fictional realism" Throughout our interview, Abe kept referencing a term he and his team use to describe what Stranger Than Heaven is striving for: "fictional realism," or the idea that, while the game is rooted in real places authentic to the time period, it still takes creative freedoms in certain elements of the game.  One example, as Abe points out, is the use of jazz throughout the game; while jazz has existed for decades, the types of jazz featured in Stranger Than Heaven aren't necessarily authentic to the eras they're heard in. "Jazz has, like, a million genres within it, right? If you ask an expert of jazz--which our music director [Mitsuharu] Fukuyama-san is, he'd say, 'This jazz wouldn't exist until like 60 years later,'" Abe said.  The demo provides multiple examples of this "fictional realism" idea through its minigames, as they are a mixture of real-world games from the past and activities that might not appear as they did back then, if they appeared at all. Multiple authentic Japanese games from the time, like oicho-kabu, a card game similar to baccarat, and tehonbiki, a guessing game using cards numbered from one to six, can be found in multiple areas. Both of these date back centuries, to when nanban karuta playing cards were introduced to Japan by the Portuguese in the 16th century.  Other activities straddle the line of possibly being real, but with some doubt. Underground fight clubs in 1929's Kure may have been a thing in real life, but here Makoto can challenge opponents and make some real money when he wins. Similarly, we don't know if rolling cigarettes was a regular occurrence in 1915's Kokura, nor do we think cigarettes helped boost fighting capabilities, but both of those things exist here in Stranger Than Heaven--and they're introduced by Orpheus, who is portrayed by Snoop Dogg, because who else would? (Now that we've mentioned Snoop Dogg, we should also confirm that Amaru, the character bearing the likeness of the late Tupac Shakur, was not in this hands-on demo.)  The Showbiz sections, meanwhile, are where those creative liberties mentioned by Abe really come to light. In our hands-on, we were tasked with helping Makoto to plan a musical performance. We had to choose the performer, the band members, the song, and extras like confetti and lighting. As long as the performance makes more than the cost, we succeed.  Makoto can find rhythms and sounds out in the world based on environmental noise, a neat way to tie the surrounding area into the player's consciousness. Some sources are obvious, like a man clapping in the street, while others are clever, like picking sounds out of the clash of weapons or the contact of Makoto's first during a fight. When a colored orb of light appears on-screen, by pressing a button, Makoto registers the sound and it becomes part of his repertoire the next time he plans a performance. When choosing the song specifically, we have a selection of genres for certain tracks. And Should I Sing Of Love, for example, can either be performed as country blues or New Orleans jazz, while other songs can be played as a slow boogie, a lively pop tune, or a parade-like march. This small selection points to a very robust catalog of music and musical genres, and I'm already looking forward to how much music Makoto can cook up in the final game. Sega packed a ton of content into our three-hour hands-on with Stranger Than Heaven, and we felt like we barely scratched the surface. Resonance wears the HD-2D aesthetic confidently; Square Enix has obviously listened to past criticisms of its beloved graphical style, and it paid off. Resonance is an HD-2D game, but its highly detailed character sprites and occasional jaw-dropping pixel-based "cinema" scene differentiate it from the Octopath Traveler series and the Dragon Quest HD-2D remakes.  Resonance's distinctions are its greatest strength, in fact. It's easy to assume at first blush that its story about a boy and his best friend is nothing but a table to prop retro Final Fantasy references upon. It's true that a key part of Resonance's gameplay involves summoning "Visions" into battle, said visions being projections of characters from other Final Fantasy games. But that's all they are: Projections. They teach transferable skills, à la Final Fantasy V's job system. Rain's roommate, Lasswell, contains notes of my beloved Kain Highwind from Final Fantasy IV, but there's a troubled nobility to him that's purely his own.
