The Splatoon franchise has historically focused on chaotic multiplayer mayhem that pits teams against each other to see who can emerge from the paint victorious. While the games have featured campaigns, these supplementary modes have largely felt more like training grounds than fully fleshed-out experiences. That all changes with Splatoon Raiders. Splatoon Raiders is the series' first foray into a strictly campaign-focused adventure, unleashing players onto a mysterious set of islands to search for treasure and find a way home. And since this game lacks the competitive multiplayer modes that have defined the franchise so far, that means you'll be tackling objectives at your own pace, either alone or in online co-op. If you want to know just how long it might take you to do so, let's take a look. How long is Splatoon Raiders? An average playthrough of Splatoon Raiders will take around 15-18 hours. This includes playing through the game's main missions while engaging moderately in its optional content, such as completing tasks that reward additional outfits, tinkering with dozens of weapon variations, and searching for some hidden Salmonid Relic collectibles. Of course, the time spent beating the game will also vary based on which of the three difficulty choices you pick: Tourist (Easy), Raider (Medium), or Survivalist (Hard). Fully completing the game is far more challenging, of course, and can take considerably longer to accomplish. Collecting every weapon, gadget, outfit, and Salmonid Relic can add significant playtime to your save file. After beating the campaign, you can dive back in alone or with friends to tackle a difficult endgame that demands you consistently upgrade your gear and prepare well for each raid. If you're aiming to complete Splatoon Raiders' post-game campaign, you can easily push past the 40-hour mark--but the fun doesn't stop there, either. Much of the appeal of this game is playing with friends (and strangers), meaning there's a lot of fun to be had coming to the aid of others with your powerful gear and assisting them in taking on harder and harder challenges. So, even once you've seen much of what the game has to offer, those who truly enjoy the gameplay loop can find dozens of additional hours of gameplay in working as a team, grinding out even more goodies, and slowly amassing stronger gear. Maybe Not Everything Needs To Be A Fortnite CollabNEW
Honestly, I thought I could no longer be surprised by the off-the-wall collaborations between Fortnite and various other IPs. At this point in Fortnite's very lucrative lifespan, the general rule of t...
Honestly, I thought I could no longer be surprised by the off-the-wall collaborations between Fortnite and various other IPs. At this point in Fortnite's very lucrative lifespan, the general rule of thumb seems to be, "If it's popular, it's gonna end up in Fortnite." Damn near every Marvel Cinematic Universe character has made an appearance, countless musical artists have held in-game concerts, and Epic has always been very good at making sure that whatever the internet is obsessed with at any given moment ends up tied to the game in one way or another. The problem is that the internet is currently obsessed with Curry Barker's wildly successful horror film, Obsession. Epic Games and Blumhouse Productions apparently thought this would be a good time to release a collaborative ad for both Fortnite and Barker's film, which just hit streaming services after an extended theatrical run. The one-minute ad features various "jump-scare" moments from Obsession, but replaces the film's female lead, Nikki (Inde Navarrette), with a Galaxy Zero Point Sprite (the rarest collectible Sprite in Fortnite, with a drop rate of roughly 1 in 300 million). Instead of using a One Wish Willow as he did in the film, main character Bear (Michael Johnston) is shown snapping a "One Llama Luck" stick in half, causing the Sprite to become obsessed with him. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HLxvcsKhwKM Editor's note: The following includes major spoilers for Obsession, and also contains references to sexual assault. If you haven't seen the film, the video reads as a silly little nod to how obsessed Fortnite players are with the extremely rare Sprite. If you have seen the film, this entire ad spot is baffling at best and offensive at worst. In Obsession, Nikki loses her autonomy after Bear wishes that Nikki "loved [him] more than anyone in the f***ing world." Bear gets his wish, but it quickly becomes apparent that the real Nikki is trapped inside her body, unable to control it as she has been possessed by whatever magic lies within the One Wish Willow. Even after it becomes painfully clear that the version of Nikki that "loves" him isn't the real Nikki, Bear continues to act as if she is his girlfriend, repeatedly raping her and pretending they have a normal relationship, despite the fact that the real Nikki occasionally claws her way to the surface and begs him to free her. In short, Bear is the villain, but he's not the sort of villain the audience is meant to like or relate to. He is dangerously obsessive, dishonest, and manipulative, and consistently makes the most selfish move every time he's presented with a choice. He's also, y'know, a rapist. Obsession's "twist" is the fact that, despite her horrific behavior while possessed, Nikki is the victim, and Bear is the aggressor. By the time the film ends, Nikki has regained her autonomy but is all alone, surrounded by the corpses of the people she once called friends--one of whom turned out to be a rapist, and two of whom Nikki killed against her will while possessed. The credits roll not to the tune of an outro song, but to the sound of Nikki's horrified sobs. How anyone watched this film and came to the conclusion it would pair well with a humorous Fortnite ad is beyond me. The ad completely misses the point of Obsession: Bear is the monster. Nikki's only "scary" because she's possessed, and whatever entity is controlling her doesn't know how to human quite right (plus the real Nikki is trapped inside, fighting it every step of the way). This leads to strange behavior that freaks Bear out, as seen in the ad. But putting this cutesy Sprite in Nikki's place, framing it as, "Oooh, the adorable sprite is so menacing, haha!" and playing it for laughs is frankly disgusting.  I get the joke; it's just not funny. I get that players are "obsessed" with the Sprites. But an ad that puts the playerbase into Bear's shoes--you know, the shoes of a rapist and all-around asshole--as if to say, "Your obsession with these sprites is cute and harmless, just like Bear's obsession with Nikki!" is wildly inappropriate and off-putting. It really feels as though Epic pounced on the chance to associate a popular film with its game, without considering the content or context of that film at all. Even if Epic had considered the context of the film and somehow managed to make a slightly less offensive ad, it would still feel out of place (and out of touch). Fortnite's playerbase tends to skew younger, with Epic's own materials stating the target demographic is 13- to 26-year-old players, and Obsession is an R-rated horror film about the nuances of rape culture. Fortnite has run into trouble with its crossovers before, and this one maybe won't cause a stir in part because it's apparently only an ad meant to drive Peacock subscriptions and not represented in the game itself. But it points to a startling lack of foresight and empathy on the part of Epic Games, which cannot seem to comprehend the fact that not every popular film IP is appropriate fodder for the ever-growing world of Fortnite, especially if the marketing team is going to miss the point this badly.

Onimusha: Way of the Sword is the latest game to grace the cover of Game Informer, and while the game has made quite a splash in the samurai-action scene, it’s easy to forget this is the first entry in the series in 20 years. Yes, we received remasters of the first and second games in recent years, and Onimusha: Dawn of Dreams (the fourth mainline game) is coming to PlayStation Plus’ Classics lineup next month, but Way of the Sword is the first new entry in two decades. That’s why, when it was time to interview the game’s director, Satoru Nihei, and one of its producers, Akihito Kadowaki, at Capcom’s Osaka, Japan, HQ for this cover story, my first question was: Why is 2026 the time to bring back Onimusha? I half-suspected it was an issue of sales, considering the series has only sold 9 million copies, a far cry from Resident Evil’s 200 million, Monster Hunter’s 127 million, and even Street Fighter’s 59 million. The other half of me assumed Capcom had simply forgotten about Onimusha – after the first game, Onimusha: Warlords (which, after recently playing the remaster on Steam, I can confidently say still absolutely rips), launched in 2001, we received three sequels in the following five years. And that was it: Onimusha disappeared as fast as it arrived. Kadowaki tells me, however, that Onimusha’s departure from the action space wasn’t for lack of trying. “[Whilst Onimusha] was always well-received, there were, at Capcom at the time, other major series like Monster Hunter, Resident Evil games, and Street Fighter, but there were also up-and-coming developing titles,” he says. “And as development became more difficult, needing more time and more people, with all those people focused on those titles, it was very difficult to get a team together to make a new Onimusha game, despite wanting to internally for a long time.” Kadowaki says he personally wanted to have a hand in bringing the series back as a longtime fan of Onimusha. It took the RE Engine coming to fruition and a soft reset in 2020 for Capcom, as the publisher-developer looked at scheduling out the new/now-current generation of hardware and the games it intended to make for it, for Onimusha’s return. The team has been working on Way of the Sword for six years, and Kadowaki tells me that while Capcom paid attention to the action space and to what players expect of it, the team worked hard to ensure Way of the Sword retained everything that makes an Onimusha game an Onimusha game. He also specifically asked Nihei to direct Way of the Sword, aware that Nihei loved the games as a teen and wanted to make his mark on the series. Much to Nihei’s surprise, his opportunity to make a mark on Onimusha also became his first time as a director.  When I ask Nihei where one even starts when making a new game in a series that’s been dormant for 20 years, he responds with one word: Issen. Present in every Onimusha game, the Issen is a powerful counterattack that players can perform by attacking just before an enemy attacks, often resulting in a critical hit. If you’re an Onimusha fan, it’s likely something you want to hear when it comes to this series’ return to the action space.“[This] was one of the critical parts of that satisfaction and the feel of the period action, and the Oni Gauntlet, the tactical elements of it in terms of soul absorption, kind of weighing when to use those abilities and when not to, and then also the elements of the historical ties to the real history of Japan,” Nihei says. “Obviously, the previous titles were set in the Sengoku period, so the Warring States period, but just mixing those historical elements with dark fantasy elements is the core of Onimusha.”This is just the first of many Onimusha: Way of the Sword articles I have planned for Game Informer’s month of coverage surrounding the game, and we’ll be publishing exclusive gameplay and behind-the-scenes videos (as well as a Rapid Fire Interview) on the Game Informer YouTube channel too. The Incident At Galley House Review – A Clever Blast From The PastNEW
Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PC Publisher: Evil Trout Developer: William Rous, Evil Trout Release: July 14, 2026 A big part of me wants to forgo explaining what makes The Incident of Galley House great for fear of revealing too much; to just say “trust me, play it. It’s good.” Unraveling the truth behind the unsolved deaths of a group of people gathering at a mansion one stormy night in 1936 is engrossing and inventive, with a tale that keeps you guessing until its final moments. Although I didn’t get all of the answers I wanted, I was immensely impressed with the revelations I did uncover and the journey to find them.  The Incident of Galley House is a reimagined take on the 2025 horror text game, Type Help, by William Rous that has been remastered and expanded by Evil Trout, creators of The Roottrees Are Dead. Players control a researcher who visits the condemned Galley House where multiple dead bodies were discovered decades prior; the causes of their deaths are unknown. Using a machine capable of reconstructing scenes from the past to watch in the present, you piece together the conversations, actions, and whereabouts of each person during the ill-fated hours leading to their demise. The present-day premise was fascinating enough to draw me in, but the events of the past hooked me. A man is invited to Galley House by someone named Rupert Galley, but none of the residence’s many guests have heard of this supposed host. This begs many questions: Who is Rupert Galley, and why did he invite this man? Why have these visitors gathered this night? And, most importantly, why and how is everyone dying? Like The Roottrees are Dead, The Incident at Galley House quickly becomes a fun, all-consuming exercise of gleaning context clues from conversations and physical evidence to uncover new information that gradually reveals the full events of a 26-segment timeline. Doing so requires engaging with an inventive system of correctly entering numerical codes that indicate the location, time, and people (who are only identified by a number until you discover their names) to find scenes matching those criteria. The experience of deducing who did what and where reminds me of Clue; I got a kick out of figuring out if, for example, two characters were in the living room during a specific time based on information I learned from a different, chronologically later scene I happened to unlock first.I appreciate how much flexibility The Incident at Galley House allows for filling out the timeline. You can follow each character’s night one at a time, focus only on reaching the end of the timeline by advancing numerous character threads (and backfill missing scenes later), or do your best to learn every character's whereabouts chronologically before moving too far ahead. I enjoy the agency in how to approach the investigation, and the game is constructed to allow multiple paths to solving its problems. The story is an immaculately well-written web of clues and references that rewards you for paying attention to not only what’s being said (both at face value and the subtext) but also the visual elements of a scene and the layout of Galley House's many areas, which in themselves must first be discovered. Revisiting a curious line of dialogue with new perspectives on the person or situation to find information hidden in plain sight never stopped feeling satisfying or, in some cases, mind-blowing. Since you follow 26 scenes for each of the game’s dozen or so characters, it’s easy to become overwhelmed by the amount of dialogue and information you have to keep track of. Thankfully, The Incident at Galley House offers many helpful tools to make sense of it all. The notepad from The Rootrees are Dead returns, letting you take in-game notes and save quotes directly from dialogue transcripts. Scenes can be paused so you can re-read and highlight key text before resuming them. A search engine surfaces conversations based on keywords, and you can even place markers on the timeline to indicate events, such as a character death, at a glance. The Incident of Galley House also communicates how many locked cinematics remain discoverable in each scene, which was one of my favorite time-saving features from The Rootrees are Dead. Though I didn’t need to use every tool often, if at all, I was happy to have them.You don’t need to find every scene to solve the mystery, but I was so fascinated by not only the overarching story, but also the smaller questions surrounding Galley House’s cast that I obsessively played until I was sure I’d seen everything. The voice performances are generally good, save for some weirdly muted reactions whenever someone learned of another’s death that sometimes took me out of the moment. I enjoyed getting to know Galley Houses’ colorful visitors and grew to like some of them enough that I felt a growing existential dread knowing they wouldn’t make it out of the house alive. Speaking of dread, the game’s eerie vibes and presentation add a welcome layer of creepiness to the already dark subject matter – and the game taps into its unsettling atmosphere for some effective hair-raising moments.The Incident at Galley House constantly made me reevaluate my theories and approach, and several big twists spurred my search for answers throughout many late-night sessions. Sadly, I found the conclusion to be a mixed bag. The story purposely withholds the biggest explanation I’d been searching for while still offering enough significant ancillary explanations to avoid falling completely flat. It’s an artistic choice that may resonate with some, but after so many hours of searching and concocting numerous wild theories, I wanted a more concrete answer than what the game provides. Still, I had a blast figuring out the fates of these doomed souls, and I’d wager any puzzle or adventure game fan will too.   Score: 8.75 About Game Informer's review system
