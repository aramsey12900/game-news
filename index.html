Fortnite's new Treasure Hunters season is such a massive success that the battle royale's player counts are five times higher than last week, because everybody and their mothers want in on the search for Tim Sweeney's golden ticket. It grants everything in the item shop for free, both for two weeks this year and two more weeks in 2027. That means two things: Most of the people playing Fortnite right now haven't been playing recently, and folks aren't necessarily playing to win. The way the hunt works is that every time you or one of your teammates opens a treasure chest, there's a chance that a rare prize will be granted to the whole party. It could be a golden A$AP Rocky skin, a Mercedes G-Wagon skin, or the extremely elusive Pirate Shop Access token, which makes virtually the whole shop free. The hunt for that Pirate Shop token in particular has changed the way people play BR. The primary goal for many players is now not to win, but to open as many chests as possible in pursuit of the token. Since your whole squad gets any of these treasures regardless of who found them, four-player Squad fills have been the place to go for the hunt--understandably, most of us don't have enough friends who want to play Fortnite 12 hours a day to keep the squad filled on our own. Playing Squad fills has always been a bit of crapshoot in terms of the quality of your teammates, but the new treasure hunt has folks acting a lot more erratic than usual. While playing entirely too much Fortnite this week, I've identified a few player profiles--let's call them recurring characters--that I've been encountering over and over again. While your teammates each game are randomly assigned, they typically follow one of a handful of archetypes during the hunt. I've played about 100 games of Squad fills since the new Treasure Hunters mini-season began on Sunday, and nearly all of my teammates ended up being one of these types of players. The Five People You Meet In Fortnite's Treasure Hunt The Looter This person is on a mission to open as many chests as they possibly can, and they will ignore everything else going on during the match. They might land near you, but they won't help you if you get into a fight, because they have something more important to do. A lot of these folks have so little desire to engage with the actual game that they end up hanging out deep in the storm, looting by themselves for most of every match. These types sometimes come in pairs. The Loner This person will land as far away from the rest of the team as they can, and then they'll proceed to play the whole match as if they were in solos because they think the key to min/maxing the squads chest-looting is to spread out as far as possible. If they don't die immediately, you'll forget they exist until the rest of your team wipes and you all end up spectating this guy doing whatever. The Hunter This person is the Looter's natural predator. They just want to get into fights, and they know this influx of new players and folks who haven't played in a while, most of whom are more interested in looting than fighting, is a great opportunity to pad their stats. But these folks are so aggressive that they either end up running off alone and wiping the lobby by themselves or getting killed quickly--but it's usually the latter. The AFKer This type just shows up and does nothing at all. They drop from the bus at the end of the line and float in the water until the storm kills them. During any other season, we would assume this kind of behavior is an accident, but it's happening too often for that to be the case right now. These folks are here to let other folks farm chests for them. The Regular Player There are still some people who play Fortnite the usual way, sticking together and looting carefully and playing strategically as a team in hopes of pulling out a win. These folks know that the chance of getting a Pirate Shop token is infinitesimally small, and so they largely just play the way they normally would and don't worry about the prizes. With how preoccupied so many people are with the Treasure Hunt, if you manage to pull a squad with two or three of these folks, you're probably going to win the round. Fortunately, this state of affairs won't last forever. The Pirate Shop token will only be around during this One Piece-themed mini-season, which only lasts until December 5. Let's hope the Treasure Hunt doesn't burn everyone out before Chapter 8 starts.

Being obsessed with zombie fiction for as long as I have been, I'm comfortable saying No More Room in Hell 2 is the year's best zombie game. Since I first played it in early access two years ago, I've loved its Romero-inspired vibes and slower, more punishing take on a Left 4 Dead-style game--doubling the co-op team size to eight players is a fun wrinkle, too.  So imagine my surprise when I recently hopped back into the game and found its latest update added a map that goes in a much different direction, trading its Night of the Living Dead inspirations for something much more at home in Friday the 13th. The Camp Blood update brings a slasher-inspired summer-camp map, Ruby Lake, to the game's wave-based Survival mode, which lives alongside its more campaign-like Scenario mode. Not that I wasn't open to the pivot, but as a horror obsessive, I wanted to know what inspired the new direction, even as the game's Romero-esque core is still well-represented in all other maps and modes. "Romero is ingrained in our DNA as far back as the original game, but we've always said that films and television, more than other games, are our main source of inspiration. This inspiration transcends just Romero and extends into horror as a broader theme," creative principal Matt Kazan told me via email, going on to name a parade of my favorite things (plus CW's long-running monster-of-the-week series).  "Whether it's Stephen King, '80s slashers, Alfred Hitchcock, Evil Dead, Supernatural, or even weird stuff like Lost or Twin Peaks, and even Backrooms, our inspirations come from all sorts of places." Associate art director Jon Wiley expanded on that point, explaining how future updates will sometimes bring similarly surprising twists to the world, but not without purpose. Maps are "heavily influenced by the stories we want to tell," he said, adding that their maps are meant to have the same thematic relevance as Romero had in mind when he cast a black man in a major role in Night of the Living Dead so the writer-director could grapple with the problem of racism, or how he set Dawn of the Dead in a mall in order to make a point about mindless consumerism."NMRIH2 is inherently a fairly depressing game world, with the player and their team being a small light in that darkness," Wiley continued. "You aren’t a superhuman, but you do embody hope when the odds are stacked against us. We want to explore what other groups of people are doing in the region. Not everyone is emotionally handling it as well as the CRC. Not everyone views it through the same scientific lens, especially with how vivid the zombie-virus infections feel, and that leads to super-interesting stories that we haven’t told yet." As much as I've loved playing the game, I've also been unexpectedly drawn to the conversation around it. Since its early-access debut two years ago, I've closely followed how community sentiment has changed over time. Some updates would set the game back, upsetting its core early-access players while prompting the team at Torn Banner to tear down ideas they planned to build on. Watching its Steam rating roller-coaster to such highs and lows for two years was unlike anything else I recall seeing on the platform. Because I care so much about finding the best zombie games and sometimes felt like I enjoyed this one more than the consensus, I found myself glued not only to the updates but to players' reactions. Hidemaro Fujibayashi Fujibayashi: Regarding the original Ocarina of Time, at the time I was working for a different company, so it was mainly me as an end user trying to looking forward to the experience of what a 3D Zelda is going to look like. The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) Review - Exceptionally FamiliarNEW

Reviewed on: Switch 2 Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Nintendo Release: November 5, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time for the Nintendo 64 is the most important video game ever made… to me. My byline would not appear in the pages of Game Informer without my original experience playing that game. But it is nearly 30 years old, and despite establishing many important staples of 3D gaming, its age is becoming more apparent. Ocarina of Time was due for a re-examination, and its remake is a wonderful recreation of the 1998 experience with improvements on basically every element: visuals, storytelling, timeless design, and more. It is, however, a remake, and the coloring (though expertly done) is firmly inside the lines.Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake – Video Review: Ocarina of Time is gorgeous. That the game is less than 25 GB is an engineering marvel. I never quite came around to the odd looks of child Link or Zelda, but every other character, environment, enemy, item, and boss looks incredible. I eagerly looked forward to meeting every weirdo in Hyrule to see their new look, and was never disappointed. The ReDeads and Stalchildren are more traumatic than ever, and every NPC childhood crush I thought I had moved on from was reignited. Watching the sun rise and set over Hyrule feels as magical as it did in the past.The same accolades can be awarded to the soundtrack. The new orchestrations are incredible, and watching Link’s 4K fingers play the proper notes on his ocarina is mesmerizing. Nintendo continues to fumble voice acting with stiff performances, but thankfully the vast majority of dialogue – one of the primary changes in this remake – is not spoken. Characters don’t necessarily have more to say, but what they say is more impactful, and there are a handful of small but exciting expansions to character and story details. Nothing is groundbreaking or changes how you look at familiar elements, but it manages to be simultaneously nostalgic and improved. And that’s the closest I got to what I would debatably call a problem: It all looks new, but doesn’t feel particularly new. Ocarina’s overall design holds up. The time travel element is surprising and creates great opportunities to see the world change and evolve; puzzles and their solutions are rewarding; the basics of combat, though simple, are thrilling. All those elements have been adjusted, but not overhauled. Dungeons and their puzzles have been tweaked to remove annoyances (such as fewer keys in the Water Temple), and the new swordplay effects make connecting your blade to monsters feel incredible. But they’re all ultimately small changes that don’t cast Ocarina in a new light or consider its history. It’s a straightforward recreation. But an admittedly very good one.The biggest, most impactful changes are the new full camera control and active jumping. Swinging the camera around to take a closer look at the upgraded environments is genuinely stirring, especially in the locations that used to be pre-rendered. There is an undefined and exclusive-to-video games emotion I experienced often while trekking locations I thought I knew so well, only to realize I have never seen or looked at it in this way, and I chased and captured that feeling all over Hyrule. Jumping may seem small, but it made me a more active participant in exploration. It also has the benefit of making some encounters feel somewhat trivial (turns out being able to jump over Blade Traps really diminishes their threat), and I was just fine with that. The beautiful 2026 version of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time does not study the legacy or impact of the landmark game on the video game medium. Instead, it is a meticulous and incredibly impressive recreation of one of the best games ever made. I was rarely surprised during my playthrough, but I was incredibly excited to see old friends and I happily share without embarrassment that playing through its final hour made me cry. For many like me, the original Ocarina of Time is an impactful adventure that arrived at a time in their lives when magic still felt real. Playing the game again 28 years later and appreciating the care taken to maintain and improve that original vision was the ultimate surprise of the whole experience: I didn’t expect to be so moved. Score: 9.25 About Game Informer's review system
