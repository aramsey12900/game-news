Here’s How Wanted Levels And Crime Work In GTA 6NEW
Grand Theft Auto 6 is overhauling the Wanted and police response systems in an interesting way. If you've played Grand Theft Auto games before, you'll know that if you do something illegal, the police...
Grand Theft Auto 6 is overhauling the Wanted and police response systems in an interesting way. If you've played Grand Theft Auto games before, you'll know that if you do something illegal, the police will show up. You'll get a wanted level represented by stars--the more stars you have, the more aggressive the police, or even the military, will be. In order to get out of the situation, you evade the police. You'll see them on the map, so it's easy to lay low for a minute and get them off your back. That is not the case in GTA 6. In the extended gameplay trailer, we did get a look at how some of this works, but more details have surfaced through some exclusive coverage. The first thing we noticed was the return of the six-star wanted level. This was missing in GTA 5, but in older games if you got six stars it would call in the military. Looking at the return of the six stars, it could happen that the military could also get involved. GTA 6 sees the return of six-star wanted levels. In GTA 6, instead of the police searching your specific area for a short time, they'll cast a much wider net. That means that wherever you go in Leonida, the police will be on alert and looking for you. There's far more than just the star system in GTA 6. Some of the icons underneath the stars tell a story, too. These symbols tell you exactly what the police are looking for when you commit a crime. An icon of a CCTV camera means you've been spotted on one, and you'll have to destroy the evidence to get rid of that. A symbol with two characters means the police have seen Jason and Lucia together, meaning you should split up to avoid being recognized. The hanger symbol means the police recognize your clothing so you should change. It seems like you can store clothes in your car, so having outfits at hand will be useful as long as nobody sees you changing. The face icon means they recognize your facial features, so you probably should have been wearing a mask. To get rid of this icon, wear a mask, or if you're Jason, you might be able to get away with changing your facial hair. The weapon icon means they've seen you carrying a specific weapon, and you can either ditch it or switch to something else. The vehicle icon means they recognize your car, and you'll need to switch to another one. Different icons represent ways the police will recognize you. There are likely other ways that the police can track you, but that's all we saw in the extended gameplay. The wanted stars will change color and pattern. If police have a direct line of sight on you, the stars will be solid white. If they start flashing, you've broken the line of sight and solid red means you're still near the scene of the crime and the police are looking for you, but they don't know where you are. The hollow stars mean the police have no idea who they're looking for--in the extended look, Lucia flips off the police while driving past with hollow stars as her wanted level, and they do nothing about it. In previous GTA games, the reaction of the police was quite binary. Either you messed up and they were coming for you, or you hadn't done anything wrong, and they didn't care. In GTA 6, it seems like this system will be more fleshed out. Police will have a wider range of approaches and reactions depending on what you've done and what they know. To make things more interesting, you don't see where the police are on the mini-map. There's no more planning routes to avoid their vision cones, unfortunately. It's a smart idea in terms of gameplay, though, as you'll be less focused on the mini-map and more focused on what's unfolding in front of you. Masks and sunglasses could be essential disguises. However, turning on Focus Mode will allow you to see through walls and spot where the police are. If you're worried about turning a corner and bumping into the cops, use Focus Mode to check for them. NPCs also seem more dynamic. If you're walking around with a weapon and aiming it at people, they'll behave more realistically by running away or calling the cops. Florida has open carry laws, so it's realistic to walk around with a weapon on your back as long as you're not aiming it at people--so NPCs won't report you for it. Cops won't start chasing you unless your crime is reported. If an NPC sees it, they can report you. If you trigger an alarm, the cops will be notified, too. There are far more ways to get caught being a criminal in GTA 6. Destroying your vehicle might be one way to evade the police. All of these changes seem to be implemented to keep you engaged during a police chase. As you're committing crimes, you'll need to consider your getaway vehicle, your outfit, your weapons, potential CCTV and witnesses. That should factor into how you want to commit the crime--you might decide it makes more sense to commit a robbery at night because there are less crowds. Another thing to consider is that if you're caught on CCTV, you might need to spend more time inside the building to find where the files are kept and wipe them so the police don't have video evidence of your crime. Generally, all of these decisions force you to follow the rules in GTA 6, which is strange for a game about breaking the law. However, it just means you need to be more conscious about how you're breaking the law. In GTA 5 you'd grab any weapon and sprint down the road, fire your weapon, and act recklessly. In GTA 6, you'll have to masquerade as a normal citizen until you can commit the crime that you want to commit. It'll be interesting to see how these systems develop the more time you spend in Leonida. Hopefully, all of these interactions will feel much more dynamic than previous entries in the series. The wanted system isn't the only thing getting a complete overhaul--GTA 6 also changes how you steal cars, while fishing and hunting doesn't work like you think it does.

How Exodus Makes Its Choices MatterNEW
The moment one mentions a narrative-driven game, one question springs to the mind of any interested player: Do my choices matter? Video games of all genres are filled with illusory choices me...
The moment one mentions a narrative-driven game, one question springs to the mind of any interested player: Do my choices matter? Video games of all genres are filled with illusory choices meant to make the player feel like they're impacting the world, even when major moments and mechanics are scripted, and that's often not a big deal. But in a game like Exodus, a choice-driven RPG that aims to follow in Mass Effect's footsteps, impactful choices can make or break someone's experience.  I played a few hours of Exodus at developer Archetype Entertainment's Austin offices and came away with a good sense of how the studio intends to give players agency. The systems are mostly what you'd expect from a game like this, but there's one major wrinkle: time dilation. It's a real scientific principle, and the backbone of Exodus' worldbuilding. Essentially, the closer you travel to the speed of light, the slower you experience time. As Jun Aslan, Exodus' protagonist, you'll embark on multiple lightspeed journeys (fittingly called an Exodus) that last months, years, or decades and only experience a few hours, days, or maybe weeks. However, the people back home on the world of Lidon experience time normally, so when you return, a lot will have changed. Your decisions in Exodus can have major ramifications for civilization down the line, and you must live with the consequences of your actions. It's a lens that reframes every decision you make.For example, at the smallest level, you can impact your companions' opinions of you through dialogue. This is pretty standard for RPGs, with pop-ups in the corner of the screen saying "Salt approves" or "Elise disapproves" based on what you choose to say in conversation with them, or with other NPCs while your companions are nearby. You can also choose which companions to bring with you on each mission, giving you more opportunity to raise or lower their opinion of Jun, the protagonist. However, time dilation can impact your relationships in major ways. According to narrative director Drew Karpyshyn, "Not every companion will always be with you at all times [...] And considering what happens when you're gone for years and how these relationships may be maintained is a huge part of the game. It doesn't happen on every mission, but the missions where it does happen – these key Exodus missions that are major points in the story – you have to consider all this."I'm not sure what specific scenarios he might be referring to here, but it's not hard to speculate that there may be decisions where you have to choose which companions to bring with you and which to leave behind. In fact, the damage caused by leaving someone behind like that is the whole pitch of the movie Interstellar, one of Exodus' major influences. It's also possible that you don't get to choose who accompanies you on a journey, and if that's the case, you'll be leaving companions on a planet on the path to a future you've already shaped.To clarify, many major decisions in Exodus involve Remnants, highly advanced pieces of Celestial technology you'll encounter on your adventure. In my playtime, I recovered one called the Logos, which is essentially a highly advanced encyclopedia. Lidon, your home, is a planet built on Celestial technology, and Jun has the choice to either lean into the information in the Logos to build on the planet's foundation or use the Logos to reverse-engineer new tech, making society independent but less advanced. This is what Archetype refers to as a breakpoint decision. You have to pick one of two significant options, and since Jun has some minor precognitive abilities, you can see abstract glimpses of the future before deciding on one or the other. The option to reject the Celestial tech has an image of some dome-like ships, while the option to embrace it shows a preview of some kind of giant snake. Ostensibly, after picking one, I'll see the impact of that choice after an Exodus journey. And if I'm leaving a companion behind, they'll have to live in whichever timeline I've committed to.Jun's precognitive abilities are best seen in the Dynasty tab in the menu, which has a timeline depicting past events and Lidon's ultimate fate. In the early hours we played, Lidon is doomed to be consumed by the Rot, which makes sense – it's the pressure that sets the game in motion. But the screen also shows points earned in three categories, each depicting elements of Lidon's society: green for culture, blue for science, and red for military. Players will face different story outcomes based on each of these levels, and the previews for breakpoint decisions give you a heads-up on which points you'll earn for each choice. All of this is to say that Archetype has injected each breakpoint decision with a lot of variables, and I'm excited about the weight that potentially provides. You're considering the immediate consequences, the long-term story consequences, how it'll impact your Dynasty points, and how it might affect your relationships. It's up to the player to decide which of these factors matter most in the moment, and whether the blowback in other categories might be worth it.However, while my hands-on time gave me a full picture of the factors involved in making decisions, I never saw the long-term results of one. I understand exactly why – Archetype doesn't want to spoil its game's biggest moments months before release – but I can't fully endorse the story's malleability without seeing it for myself. Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: 2K Games Developer: Visual Concepts Release: September 3, 2026 Rating: Everyone The NBA 2K series is perpetually the most frustrating sports franchise for me. On one hand, NBA 2K delivers the best gameplay of any sports franchise year after year; truly, its ability to replicate the action of its corresponding sport is unmatched even by its strongest contemporaries. Unfortunately, the other side of the coin is that its egregious monetization makes the premium product feel more akin to a free-to-play game. NBA 2K27 continues to deliver (and even refine) the series’ incredible gameplay and unrivaled feature set, but like a star player who can’t shed their bad habits, it too often gets in its own way.As with every entry since the series’ debut on Dreamcast, I couldn’t wait to step onto the court with the latest rosters. Outside of times when my teammates weren’t aware enough to avoid stepping out of bounds, basketball has rarely felt smoother in video game form. Calling plays on the fly, setting screens, and finding the open teammate to drain a clutch three never ceases to excite. And you’ll need to ensure you’re using all the tools at your disposal to find the best shot possible if you hope to succeed in this year’s entry. The new rhythm shooting mechanic, where you must release your shot at the exact right time to sink it, adds a new layer of challenge to nailing jumpshots. I struggled at first, but I eventually got the hang of it. Still, I wish it wasn’t an all-or-nothing model; I would vastly prefer it simply buffing or nerfing your shot percentage, rather than outright making you miss if you don’t hit the green part of the meter. Thankfully, if you’d prefer, you can turn it off in most modes and rely solely on your players’ attributes instead. Scoring is made even more challenging by round defensive boosts, which give insight into where the pressure on the ball handler is and let defenders more aggressively contest shots. The offense earns a bump with a revamped Takeover system that rewards strong play with attribute buffs and special skills, as well as more precise ball handling, but even as my games typically ended with sky-high stat lines, NBA 2K27 feels like the pendulum starting to swing back in favor of the defenders.The incredible gameplay extends to the most impressive suite of modes in the genre. MyNBA continues its run as the best Franchise mode in all of sports, with options to play in the modern NBA or various eras spread across the last four decades, complete with fairly accurate rosters, draft classes, rules, presentation, and more. I loved jumping into the Jordan Era and seeing if I could craft a team able to take down the legendary ‘90s Bulls. Regardless of if you’re playing in the modern era or one of the retro periods, MyNBA benefits from improved free agency, contract negotiations, and trade mechanics, improving the gold-standard long-form mode. Though you can't play in other eras of the women's game, I'm happy to see many of the features of MyNBA implemented into the MyWNBA mode as well.On the player-focused MyCareer front, 2K27 delivers a compelling narrative starring a high school prospect who derails their career in a car accident and must rebuild in the NBA G League. When it’s at its best, the cinematic story interweaves elements of the off-the-court narrative into gameplay, such as the point guard who won’t pass to you or a 3v3 streetball tourney against NBA stars at Rucker Park, but it quickly nosedives once it introduces your Running Mate, a player meant to be the Robin to your Batman; I thought I’d be rid of him once I escaped to the NBA, but the story twists to land him on your NBA squad too.  Soon, most in-game goals revolve around monitoring your Running Mate’s stat lines as well; it quickly felt like I was responsible for two careers, and it made me wish I had taken the other MyCareer options and played in a retro era or simply skipped the narrative altogether. I play these modes to feel like I’m controlling a young player who is part of an actual NBA squad, but when a random fictional player shadows you at all times, it shatters the immersion. Speaking of destroying an otherwise immersive career experience, the moment you step outside of your apartment or arena and into the shared hub of The City to either buy new gear or access the online portion of MyCareer, you’re inundated with ads, players zipping around on hoverboards dressed in ridiculous outfits, and even sponsored questlines like one starring Jake from State Farm. It all points towards NBA 2K’s unhealthy relationship with being unsatisfied with the $70 (or more) you already dropped on the game; it also wants you to spend money on VC or, at the very least, see some ads that will earn it additional revenue. Unfortunately, VC serves as one of the key points of frustration for any multi-mode player.To level up your MyCareer player – even if you’re just in single-player – you must use VC. And just like past games, VC is a shared pool for upgrades and purchases across all game modes. I hate deciding between improving my MyCareer player’s ballhandling skill, buying a new pair of in-game shoes at the Nike store, or spending it in the marketplace in MyTeam.   It’s inevitable that certain modes in modern sports games are built around microtransactions and convoluted currencies, but it’s annoying when they break containment to taint largely single-player modes like MyCareer. This isn’t anything new for the series, but it’s disappointing to see the trend continue year after year. In isolation, MyTeam is a fun mode built around the cards you collect from various NBA eras and the WNBA over the course of play. Whether you play online or offline, MyTeam provides myriad ways to add to your collection on top of the obvious “spend VC to acquire cards” route, but at the end of the day, those who do spend the VC will have the better teams.NBA 2K27 aptly builds upon features in prior entries, even when parts of that foundation are less sturdy. The gameplay remains stellar, and MyNBA stands tall as the reigning champion of Franchise modes. Unfortunately, MyCareer’s stumbles and the pervasive level of microtransactions cause me to caveat an otherwise easy recommendation. Score: 7.75 About Game Informer's review system

NHL 27 Review - Two Minutes For HoldingNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S Publisher: Electronic Arts Developer: EA Vancouver Release: September 11, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ Despite its dedication to authenticity, NHL 27 has an identity crisis. The annual hockey sim from EA Vancouver spent the 2010s cementing itself as a persistent fan favorite, with NHL 11, NHL 14, and NHL 19 intertwining modes and customization options with ambitious intervals of innovation and approachability. This year’s iteration tries to imitate that era with a rising cover star in Macklin Celebrini, a modernized revision of GM Connected, and all-new broadcast packages built with hockey tourism in mind, but the serotonin thins into a sobering one-tee of familiarity.On the ice, NHL 27’s skating is where it should be. Whereas NHL 26 introduced a 2.0 version of 25’s Ice-Q technology that uses NHL Edge data to inform player attributes like top speed, shot power, and positioning to provide a realistic contrast between the league’s best, the latest entry refines it through motion. Passing is more intuitive with dump angles and cycling countering better board pins, poke checks, stick lifts, and defensive awareness. Goaltenders now have excellent reaction times across the board, but their crease awareness remains nonexistent. More aggro netminders challenge breakaways and two-on-one scenarios while elite starters are dialed in on setups and one-timers that would normally leave them beached between the posts. Hitting isn’t as reckless as in years past, and due to smarter AI adjustments, the pacing in online and offline modes is a lot more fluid in the neutral zone, promoting user creativity that extends beyond left-trigger glitches, which have been nerfed. It adds realism and, as a result, makes X-Factor abilities that boost mobility and saucer passes more essential than enforcer metas. New team-specific playbooks add to NHL 27’s renewed focus on cohesive AI behaviors as they use real-world positional data to determine how teams react to tactical strategies and pressure, but the idea falls short in execution. Instead of sharing three different presets, every team uses a unique tactical identity, replicating possession-heavy zone entries, defensive collapses that prioritize physical box-outs to protect the slot, and forechecking speed that’s amped to produce chaos in front of the net. It’s a great addition, but in theory, it’s barely recognizable on the ice as CPU teammates rarely pinch or stay in a specific formation after a faceoff is won. Playbooks also stick to unique team strategies that can’t be edited in online competitive modes, which goes against the philosophy of “playing your way.” Being able to control breakout styles and mix and match contextual shifts with rivals offers personalization, but it also takes it away by limiting whether a line cycles or shoots. As a result, defensive-minded teams like the New York Islanders and Detroit Red Wings are boring compared to the feral playstyles of the Carolina Hurricanes and Philadelphia Flyers that integrate pressure to level contenders into submission.Team dynamics are also accentuated by new team atmospheres, with all 32 NHL arenas being reconstructed through distinct lighting systems, real-world jumbotron videos, and licensed goal songs that have replaced the series’ punk-leaning soundtracks. It’s a plus for Franchise lifers and anyone who has taken in Montreal’s Bell Centre or Nashville’s Bridgestone Arena up close, but it is another area that falls short of greatness; Team introductions are identical and fail to show tunnel walks, locker rooms, or Flyers mascot Gritty staring into someone’s soul. ESPN’s John Buccigross and TNT’s Darren Pang replace James Cybulski and Cheryl Pounder in the booth. While their quips are new, the commentary library is expanded using generative AI. Unfortunately, the results are noticeable, with frequent sequences that use dated vernacular and terrible name pronunciations.
