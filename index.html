It’s Time For Epic Games And Its CEO To Touch GrassNEW
Epic Games is under fire once again this week for its stance on AI, as the company is apparently pivoting to "AI-accelerated development"--and supreme overlord Tim Sweeney has been trying to make it s...
Epic Games is under fire once again this week for its stance on AI, as the company is apparently pivoting to "AI-accelerated development"--and supreme overlord Tim Sweeney has been trying to make it seem as though he and his company are not bothered by the backlash even a little bit. But everything that Sweeney and his spokespeople have said this week makes them look like they're embarrassingly out of touch with the reality of our present moment. Maybe it's time for Sweeney to go outside and get some fresh air and remember what it was like to be a regular person who doesn't have a billion dollars and a powerful corporation that he rules with an iron fist. It kinda seems like he hasn't done that in a while. The fun this week started with a Bloomberg report about troubled production on a Marvel- and Star Wars-themed shooter game: one that would hook into Fortnite's system of cosmetics while being a standalone product. The project is reportedly behind schedule, and Bloomberg says the bosses at Epic are trying to compensate by offloading the so-called grunt work to Unreal Engine's AI tools, with new mandates that all code be written by AI and all art created with "full AI workflows." Since there's never been a game worth knowing about that was made that way, the report was met with plenty of eyerolls and mockery from the gaming community. Frankly, the move looks like desperation. Disney gave Epic $1.5 billion a couple of years ago, and all they have to show for it so far are a bunch of Fortnite skins and some Creative islands that nobody plays--perhaps Disney is getting annoyed about that. But Epic made every effort to appear unbothered by the criticism. Sweeney tweeted through the Bloomberg report, claiming that "the aim isn't to do the same stuff with fewer people, but to enable everyone here to build MORE and BETTER stuff, and to bring the benefits to all teams using Unreal."  Then, on Wednesday, the company revealed a new beta version of its Epic Games Launcher. The new version of the launcher is snappier to use and Epic claims it launches more quickly than Steam does--a small benefit very few people would even notice. But otherwise it has no new significant features, looks like it was created from a free design template, is very buggy, and Epic says you shouldn't use it with Fortnite or Rocket League for reasons it didn't bother to explain at all, which renders the beta useless for nearly everyone who might care about it. But it was apparently coded by AI, and Epic thinks that's really cool.  "Programming is hard. So, instead of doing that, we vibe coded a new Epic Games Launcher that’s way faster and more responsive than the current one, and has a new look," Epic's sarcastic announcement about the launcher read. In other words, it's the same launcher you've hated for years, but it works a little faster now. But it actually wasn't faster than Steam, as Valve's newest Steam client update, which began rolling out a day before Epic revealed its new launcher, improved Steam's startup time--on my rig, both launchers now start up in about six seconds. Fortunately, no normal user will ever care about this competition either way. This whole topic is silly, as we know. AI does not improve productivity because workers have to spend more time reviewing the code that the AI spits out. AI code is harder than human code to maintain over time because it's esoteric, and those workers who spend all their time prompting AI tend to lose some of their actual coding skill, which means they gradually become worse at reviewing the AI-generated code. Software products that are vibe-coded are more vulnerable to security breaches than those made by people. And there has never been a noteworthy game produced this way on any scale, so on top of everything else, the new approach to the Disney project is also unprecedented.  Workers hate working with AI this way, so it's unsurprising that the Bloomberg report mentions Epic employees were upset about it. And, lastly, the general public sentiment about AI and the data centers that power it likewise doesn't reflect well on this pivot. It's almost as if Epic were going out of its way to avoid reading the room. Before he became an out-of-touch billionaire ruler of his private empire, Tim Sweeney used to be an excellent game designer. I suspect that, back in the day, he would have understood the value of actual human expertise over the approximation of expertise that AI offers. But as the king of Epic, where he is the sole majority stakeholder and can do basically whatever he wants, he's come to appear like just another rich boss who believes everything he does is above criticism, and who doesn't care about his workers or the work they're doing beyond whether it improves his legacy and gets him more money and power.  This entire pivot to AI is actually itself a perfect example of this, because none of this is for the players anyway. Sweeney is pushing these AI tools in the Unreal Engine as a sales pitch to developers and TV and movie studios to try to get them using his product. It's a buzzword feature to toss on the back of the box in order to impress managers who don't know better. It doesn't matter to him, apparently, whether he pisses off all us regular folks while making his sales pitch--he seems openly contemptuous of everyone who holds any anti-AI sentiment. "Our job, as the provider of tools and technology to much of the industry, is to design for the present that is and the future that will soon be," Sweeney tweeted in response to a joke about his inability to read the room. In other words: Tim Sweeney gets to decide what the future is, and it doesn't matter to him if you dislike it. It's an interesting tack to take during Fortnite's worst year ever, but it's the one Sweeney is going with. Maybe Epic would be better served if Sweeney and his pals took a different approach, though. We just need him to go outside, touch some grass, and reconnect with humanity for a minute. Doing that may not get Epic's Disney shooter finished any faster, but it might help him remember why he used to like making games.

Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: XSEED Games, Marvelous Games Developer: Marvelous Inc. Release: March 4, 2027 Eternal Anima is the time-twisting RPG revealed during the big September Nintendo Direct that looked like a Square Enix HD-2D game but isn’t. It is also the game with the “Never Strive For A Better Tomorrow” splash screen. After playing the game for roughly an hour within developer Marvelous Inc.’s Tokyo office, I eschew that line – I will, in fact, strive for a better tomorrow, if only so I can play more Eternal Anima one day.  The world has essentially come to its final act in Eternal Anima. Urvis Ael is the last remaining bastion of civilization in a flooded world. That said, living in Urvis Ael isn’t all that bad; just don’t strive for a better tomorrow or really any kind of change. Protagonist Wade breaks those rules and sets out on an adventure, feeling deep down that a better life is possible. Wade is bestowed with the power of Psychometry, which lets him look into the past, view memories, and inherit abilities and classes for use in combat. My hands-on time with Eternal Anima is mostly focused on combat, and for good reason: it rules. It is turn-based combat, but with a time-turning twist. Wade also has the power of Future Sight, which means he already knows exactly what every enemy will do each turn, reflected in the UI. Every ability has an action speed shown on the timeline at the bottom of the screen. You can always see an enemy’s upcoming action, who they will target, and even the outcome.  Combat in Eternal Anima is less about hitting enemies harder and more about hitting them smarter. When you attack an enemy's weaknesses with Wade or other party members, you knock them back on the battle timeline, pushing their predecided actions back in the move queue. Your goal is to push them back enough that you kill them before they can attack, or, for bigger enemies, knock them off the timeline in what’s known as a Break. This cancels their action, opening up your party for another round of attacks. The battle system doesn’t feel as active as a standard turn-based RPG; instead, it makes you feel more like a god playing with action figures. You decide what happens, then sit back and watch it all unfold. Everything is depicted in a beautiful, bokeh-enhanced art style that’s unique to Eternal Anima, despite the initial HD-2D impression. I love how the world and enemies look, which makes it even more disappointing that the characters are rendered as chibi. It doesn’t gel with the game’s painterly visuals and clashes with its heavier thematic tones.  As I explore Eternal Anima’s miniature settings, which resemble closed-off dioramas placed carefully on a larger world map, I take part in fishing minigames, collect items, defeat enemies, and encounter NPCs from the past, like Fletcher. After I interact with a glowing sword on the ground, an animated flashback plays, detailing Fletcher’s story and ending with a notification that I received a bow from his past. As I continue through this world, some two centuries before my time, I encounter a man from this period who’s trying to save his forest hometown from flooding. Of course, I already know this will happen in my timeline because the entire world is flooded, but Wade suspects that if he can convince this person of the looming threat, he can change the present by altering the past. My demo ends shortly after, but now, a month removed from the preview, I’m still thinking about Eternal Anima, specifically its combat. A lot of turn-based combat makes you feel powerful. But Eternal Anima’s makes you feel like a genius, and that’s smart and novel. I’m left striving for March now.

I've been tracking Nekome: Nazi Hunter here at Game Informer since its reveal earlier this year. I finally went hands-on with it during Gamescom 2026 and relished just how disrespectfully brutal it is to Nazis. You will soon get the chance to experience that brutality yourself because developer ProbablyMonsters is releasing a demo on Steam soon on October 19, and it will be available through October 26. Ahead of that demo launch, ProbablyMonsters also released a new trailer highlighting the game's narrative and villainous Hyena troupe of Nazi leaders as part of today's Galaxies Autumn Showcase. These Hyenas are at the center of protagonist Vano's Nazi hunting, and they are a notorious squad of Nazi agents whose extreme brand of cruelty made them feared across occupied Europe and the world, according to a press release. Players will control Vano as he kills countless Nazis in violent and bloody ways while tracking down each of the Hyenas (who he will presumably also kill violently and bloodily). Check it out in the latest Nekome: Nazi Hunter trailer for yourself below:  Game Informer also spoke to Nekome: Nazi Hunter game director Jeronimo Barrera about the game in the lead-up to this trailer and the incoming Steam Next Fest demo. In the interview, which took place over email, Barrera talks about the game's politics (or lack thereof), the Hyenas, visual inspiration, and more: Game Informer's Wesley LeBlanc: How did the team decide on the Hyenas as the antagonists/villains of the game, rather than say, the obvious choice of Hitler? Barrera: As we developed the narrative, we had already committed to pursuing the grindhouse aesthetic that would drive the presentation. We needed a disgusting set of villains that would be fun to hunt down and murder. Although we are rooted in real-world events, the player fantasy we are after is to hunt down and murder as many Nazis, both big and small, and that needs to deliver over-the-top fun. Can you tell me about the Hyenas? What are their differences from a narrative perspective, and what are their differences from a gameplay perspective? Does each Hyena approach the hunt for Vano in different ways? We introduce the Hyenas almost immediately in the narrative. After that, the player won’t be confused as to why they need to hunt them down, not the other way around! Vano hunts them down, and each Hyena boss fight is a unique challenge that pushes the players' abilities.  It sounds like Vano will be hunted across occupied Europe and beyond? What can you tell me about that beyond?Our research into how we can tell a new story and give a new experience has given us a lot of surprising locations to explore. For example, we were shocked when we realized that we could have our first encounters with Nazis on U.S. soil! Vano’s adopted home is New York City. It’s hard to believe, but there were Nazi Party rallies in New York. This is where Vano starts his hunt through a tragic coincidence, which then takes Vano to Europe. But he’s not being hunted; he is the hunter.Is there anything larger than life or supernatural or demonic happening narratively, or is it Vano versus some Nazis that are led by the Hyenas? I ask because scenes in this new trailer almost imply some monstrous transformation, or maybe even something vampiric, as blood drips from the Hyenas' faces (though I recognize that could just be a style choice).The Nazis were hideous monsters. We play on those themes for our abilities and how we present them to the player. There are themes we explore that could be considered metaphysical. But with that said, it’s up for people to interpret the narrative on their own. How would you describe the art style/visual design of the game? Our biggest inspiration came from the grindhouse film genre. Turning the stylized pulpiness and violence of movies like Inglourious Basterds into a video game was a challenge that the team was excited to tackle, and we believe this grindhouse spirit gives our game a distinct visual style.Graphic novels were another major influence. Our goal with the art style was to create something over the top and entertaining, while respecting the serious subject matter. The game would lose its sense of fun if it looked too real, and graphic novels helped us find a look that is visually interesting yet able to handle the story’s darkness and violence. How long has the team been working on the game? The team began working on the game in 2024. The genesis of the game was a meeting inside ProbablyMonsters where we started quoting Inglourious Basterds, and we all became set on the idea of making a game about hunting down and killing Nazis through an untold Second World War story.How big is the team? Nekome: Nazi Hunter’s development is led internally at ProbablyMonsters by Brett Bourbin, Brand Inman, Sergei Kuprejanov, Kay Willis and me, with assistance from co-development partners. What has it been like working on a smaller title like this and what does your AAA experience bring to the project? Going from concept to the player’s hands in 18 months is an epic lift that’s only possible with amazing devs that bring extraordinary experience and expertise. The AAA sensibilities run deep at ProbablyMonsters; there are some legends in our house. What makes us different is that our independent approach allows us to tell unique stories and make experiences like Nekome that would have a hard time getting to market. After Gamescom, ButWhyTho? published a story about how Willis said the game isn’t political, and it caused a lot of discussion/backlash about the game. I wanted to see if the team had any follow-up on the idea that Nekome isn’t political now that some time has passed. At least from my perspective, all art is political; not to mention, making a game where you’re very much visibly and graphically killing and mutilating Nazis is sending a very clear message, especially in 2026 when we have people proudly stating they are a Nazi today. But that’s just my line of thinking – I’m curious where the team lands on this discussion now that some additional time has passed.  I think we're overcomplicating this. Nazis are bad. That shouldn't be a political statement, just common sense. Nekome is a revenge story about people who suffered horrific atrocities at the hands of Nazis. We're not making a statement about modern politics. We're telling a story with villains who are unquestionably evil. If someone feels politically attacked by a game where you kill Nazis, maybe they should take a long, hard look in the mirror. Anything I missed you’d like to touch on? We created Nekome because we saw an opportunity to tell a Second World War story through a perspective that games have rarely depicted. World War II is one of the most common settings in media. And yet, there are almost no games that explore the persecution of the Roma and Sinti during that period.That is a real, deeply horrible part of history, so our story also comes with responsibility. The team has worked with historians and cultural consultants to help us approach Vano’s identity, his community and the historical context thoughtfully and credibly. We promise to tell Vano’s story honestly and approach that history with care.
