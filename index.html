I think Star Wars Battlefront 3 might be cursed. For the past 20 years, several different game studios have attempted to create a third entry in Star Wars’ premier shooter sub-series, yet have failed over and over again. We learned last week that EA is reportedly developing Battlefront 3, but also that the project came at the cost of shutting down BioWare. Battlefront 3 has always seemed like a no-brainer to make; 2005’s Star Wars: Battlefront 2 is widely beloved, and more personally, my favorite game of all time. Yet, the video game industry has never been able to actually launch a third installment in the popular series despite multiple attempts. After Pandemic Studios released Battlefront 2 in 2005, it would’ve made sense for the studio to roll right into making a third game, but that didn’t happen. In a 2025 interview with VideoGamer, game designer Dan Nanni revealed that Pandemic started work on a follow-up, but negotiations fell through as the console generation shifted and Pandemic couldn’t meet LucasArts' one-year deadline, reflecting the additional challenges of developing a big-budget licensed game. This wouldn’t be the last time a set-in-stone deadline would get a Battlefront game canceled.  In lieu of making a new game with Pandemic Studios, LucasArts pursued a partnership with TimeSplitters developer Free Radical Design. This relationship led to what has become the best-known (and most infamous) prototype of Star Wars Battlefront 3. Leaked footage shows how this take on Battlefront 3 would’ve been a very ambitious, large-scale shooter, wherein players could fight on a planet’s surface, then get in a ship, take off, and fight in space. Plenty of footage from this take on Battlefront 3 has leaked over the years, so we have a somewhat good idea of what could’ve been. Some developers have even claimed the game was between 75% and 95% finished and that LucasArts was at one point interested in greenlighting a sequel, but Free Radical’s Battlefront 3 was never released. According to reporting from GameSpot, as well as GamesIndustry.biz and Eurogamer, corporate politics kept this version of Battlefront 3 from ever being able to cross the finish line. LucasArts president Jim Ward, who had established and maintained the relationship with Free Radical, left the company. Subsequently, when Free Radical started to miss deadlines and milestones, it no longer had a LucasArts executive in its corner to keep the relationship healthy. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=izsSndr3IOs Rather than work things out with Free Radical, LucasArts’ new management ultimately chose to cancel Free Radical’s Battlefront 3 when it became clear it likely wouldn’t have hit a Fall 2008 release window. A combination of missed deadlines and corporate politics did this version of Battlefront 3 in. Some assets from the canceled game made their way into Rebellion Developments’ Star Wars Battlefront: Elite Squadron for the PlayStation Portable, but the original Battlefront series would conclude after that. Several years later, EA and DICE would reboot Battlefront, essentially creating Star Wars shooters built on the foundation of their Battlefield games. 2015’s Star Wars Battlefront and its 2017 sequel garnered their fair share of controversy due to a lack of single-player content and the inclusion of loot boxes, respectively. Still, those Battlefront games found their audience and continue to attract players to this day. While EA publishing Battlefront 3 seems like it should have been a natural next move, the path to get to that point was not as straightforward as you think. In 2020, Kotaku reported that a Battlefront spin-off with open-world elements codenamed Project Viking was canceled. EA wanted its Vancouver and Criterion Studios to quickly make Project Viking to hit a Fall 2020 deadline, but by early 2019 the project was canceled when it became clear the studios could not handle such a tight turnaround.  It’s unclear if this game would’ve been titled Battlefront 3 outright. Still, this is another example of publishers prioritizing hitting deadlines over realizing the potential of a new Battlefront game. That again may stem from the constraints and realities of developing a game with a license, which can come with requirements on release timing and financial considerations that make profitability—let alone explosive growth that mega publishers like EA expect — all the more challenging. As of now, nothing has filled the Star Wars multiplayer shooter void since 2017.  However, recent reports indicate that EA and DICE are finally planning to make Battlefront 3 following EA's controversial sale to Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund. This came at a cost, as Bloomberg reports BioWare was shut down--and Mass Effect Universe canceled--in favor of this game. Even if this finally is the version of Battlefront 3 that launches, the game will always have the blood of BioWare--and hundreds of laid off developers--on its hands through no fault of its developers.  When I eventually play Battlefront 3, there will be a part of me always thinking about the games sacrificed to make it a reality. Greenlighting it was apparently an either-or choice for EA leadership. That’s a cursed mindset to be in when thinking about Battlefront 3, as there’s a universe where I could’ve been playing a game bearing this title as early as 2006 and Mass Effect Universe would still be in development. The real game development curse that all of these canceled or troubled versions of Battlefront 3 represent is this: Even games that seem like they should be sure things can succumb to the demanding corporate whims, politics, and deadlines of the video game industry.

An AI voice in your head wants your help escaping a simulation where thousands of runners attempt to reach the end of multi-arena stages to defeat a boss. It’s all standard stuff for the rogue genre, but if anything, its simplicity pushes you back to the meat and potatoes of this shooter. Void/Breaker’s shorter runtime – I rolled credits at 12 hours – ensures nothing gets too repetitive in its roguelite structure, but an expansive endgame promises even more to engage with, like different run modifications you can enable to make an already chaotic shooter even more anarchic. I’ll certainly be running it back (again and again) to see what other disruptions I can bring to this AI-simulated adventure. It’s hard not to when destruction feels this good. Was that part of the thinking behind why the game was built that way, or was the more linear path there for other reasons?Ultimately, we made a game suited for a Wolverine story that had comic book pacing – action and meaningful story development on every page.We experimented with different structures and stories, evaluating from the perspective of: “Is this the best way to do Wolverine justice?” As the various things we tried didn’t feel right, we had to challenge our assumptions and look to our favorite Wolverine stories for inspiration, which led us to where we ended up – a story with momentum in which every beat felt authentic to the character.Scenes and gameplay sequences that might have been fine in other games but didn’t strongly evoke Wolverine undermined the consistency of the fantasy we were trying to create, so we tried to keep things very focused.After the game’s release, there was an active online conversation about some of the breadcrumb-trail guidance the game offers to lead you to pickups, or to mark the continued golden path of progression. I think it’s great that on top of that, players now have more flexibility in how they experience them.As a longtime comics fan, I found Logan’s combat system especially exciting, particularly for the relentless forward momentum it provided. What do you think were some of the keys to nailing that dynamic, as distinct from other high-quality third-person action games?There were a few key elements we wanted to get right - Logan’s resilience, rage, claw feel, and overall lethality.For resilience, Logan’s healing factor needed to feel important for survival - a prominent element of the system. A natural starting point there was to try continual regeneration so you could outlast enemies, but that quickly fell apart as it incentivized the player to slow the pace of combat or even retreat for maximum advantage. Even though it was incredibly effective at enabling survival, it was also hard to notice by the player so it didn’t feel prominent.The key to solving this problem was making a connection between rage and healing factor. The Last Stand mechanic is a healing surge that can happen when you lose all your health, themed as using adrenaline to keep Logan’s heart beating. This incentivized aggression and made a space for an up-close look at visceral healing. Speaking of rage, it’s the backbone of the combat system where being an indicator of momentum is key. At the low end of rage, you don’t have the safety net of Last Stand, so your decision-making naturally biases towards survival; ensuring you prioritize threat assessment and defense. As you climb into the middle tier, you have the leeway to push your luck for longer combos or barrages that let you build up rage and get kills faster. Finally, you get to rage tier 3, where the quick decay of rage makes your rate of kills the top priority, so you might prefer to attack groups over ranged stragglers just for the improved kill density. The presentation and systemic incentives drive the player into a frenzy, just like Wolverine.We invested a lot in the details of claw feel to make sure it was distinct from general game attacks. Claws shred, so we added locational body damage to our enemies to reflect that, along with a dynamic blood system where it globs together as it is spilled, splatters on contact with characters or the environment, drips down the walls, diffuses in the water, and other details. Many kills throughout combat are punctuated with custom animations with cinematic camera work that pushes in and frames the puncturing of claws into the body for a strong impression of the connection and weight of the puncture. Severing limbs on these attacks is an occasional reminder of how sharp Logan’s adamantium claws are. Reinforcing this is top-notch sound and haptics design, so each hit registers.The last aspect I’ll touch on is lethality, the rate at which Wolverine is able to dispatch enemies and vice versa. We found that fights where enemies were more technical and could survive a lot undermined what players expected to experience as Wolverine, which was aggressively and lethally tearing through a lot of enemies. On the other hand, combat was less exciting if the enemies didn’t feel like a serious and deadly threat, and you didn’t feel like Wolverine if a little bit of caution could enable you to defend perfectly and avoid all damage.These aspects led us towards encounters where you confronted large groups of enemies that were highly aggressive, to the point of feeling unfair. We wanted a story where Logan is pulled into conflict not because he’s a crusader, but because there’s no other option. There are no X-Men to help. His growth as a person should be a challenging journey, which could go unearned if he was surrounded by wise, supportive, heroic characters pushing him in the right direction. In contrast, his dynamic with the members of Team X evokes his struggle. The adversity to personal growth is what makes it interesting.Jean does end up being the person that pushes him in the right direction, and that works because they can relate to each other, having had similar struggles. In several versions of Marvel storytelling, Jean and Logan share a special bond, but Jean ultimately chooses Scott Summers as a partner. That goes for his emotional pain as much as physical. He’s a tragic character in this regard – all he wants is to be at peace but can never find it for long.Wolverine gets to take on a lot of the “heavy hitters” of his comic mythology in different boss fights throughout the game. His inner turmoil and chaotic behavior helps illustrate Logan’s own inner beast and his struggle to resist it. Sabretooth’s strength and mutant powers also provide a mirror to Logan in combat, and his savagery creates shocking moments that leave an impression.
