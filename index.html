It's for that last reason an upcoming extraction "RPG" called Hunger caught my attention. Developer Good Fun Corporation--founded by Max Rea, the former CEO of original Hell Let Loose developer Black Matter--has pledged Hunger won't have forced gear or character wipes. It will feature permanent, long-term progression systems once it launches in early access, meaning any progress I make will be mine. Forever. As someone who loves MMORPGs and lasting character progression, that's a tempting proposition. After a Steam server-slam playtest with Hunger, during which I played for eight hours straight (despite knowing the playtest would soon end and all my progress would disappear), I think I figured out where the game gets its name. I forgot to eat, instead opting for one just one more level, one more expedition, one more quest, until suddenly it was midnight. I only knew Hunger. https://youtu.be/6kGmgDnT6h8?si=2tfsvvoFfAzR76xx I can confidently say Hunger's progression systems are a feast for RPG-minded players like me, combining the best elements of MMORPGs with the intense fights with other players extraction shooters are known for. There are six hero-like characters to choose from in Hunger, at least in the playtest. Each brings unique abilities to the table, and level up as in any RPG by earning XP from completing quests, killing monsters out in its post-apocalyptic Napoleonic Wars setting, simply staying alive, and extracting from expeditions.  By level 9, you'll have unlocked all three of that character's unique abilities. At level 10, all the way up to 100, you'll start to earn Mastery points, allowing you to invest in different skill trees that improve your character. These passive upgrades might be as simple as faster revive speeds, finding more ammunition in containers, or the ability to reload a firearm while sprinting. They won't grant you more raw power in the form of dealing more damage or having more health, however. Depending on where you spend your Mastery points, you can build characters in numerous ways, opting to focus on different playstyles like melee, ranged, crafting, survival, or some combination of them.  What these Mastery skills do isn't all that important. Instead, knowing that once I've leveled up and unlocked them they would be mine to keep--that I had permanently gained some small amount of progress for that specific character--made each point gained feel immensely impactful. Even in a temporary playtest, it made me want to keep playing and investing in my character, eager to complete my build and see that character reach their full potential.  The same can be said for professions, another RPG system that makes Hunger stand out from the extraction-shooter crowd. Like in many MMOs, players can choose two professions to learn for a character, slowly leveling them up with resources found on expeditions and unlocking abilities that allow you to craft consumables, armor, or weapons. Only being able to pick two professions means you won't be able to do everything. That makes your choice feel like it truly matters, and makes it even more satisfying as you level them up and reap the benefits they can bring.  Building up my coffers with money, consumables, armor, and weapons felt rewarding in a way it often doesn't in other extraction games where gear wipes happen frequently. Knowing that all the currency and weapons I accumulated were mine to keep made it all more meaningful, as it meant I would have it should I hit a rough patch where I fail to extract multiple times in a row or take a break from the game. If I played my cards right, I would have a nice safety net to fall back on, making every victory all the more lasting since it would be mine to keep, not just for a few months, but potentially years. In many ways, this is just skimming the surface of Hunger's systems. Suffice to say, there is plenty here to sink your teeth into, particularly for those who don't mind slow-but-steady progress. Combined with its early 19th century sword-and-musket fantasy setting, a social hub filled with intriguing characters and questgivers, and the high skill ceiling of both its melee and ranged battles, Hunger had me eager to invest in its world, people, combat, and systems.  Without the fear of losing all my progress to a wipe should life get in the way and I'm not able to play for a few months, I can see myself sinking dozens or even hundreds of hours into Hunger. How Good Fun plans to handle characters becoming potentially too wealthy or powerful in a game with no wipes is still uncertain, but clearly a ton of thought and care has gone into making Hunger something worth investing time in. After the playtest, all I know for sure is that when Hunger launches in Steam Early Access (supposedly before the end of the year), I will be eagerly seated for what Good Fun is serving.

But it is nearly 30 years old, and despite establishing many important staples of 3D gaming, its age is becoming more apparent. Ocarina of Time was due for a re-examination, and its remake is a wonderful recreation of the 1998 experience with improvements on basically every element: visuals, storytelling, timeless design, and more. It is, however, a remake, and the coloring (though expertly done) is firmly inside the lines.Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake – Video Review: Ocarina of Time is gorgeous. That the game is less than 25 GB is an engineering marvel. I never quite came around to the odd looks of child Link or Zelda, but every other character, environment, enemy, item, and boss looks incredible. I eagerly looked forward to meeting every weirdo in Hyrule to see their new look, and was never disappointed. The ReDeads and Stalchildren are more traumatic than ever, and every NPC childhood crush I thought I had moved on from was reignited. Watching the sun rise and set over Hyrule feels as magical as it did in the past.The same accolades can be awarded to the soundtrack. The new orchestrations are incredible, and watching Link’s 4K fingers play the proper notes on his ocarina is mesmerizing. Nintendo continues to fumble voice acting with stiff performances, but thankfully the vast majority of dialogue – one of the primary changes in this remake – is not spoken. Characters don’t necessarily have more to say, but what they say is more impactful, and there are a handful of small but exciting expansions to character and story details. Nothing is groundbreaking or changes how you look at familiar elements, but it manages to be simultaneously nostalgic and improved. And that’s the closest I got to what I would debatably call a problem: It all looks new, but doesn’t feel particularly new. Ocarina’s overall design holds up. The time travel element is surprising and creates great opportunities to see the world change and evolve; puzzles and their solutions are rewarding; the basics of combat, though simple, are thrilling. All those elements have been adjusted, but not overhauled. Dungeons and their puzzles have been tweaked to remove annoyances (such as fewer keys in the Water Temple), and the new swordplay effects make connecting your blade to monsters feel incredible. But they’re all ultimately small changes that don’t cast Ocarina in a new light or consider its history. It’s a straightforward recreation. But an admittedly very good one.The biggest, most impactful changes are the new full camera control and active jumping. Swinging the camera around to take a closer look at the upgraded environments is genuinely stirring, especially in the locations that used to be pre-rendered. There is an undefined and exclusive-to-video games emotion I experienced often while trekking locations I thought I knew so well, only to realize I have never seen or looked at it in this way, and I chased and captured that feeling all over Hyrule. Jumping may seem small, but it made me a more active participant in exploration. It also has the benefit of making some encounters feel somewhat trivial (turns out being able to jump over Blade Traps really diminishes their threat), and I was just fine with that. The beautiful 2026 version of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time does not study the legacy or impact of the landmark game on the video game medium. Instead, it is a meticulous and incredibly impressive recreation of one of the best games ever made. I was rarely surprised during my playthrough, but I was incredibly excited to see old friends and I happily share without embarrassment that playing through its final hour made me cry. For many like me, the original Ocarina of Time is an impactful adventure that arrived at a time in their lives when magic still felt real. Playing the game again 28 years later and appreciating the care taken to maintain and improve that original vision was the ultimate surprise of the whole experience: I didn’t expect to be so moved. Reviewed on: PC Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Playstack Ltd Developer: Stubby Games Release: November 4, 2026 Rating: Teen Many games tout impressive destruction systems under the hood, but few implement it as well as Void/Breaker. The ruin is visually spectacular, sure – who doesn’t love to watch buildings crumble because of your grenade or gravity-thrown explosives? But importantly, it’s a critical aspect of Void/Breaker’s roguelite design. Enemies caught beneath falling rubble become instantly staggered, and that’s your moment to kill. Grenades do this too, as do most items you throw at the hostile AI machinery trying to end your run, creating a vortex of shifting priorities as you dash, jump, and shoot at sequential staggered enemies in your sightlines. Countless weapon mods and permutations make fewer than a dozen weapons feel like hundreds in your arsenal, adding even more blissful chaos to the mix. Though it can be difficult at times, if you can see through the literal remains of your destruction, one of the year’s most engaging shooters awaits.  Void/Breaker wastes no time getting a gun in your hand. An AI voice in your head wants your help escaping a simulation where thousands of runners attempt to reach the end of multi-arena stages to defeat a boss. He asks that you simply follow the simulation’s commands; meanwhile, he uses the data you collect along the way to find new exits for you and him. The storytelling style is very reminiscent of Valve’s Portal. Something much larger is at play here, but it’s worth discovering for yourself.This narrative framework supports the game’s roguelite structure. Mechanically, however, you enter various themed digital stages, from liminal spaces to beaches to snow-capped cliffs and more, and attempt to survive. Most arenas consist of a couple of starkly white structures that your grenades and thrown explosives can topple (ideally onto waves of enemies below). Learning the language of Void/Breaker means constantly scanning your field of view for explosives and structures practically begging to come down, and then eyeing neon-green bars above enemies that tell you they’re staggered and ready for a quick kill. Developer Stubby Games throws plenty of wrenches into this formula, like armored foes and electrostatic enemies you must melee first, and elite bots that will have you on the back foot, but for the most part, Void/Breaker’s formula is “Break stuff, then kill.”  The game emphasizes explosives and bringing buildings down, but your guns do most of the killing; you can only select one weapon per run. You choose from a pistol, shotgun, machine gun, sniper, and more, and each has its perks (though once I unlocked the SMG, I rarely picked anything else). There’s no sense overthinking your weapon, though, because the dozens of mods you pick up each run determine its effectiveness. As you pick up more schematics, the pool of mods you find in chests or drop from enemies expands, and soon your gun fires corrosive acid, electricity, fire, projectile missiles, and more. Not to mention, mods change how the gun fires, how much damage certain bullets do, recoil, accuracy, and just about any other stat you associate with firing a video game gun. Wild combinations are easy to create, and a unique mod-stacking mechanic that can double or triple effectiveness adds a light touch of puzzle design to this otherwise straight shooter.  Stubby Games smartly emphasizes player expression, never getting in the way of the zanier combinations available to players. The SMG I used to beat the final boss featured mods that made each bullet electric, shot missiles with each shot, covered each bullet in a destructive orb, and featured a ricochet perk that made each bullet bounce off walls and split into even more bullets. I’m thankful for mid-run saves because this SMG permutation, paired with an ability mod that let me throw infinite grenades for a limited time, filled my screen with so much destruction that the entire game crashed. I’d usually bemoan such a thing, but I couldn’t help but laugh. I pushed my PC to its limit with my on-screen chaos. Boss fights leave a little to be desired – I rarely died because of their simple bullet-hell arenas. Instead, most runs ended because, after bringing down every structure around me, I’d get so lost in the chaos that I wouldn't realize I was stuck in debris while enemies swarmed my location. As stunning as it is to see so much happening on screen, it can be hard to understand what is going on during the most chaotic moments.  Between enemy-filled arenas en route to a stage’s end boss, you might find a shop to buy more mods, ominous obelisks with risk-reward propositions (gain a lot of health or lose half your health, for example), optional trials, and strange phenomena that transport you to different locations. These moments are quick breaks in between bouts of adrenaline-fueled shooter action that I always welcomed – enemies can be relentless, especially as new types are introduced in each stage. The rest of the Void/Breaker package offers few genre surprises. You can upgrade various traits between runs, research new mod schematics to add to the random pool, purchase new weapons, and discover fun secrets. You can unlock shortcuts, glean in-world information from computer terminals, and hear intriguing dialogue barks from the AI partner who needs your help. It’s all standard stuff for the rogue genre, but if anything, its simplicity pushes you back to the meat and potatoes of this shooter. Void/Breaker’s shorter runtime – I rolled credits at 12 hours – ensures nothing gets too repetitive in its roguelite structure, but an expansive endgame promises even more to engage with, like different run modifications you can enable to make an already chaotic shooter even more anarchic. I’ll certainly be running it back (again and again) to see what other disruptions I can bring to this AI-simulated adventure. It’s hard not to when destruction feels this good.
