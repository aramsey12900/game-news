The Live-Action Street Fighter Movie Curse Has Yet To Be BrokenNEW
Street Fighter has long been more than just a fighting game. It’s impossible to discuss the most popular video game characters of all time without mentioning Chun-Li, Ryu, or Ken. For many of us, it s...
Street Fighter has long been more than just a fighting game. It’s impossible to discuss the most popular video game characters of all time without mentioning Chun-Li, Ryu, or Ken. For many of us, it shaped our '90s childhood at the arcade and served as our introduction to the fighting-game genre. It also has inspired multiple movies, comic books, an anime series and manga, on top of endless collaborations with other fighting game and comic book franchises. Street Fighter's massive popularity is also a curse when it comes to live-action video game adaptations: 1994's Street Fighter and 2009's Street Fighter: The Legend of Chun-Li were poorly received, despite both films having big budgets and being hyped among fans. The franchise failing twice as live-action films was an embarrassment to Capcom, so it's no surprise fans would be concerned about a third attempt at creating a decent live-action film. When Kitao Sakurai was announced as the Street Fighter director, fans were both excited and concerned about him taking reins due to a history of working closely with Eric Andre. There was hope though, especially with the positive reception of a few other video game adaptations in the last few years: Perhaps the experience of Andre’s eccentric style of comedy could mean a more unconventional approach, similar to Zach Cregger’s approach with Resident Evil? Maybe the casting mix of Andre, pro wrestlers, martial artists, and, for some reason, podcaster Andrew Schulz could produce a very chaotic live-action comedy? Unfortunately, the questions around the movie's identity are ultimately what hurt the adaptation the most because, even with  cartoonish action and plenty of attempts at humor, this Street Fighter movie doesn't really figure out what it is.  As with many other adaptations of Street Fighter, Ken is rich and famous, but he is also a shell of the fighter he once was--Ryu's absence has clearly taken its toll. Noah Centineo encapsulates Ken’s flashy personality and inflated ego excellently, while showcasing his more fragile and sensitive side as he reminisces about his past life with Ryu. Meanwhile, Ryu is now living in the shadows as a homeless ramen chef. Andrew Koji as Ryu is also fantastic casting: His serious and noble demeanor, even when it comes to making terrible ramen, feels natural. The first act of Street Fighter is engaging thanks to its focus on Ken and Ryu’s relationship. Their individual personality quirks should have set the tone for the rest of the film, but sadly it starts to have an identity crisis when it transitions to the main plot. Don’t get me wrong--there are still many aspects of the film that capture the essence of the Street Fighter games: vibrant and thrilling fight scenes, cheesy dialogue, and some initially questionable casting choices that finally make sense after seeing the movie, especially Noah Centineo's Ken Masters. Street Fighter also immediately captivates you. Shortly after the Capcom logo hypes up the audience, the film transitions to a beautifully animated sequence that highlights the deep bond between Ryu and Ken. The animated sequences are the best Street Fighter has ever looked on screen, and luckily these scenes become a staple throughout the film to highlight the history between the two young fighters. Once the opening credits are over, the film continues to learn into '90s nostalgia with cool environments and oddly endearing comedic moments, as it depicts Ken living his life in 1993 without Ryu, his childhood rival and sworn brother who has been missing for years. Ken and Ryu’s last moment together was witnessing the death of their master, Gouken, by the deity Akuma, who was awakened by Ryu’s powers in the first World Warrior Tournament. Haunted by the death of their mentor and blaming himself for causing it, Ryu has disappeared from the fighting world. As with other movies based on fighting games, Street Fighter is in a rush to reach its core goal: Get Ryu and all the iconic characters to battle in the World Warrior Tournament. The rush never fully allows the audience to absorb the other characters and environment. This flaw could have been balanced with the proper tone and character focus, but Street Fighter fails to do so, doing the cast a disservice. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U6sbm1OaJb8&t=1s The biggest letdown is the dialogue, as the humor is inconsistent and the movie never fully understands its own tone. Some characters, like Chun-Li and Dan Hibiki, don't fully commit to their personalities, either. Perhaps it’s the juvenile scripting or a casting issue, but either way, it felt like I was watching amateur actors in poor cosplay rather than seeing well-realized versions of these characters coming to life on screen. At first, the cringeworthy interactions got a few laughs out of me and had some very minor endearing moments, but it tries too hard to keep that comedic momentum going and fails miserably. The film forces you to accept bad writing and performances to drive it forward instead of allowing any of the interactions to feel natural.Chun-Li’s character was the most disappointing to me. Her tragic backstory of avenging her father is already canon, which should’ve made it easier to adapt into a decent side plot with the right amount of tweaking, but instead she is incredibly flat and never fully fleshed out. Actor Callina Liang seems bored and uncomfortable throughout the entire film, even during her intense interactions with M. Bison and minor action moments. The writing and odd directional choices cater to the male gaze, making it obvious she was lazily written by a man. As one of the most capable and badass female characters in gaming, Chun-Li and, by extension, Callina Liang deserved better, and so did the fans. The dumbfounding casting and scripting choice for this film was Andrew Schulz as Dan Hibiki. When Street Fighter was first announced, there was an immense amount of backlash for the casting of Andrew Schulz--a controversial podcaster and stand-up comedian. I could go on about the drama surrounding Schulz’s audience, his racism, hot takes, comedic style, etc., but for the sake of our collective sanity, I'll just say  his inability to portray  Dan’s character with any competency is yet another reason to question why he has any involvement in the production. In Street Fighter canon, Dan is often portrayed as cocky and arrogant, while also being a bit inept as a fighter, yet in the movie his characterization is unclear--is he slimy or charismatic? Is he supposed to crack jokes, or just be an awkward friend? It’s also very odd how prevalent Dan is throughout the film, especially since he is without an arc or story to justify his frequent presence. The movie tries to sell you on a bond between Dan and Ken, but it’s a very uncomfortable friendship that never seems realistic or entertaining. The one vulnerable moment between them is immediately ruined by a weirdly homophobic joke--which, sadly, the film has a few of--and it comes off unsettlingly. In general, Dan and his casting are flat and uninspiring. The initial joy of '90s nostalgia also wears off pretty quickly. It was certainly a welcome choice to have '90s hits like Montell Jordan’s “This is How We Do It” and 4 Non Blondes’ "What's Up?” feature on the soundtrack, but the movie goes on to then disregard the era the movie takes place in by blasting “Who Let the Dogs Out?,” a song that came out in 2000. While the film still remains pretty colorful, the lack of commitment to being period accurate undermines the vibe frequently.The only aspect of Street Fighter that maintains a consistent quality is the fight choreography. This film is not afraid to embrace the cartoony vibes and reference unique movesets: hurricane kicks left and right, the iconic Hadoken, Blanka’s Electric Thunders, Zangief’s spinning piledriver, and a few more are sprinkled throughout each fight. Although the action was a little more limited due to the PG-13 rating, I was still impressed with the over-the-top stunts and tonally varied fights--silly spats with gags, a John Wick-style club brawl, and a few unique 1v1 battles that closely resemble what you would see in a Street Fighter game.It’s also disappointing that a movie called Street Fighter fails to use its tournament structure for more individual fights between side characters. I would’ve loved to see Guile bust out some Sonic moves, or M. Bison doing anything other than complaining he needs more psycho power, but the film attempts to make up for it with a mix of impressive martial arts and campy action sequences elsewhere. Despite a PG-13 rating that limits the level of violence, the choreography team does fantastic work nevertheless. I just wish it had more Street Fighter style fights with different characters instead of being overshadowed by the goofy brawls you see in typical martial arts films.Street Fighter is full of goofy action, colorful shots, and gorgeous animated sequences that focus on the bond between Ryu and Ken. It’s entertaining for some silly, mindless fun, but also a very messy movie that never quite figures out its tone and fails to sell you on its plot or characters. This is the best Street Fighter live action we have as far though, but unfortunately that's not an accolade worth bragging about.

I've been tracking Nekome: Nazi Hunter here at Game Informer since its reveal earlier this year. I finally went hands-on with it during Gamescom 2026 and relished just how disrespectfully brutal it is to Nazis. You will soon get the chance to experience that brutality yourself because developer ProbablyMonsters is releasing a demo on Steam soon on October 19, and it will be available through October 26. Ahead of that demo launch, ProbablyMonsters also released a new trailer highlighting the game's narrative and villainous Hyena troupe of Nazi leaders as part of today's Galaxies Autumn Showcase. These Hyenas are at the center of protagonist Vano's Nazi hunting, and they are a notorious squad of Nazi agents whose extreme brand of cruelty made them feared across occupied Europe and the world, according to a press release. Players will control Vano as he kills countless Nazis in violent and bloody ways while tracking down each of the Hyenas (who he will presumably also kill violently and bloodily). Check it out in the latest Nekome: Nazi Hunter trailer for yourself below:  Game Informer also spoke to Nekome: Nazi Hunter game director Jeronimo Barrera about the game in the lead-up to this trailer and the incoming Steam Next Fest demo. In the interview, which took place over email, Barrera talks about the game's politics (or lack thereof), the Hyenas, visual inspiration, and more: Game Informer's Wesley LeBlanc: How did the team decide on the Hyenas as the antagonists/villains of the game, rather than say, the obvious choice of Hitler? Barrera: As we developed the narrative, we had already committed to pursuing the grindhouse aesthetic that would drive the presentation. We needed a disgusting set of villains that would be fun to hunt down and murder. Although we are rooted in real-world events, the player fantasy we are after is to hunt down and murder as many Nazis, both big and small, and that needs to deliver over-the-top fun. Can you tell me about the Hyenas? What are their differences from a narrative perspective, and what are their differences from a gameplay perspective? Does each Hyena approach the hunt for Vano in different ways? We introduce the Hyenas almost immediately in the narrative. After that, the player won’t be confused as to why they need to hunt them down, not the other way around! Vano hunts them down, and each Hyena boss fight is a unique challenge that pushes the players' abilities.  It sounds like Vano will be hunted across occupied Europe and beyond? What can you tell me about that beyond?Our research into how we can tell a new story and give a new experience has given us a lot of surprising locations to explore. For example, we were shocked when we realized that we could have our first encounters with Nazis on U.S. soil! Vano’s adopted home is New York City. It’s hard to believe, but there were Nazi Party rallies in New York. This is where Vano starts his hunt through a tragic coincidence, which then takes Vano to Europe. But he’s not being hunted; he is the hunter.Is there anything larger than life or supernatural or demonic happening narratively, or is it Vano versus some Nazis that are led by the Hyenas? I ask because scenes in this new trailer almost imply some monstrous transformation, or maybe even something vampiric, as blood drips from the Hyenas' faces (though I recognize that could just be a style choice).The Nazis were hideous monsters. We play on those themes for our abilities and how we present them to the player. There are themes we explore that could be considered metaphysical. But with that said, it’s up for people to interpret the narrative on their own. How would you describe the art style/visual design of the game? Our biggest inspiration came from the grindhouse film genre. Turning the stylized pulpiness and violence of movies like Inglourious Basterds into a video game was a challenge that the team was excited to tackle, and we believe this grindhouse spirit gives our game a distinct visual style.Graphic novels were another major influence. Our goal with the art style was to create something over the top and entertaining, while respecting the serious subject matter. The game would lose its sense of fun if it looked too real, and graphic novels helped us find a look that is visually interesting yet able to handle the story’s darkness and violence. How long has the team been working on the game? The team began working on the game in 2024. The genesis of the game was a meeting inside ProbablyMonsters where we started quoting Inglourious Basterds, and we all became set on the idea of making a game about hunting down and killing Nazis through an untold Second World War story.How big is the team? Nekome: Nazi Hunter’s development is led internally at ProbablyMonsters by Brett Bourbin, Brand Inman, Sergei Kuprejanov, Kay Willis and me, with assistance from co-development partners. What has it been like working on a smaller title like this and what does your AAA experience bring to the project? Going from concept to the player’s hands in 18 months is an epic lift that’s only possible with amazing devs that bring extraordinary experience and expertise. The AAA sensibilities run deep at ProbablyMonsters; there are some legends in our house. What makes us different is that our independent approach allows us to tell unique stories and make experiences like Nekome that would have a hard time getting to market. After Gamescom, ButWhyTho? published a story about how Willis said the game isn’t political, and it caused a lot of discussion/backlash about the game. I wanted to see if the team had any follow-up on the idea that Nekome isn’t political now that some time has passed. But that’s just my line of thinking – I’m curious where the team lands on this discussion now that some additional time has passed.  I think we're overcomplicating this. Nazis are bad. That shouldn't be a political statement, just common sense. Nekome is a revenge story about people who suffered horrific atrocities at the hands of Nazis. We're not making a statement about modern politics. We're telling a story with villains who are unquestionably evil. If someone feels politically attacked by a game where you kill Nazis, maybe they should take a long, hard look in the mirror. Anything I missed you’d like to touch on? We created Nekome because we saw an opportunity to tell a Second World War story through a perspective that games have rarely depicted. World War II is one of the most common settings in media. And yet, there are almost no games that explore the persecution of the Roma and Sinti during that period.That is a real, deeply horrible part of history, so our story also comes with responsibility. The team has worked with historians and cultural consultants to help us approach Vano’s identity, his community and the historical context thoughtfully and credibly. We promise to tell Vano’s story honestly and approach that history with care.

The game never materialized, though, and now Dan Houser has shared more details as to why. Speaking to IGN, Houser said Agent never coming out shouldn't be a total shock because he worked on "a bunch of games that never came out." He said Rockstar tried with two or three different teams to make Agent--an open-world spy game--but the teams just "could not get it right." "And it never came out. We concluded that it was not a game we were ever going to figure out how to make. Other people can try, but I personally think it's hard," he said. Great spy movies like the James Bond series work so well because they take viewers all over the world, but "that's not an experience in an open-world game that really works," Houser said. "So we tried lots of things and couldn't get it quite right," Houser said, going on to say that the teams built great elements but they never came together in a full package. Images of Agent, which was reportedly set in the '70s, have emerged over the years, showing off some of what Rockstar was planning. In 2009, then-Take-Two president Ben Feder told GameSpot that Agent was being positioned to be the next Grand Theft Auto. “It’s the A team working on an AAA title,” he said at the time. “I think the subject matter, the characters, the story, the environment, the background–all the elements are there. It’s such a rich environment to play in.” Across the industry, games get canceled regularly--Blizzard has said it cancels 50% of the games it starts--but Rockstar's Agent got more traction because of the studio's profile. Rockstar never gave Agent a release date, but it would have followed up 2008's Grand Theft Auto 4. From Liberty City To Leonida: Following The Evolution Of Rockstar’s GTA Launch TrailersNEW
Rockstar has finally given us the launch trailer for GTA 6 ahead of its November 19 release date, and it looks spectacular. This shouldn’t be a surprise, considering the developer has been honing its ...
Rockstar has finally given us the launch trailer for GTA 6 ahead of its November 19 release date, and it looks spectacular. This shouldn’t be a surprise, considering the developer has been honing its cinematic craft for decades now as it pushes the boundaries of what games are capable of. This 90-second extravaganza is no exception, with its stellar combination of sumptuous landscapes, fascinating characters, and an impressively detailed world.  But if you’ll permit me to put our mounting anticipation aside for a second, this launch trailer also represents the next step in a long journey for Rockstar. Every single blockbuster it has released over the years, whether it be San Andreas or Red Dead Redemption 2, has acted as an exceptional example of what video games can be in the modern era.  To celebrate the imminent arrival of GTA 6, let’s take a look back at Rockstar launch trailers of the past and how they’ve evolved over the years.  Grand Theft Auto 3 (2001)  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WUi3davvBmM Nobody expected Grand Theft Auto 3 to revolutionize the medium when it first launched for PS2 back in 2001. The first two games were enjoyably chaotic romps with an incredible sense of attitude, but it wasn’t until the third core entry that Rockstar’s destiny changed forever.  Even 25 years later, its release-date trailer is a confident glimpse into the pixelated grime of Liberty City. It was a living, breathing metropolis that dwarfed anything we’d seen in video games with a lead character in Claude who felt like he didn’t just exist in this world: He lived in it.  There are few narrative scenes in the trailer itself, with the focus otherwise on pulling off sick handbrake turns in luscious cars or blowing away innocent pedestrians with sniper rifles. It is representative of where Rockstar--back then known as DMA Design--and its priorities sat at the time. Despite its archaic presentation at points, the editing remains top-notch and I can’t imagine watching this back in 2001 and not being blown away.  Grand Theft Auto: Vice City (2002)  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iTxmx0SG0qU Coming just a year later, Vice City was a profound evolution of everything GTA 3 did well and then some. It had more style, more music, and more confidence in the crime experience it delivered. Even today, the atmosphere that drips from this game simply by going for a drive and switching on the radio is amazing. Toto’s "Africa" on the strip hits different.  While Liberty City was an innovative open world, from a visual perspective it was dark, grey, and consistently industrial without a memorable aesthetic identity. Vice City is a different story, and the trailer is proof of how confident Rockstar can be with an audience of millions already signed up for the ride.  Don’t get me wrong--there is still plenty of excessive violence, police chases, and cars flying across the screen, but it’s presented with a renewed philosophy that can still be seen in how Rockstar produces trailers to this day. It’s all about pulling viewers into its virtual worlds and showing what their own actions will soon inflict upon it.  Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas (2004)  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yOzcbtsw_pQ San Andreas marked the first time Grand Theft Auto trailers stopped focusing on what you can do in the open world and instead gave attention to characters and narrative, along with all the different regions this virtual take on California had to offer. Even today, it’s a huge world that begins in protagonist CJ’s run-down neighborhood before introducing us to Los Santos and beyond.  Music also plays a pivotal role in setting the tone, as "Welcome to the Jungle" by Guns N' Roses slowly but surely builds tension until CJ is firing an RPG at helicopters before walking away from explosions like a badass. I haven’t played San Andreas in years, but rewatching this trailer and being hypnotized by the sun-drenched hues of fictional California makes me want to surrender myself to it all over again.   Grand Theft Auto 4 (2008)  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tNyt1Y-7TTI This is when Rockstar started getting serious, or at the very least realized it was in a position to elevate the medium in terms of interactivity and storytelling. Beginning right as Nico Bellic steps off the boat and into the arms of his overbearing cousin, the trailer never once leaves his side. GTA 4 is Nico’s story, and Rockstar wants us to know how his first steps into Liberty City and the people he meets and grows close to will change who he is as a person.  I love how we witness him simply walking the streets of Liberty City as a normal fellow who isn’t determined to shoot things up all the time. Things eventually escalate after we’re given brief windows into conversations with characters like Brucie and Playboy X before ending in a shower of epic explosions, but Nico always remains at the center. He is a man without any purpose in life who is unafraid to die for his family, and Rockstar used this as a foundation to tell what is arguably still the series’ most human story.  Red Dead Redemption (2010)  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-o7rES_3ymA Red Dead Redemption might not belong to the Grand Theft Auto series, but the cowboy epic still saw Rockstar continue to evolve its cinematic presentation with more realistic characters and cutscenes that would naturally bleed into the future. The launch trailer provides a simple yet effective introduction into the game’s fading Wild West as John Marston narrates exactly how he ended up in this sticky situation.  What I love most about it is how Rockstar begs the viewer to ask questions about who these characters are and what their relationship to Marston might be. It’s the first time we had ever seen most of them, yet the launch trailer helped cement outlaws like Dutch, Bill, and Javier into legend. Rockstar knew it had a gun-slinging masterpiece on its hands here, with Red Dead Redemption taking inspiration from countless cowboy classics only to soon become one in its own right.   Grand Theft Auto 5 (2013)  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hvoD7ehZPcM The launch trailer for GTA 5 is awfully similar to what Rockstar just dished out with GTA 6. It's a brief, stylish, and supremely confident glimpse into a boundary-breaking world with a cast of characters we’d grow to love and despise in equal measure. The studio knew it was about to release one of the biggest video games of all time, and even 13 years ago, we were following each new piece of pre-launch information with feverish anticipation.  I remember watching breakdowns of the launch trailer picking out specific details in the open world or trying to figure out how the individual stories of Michael, Trevor, and Franklin would be connected. Graphically it seems a smidge antiquated by today’s standards, but by the standards of the time, it was unbelievable.  Red Dead Redemption 2 (2018)  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HVRzx17WHVk And now we’ve arrived at the present day. Well, kinda. Video games take a really long time to make. Red Dead Redemption 2 was the last major project released by Rockstar, but as I rewatch the launch trailer, I’m convinced it could have come out yesterday. Arthur Morgan is the lead of a mature, introspective story about a society that is leaving the lawless times of mysterious outlaws behind. The weight of this era and everything our troubled hero is set to go through is conveyed immaculately in just a single minute.  It also has the benefit of being a prequel to one of the best video games ever made, but as a consequence must make us warm to characters we know will eventually become our sworn enemies. Managing this alone is a monumental achievement, not to mention how this trailer made us want to spend hundreds of hours in a world we didn’t know much about.  What blows me away most, though, is how GTA 6 still feels like a massive leap forward when it comes to visual and mechanical complexity, despite releasing during an era where so many other games are hitting an awkward technological plateau.  Nobody does it like Rockstar, and GTA 6 once again feels poised to change gaming forever if it can stick the landing. In a decade’s time, I imagine we’ll be reminiscing about this launch trailer and precisely how it made us feel ahead of release. Only a month to go, so I’ll see you in Leonida!
