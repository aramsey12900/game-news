Nintendo Switch Sports Resort Preview - A Return Visit To Wuhu IslandNEW
...
Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Nintendo Release: October 22, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ The 2006 release of Wii Sports was a cultural phenomenon. People fought each other in the aisles of Best Buy to secure their Wii consoles before the holidays, primarily because of the Wii's pack-in motion-control tech demo. Family gatherings often devolved into bowling or tennis matches, regardless of whose house we were at since everyone owned a Wii. Three years later, Wii Sports Resort didn't have the same cultural impact, but it introduced the Wii Motion Plus, giving fans a reason to upgrade. With Nintendo Switch Sports in 2022, the novelty came from the series' long absence and the franchise arriving on the wildly popular and far more advanced Nintendo Switch. But with this month's release of Nintendo Switch Sports Resort, the cultural significance has faded substantially with each and every entry. However, after spending an hour touring the newly renovated Wuhu Island, I'm not concerned much with how much of a mainstream impact it will have. Instead, I'm more focused on the several hours of lighthearted fun it will deliver to my friends and me.  Nintendo Switch Sports Resort delivers a heavily upgraded version of Wuhu Island, which most famously appeared in Wii Sports Resort. The resort island is explorable on land via Skateboard, air via Prop Plane, and sea via Power Cruiser. I had the opportunity explore by all three of these extremely intuitive methods. Gliding across the ground on your Skateboard is as simple as holding one Joy-Con in Mouse Mode and mimicking the kick-push motion, with tilting the Joy-Con to steer and lifting it off the surface (in my case, my leg) to jump and perform tricks. The Prop Plane has you hold the Joy-Con like a paper airplane you're ready to throw, with tilt controls and the gyroscope controlling your pitch and speed. Power Cruising has you jump into the seat of a jet ski by holding two Joy-Cons like handle bars, tilting to steer and twisting to rev the engine faster. Exploring the island is enjoyable, as you search for touristy photo areas that explain a bit about the history of Wuhu Island, as well as abundant collectibles that reward you with points to customize your Mii or avatar.I enjoyed the exploration element of Nintendo Switch Sports Resort, and it will almost certainly add a new layer of replayability to the overall package, but the main attraction is the individual sports. Thumb Wrestling is a goofy minigame where you hold the Joy-Con upright and try to pin your opponent's thumb, while they furiously shake their Joy-Con to escape your grasp. The Archery game's special mode using two upright Joy-Cons lets four people aim at different parts of the same target simultaneously (with points given to your opponent if you accidentally hit their section).  Boxing has you defending from incoming attacks and then picking your shots when you see an opening. Table Tennis has you holding the Joy-Con like a paddle and trying to get your opponent to make a mistake so they either miss the table or you smash their weak return shot. And, of course, bowling is still as fun and straightforward as ever; if you haven't played since Wii Sports, you'll notice it's no longer about release timing and more about spin and aim. Each sport I played controlled well, even if the Boxing motion controls struggled if I threw a punch the correct way and I miss being able to cheese the release timing of the Wii Sports iteration of Bowling. You can play them in a variety of different formats, including special variants, like Bowling with a hundred pins, as well as the Island Crown mode, where you compete against 20 CPU characters in nine sports with the mission to climb the ranks and take on the Island Legend boss of that sport. Island Crown, in particular, sounds like a mode that will steal away hours of my time when the final game arrives. Regardless of how big a splash Nintendo Switch Sports Resort is poised to make, I got what I needed from the hour I spent wailing and waggling my Joy-Cons: a fun, casual sports experience full of bite-sized minigames that will deliver enjoyment whether I'm alone or in a party setting. Thankfully, it won't be long before I can have Nintendo Switch Sports Resort on my personal console, as it arrives on Switch 2 on October 22.

Switch Sports Resort Is An Overly Familiar VacationNEW
It's hard to overstate the impact of Wii Sports on Nintendo's fortunes. A simple motion-controlled game concept became a cultural phenomenon and arguably pulled the company back from the brink of a fl...
It's hard to overstate the impact of Wii Sports on Nintendo's fortunes. A simple motion-controlled game concept became a cultural phenomenon and arguably pulled the company back from the brink of a flagging game business. Switch Sports Resort is now the fifth game in the franchise, and while it still finds some depth in surprising places, the novelty is also wearing thin. I got an extended hands-on with Switch Sports Resort, trying out a total of eight sports and activities from the selection, and giving me a good sampling of how this next sports game includes both competitive sporting events and free-roaming exploration around Wuhu Island. About half of the events were returning from previous Sports titles, and the others were new for this incarnation. So while there was a good sampling of new activities, my brief time with them prevented me from answering my biggest question: How much mileage will they all have collectively? Switch Sports Resort prop plane Three of the events we tried were focused on exploring Wuhu in a mostly freeform fashion with a time limit: Skateboarding, Prop Plane, and Power Cruising (aka jet skiing). All of these used the Joy-Con controllers in novel ways that reminded me of the early days of the Wii, when the controller was being used sort of like an oddball blend of video game and toy. That means it's potentially fun party fodder as players experiment with strange new control schemes, but also it's hard to see how much longevity they could have. Skateboarding was the oddest of the bunch, a strange blend of mouse and motion controls. We were instructed to turn the Joy-Con on its side to use as a mouse, but that was simply for sliding across a surface (in this case, my pant leg) to emulate pushing off on your skateboard to build speed. The rest of the mechanics were tied to motion-control, including tilting the controller left or right to steer, or flicking it for mid-air tricks. My random wrist-flicks consistently pulled off tricks, but I didn't get a sense for how much skill there was in choosing which tricks you're doing, or chaining them together. Wuhu Island is littered with landmarks to find and photo spots to grab, so my partner and I simply roamed around finding things until the time limit ran out. You can separate from your partner and explore in different directions with split-screen support, and afterwards it will show you the paths you traveled similar to the Hero's Path screen in the recent Zelda games.  Power Cruising was similar, but obviously set in the water. Rather than hold your Joy-Con like a mouse, you would hold them horizontally in each hand to imitate the feel of handlebars of a jet ski. (They aren't connected, of course, so this sensation is a little floaty and you have to use your imagination.) Then you can twist the right Joy-Con to rev up the engines, and tilt the two Joy-Con to steer. Again, we were simply allowed to free roam for a few minutes to grab little collectibles and photo icons of tourist spots around the island. There were a few boost ramps for little stunts like jumping over a bridge, but this event was clearly meant more to be a relaxing tour than an extreme sports event. Switch Sports Resort skateboarding The best of the free-roaming events was Prop Plane, which had you hold the Joy-Con like a toy airplane. If you ever held up a plane as a kid and made "neee-yowwwww" sounds as you ran around the living room, it's like that, but you get to actually control the plane around Wuhu Island. Like the others, this event was about roaming around, collecting doodads and photo icons, but it had a little more skill and finesse, and there was more room to make your own fun. Upon noticing the volcano in the middle of Wuhu Island, I made it my mission to bee-line straight for it and see if I could nosedive inside. I was rewarded for my curiosity with a tricky obstacle course to steer through that took me underneath a significant part of the island and out through a grotto. As you explore in these modes, you can unlock more starting spots, so even within the brief time limit, you'll be able to go further and see more as you pick a location to begin exploring from. A Nintendo rep also told me that Skateboarding and Power Cruising would have a free roam function without a timer or collectibles, but Prop Plane wouldn't. I don't know why the best event has to be the only one without the ability to explore freely. Two events return from Wii Sports Resort, with mixed results. Boxing has you use the Joy-Cons to throw punches or move your hands into defensive positions, or "lean" by tilting them to dodge shots. Maybe it was simply that we only played one match against the easiest opponent on the ladder, but this was easy to cheese by just pummeling the opponent mercilessly. (Granted, the sheer cruelty of beating the absolute crap out of a poor hapless Mii was the biggest laugh I had during the preview session.) The full event may add more complexity as you go against higher ranked opponents, but if that's the case, the short demo didn't show that well.  Switch Sports Resort boxing Archery has gotten a nice upgrade from its Wii Sports Resort incarnation, as a competitive event with solid motion controls that emulate pulling back on the bow string to release a shot. What makes it stand out is the ability to score points for your opponents if you miss, as well as a final round that slices the target up like a pie chart, with thinner slices for the current score leaders.  Bizarrely, the killer app of the bunch may be Thumb Wrestling. Wait, hear me out! This event was very silly, and I'm not sure how much longevity it would have, but there actually is a bit more depth than you would expect. You hold the Joy-Con vertically with a thumb on the trigger button, and you press and hold it to push your thumb down, and tilt the controller to shift your maneuvering. You can pin your opponent, and they can shake their own remote to break out of your hold.  But you can also power up your thumb by briefly holding it down, giving the whole thing a tense risk-reward element. Putting your thumb down risks your opponent pinning you, but if you do it just long enough, your thumb grows bigger and more powerful, and it will be easier for you to pin your opponent. So in my brief match, both my opponent and I were constantly trying to quickly put ourselves into a vulnerable position to muscle up our thumbs, dodge one another's attempts to exploit our respective momentary vulnerability, pin one another, and shake free. The game fits nicely into that easy-to-play, hard-to-master element that makes a great party experience. Switch Sports Resort bowling Finally, we tried two more events returning from the original Wii Sports: Table Tennis and Bowling. In my limited experience, both of those were exactly as you'd expect from the originals, and after a little practice, my bowling skills came back from 2006. There may be more twists and more variety of challenges, but as far as I saw, they're both faithful recreations of those original events that helped Wii Sports become such a breakout success. Switch Sports Resort looks to be very much a part of the continuity of Wii Sports and its successors, for better and worse. Like the rest in the series, it's built around minigames that are enjoyable and toy-like, if a bit shallow, and it will be best if you have some friends to play it with. My main concern is whether it has enough depth to sustain yet another trip to Wuhu Island, or if this is one vacation you can safely skip.

Dungeons & Dragons and World of Warcraft are two of the biggest fantasy games around, even though they exist in completely different mediums. As such, a crossover between the two is a natural fit (I bet there are plenty of D&D groups who also run raids in World of Warcraft together), but the form of that collaboration is less certain. In a private presentation breaking down the upcoming Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft project, the designers made their approach clear: this book is a toybox. "This book is absolutely a toolbox or toybox that you can use to fill your D&D game with these sorts of adventures, and I think the dungeons represent that really well," senior game designer Joey Haeck said. "There are a lot of tools at your disposal that I think make it really easy for a DM to explore the world of Azeroth with their players."This isn't a linear, pre-written campaign, but a set of curated crossover locations, character options, magic items, and monsters for players to build their own version of events. Of course, as Haeck mentions, the book also includes some of the most iconic dungeons from World of Warcraft's history, and if your DM isn't interested in coming up with a narrative framework to tie everything together, you're welcome to just storm some castles together. The book also includes advice on building campaigns around World of Warcraft villains, many of which have stat blocks included.Each dungeon is designed for parties of a certain level, defined in D&D as tiers of play. You can venture into the Deadmines and Shadowfang Keep at levels 1-4; Blackfathom Deeps at levels 5-10; Dire Maul at levels 11-16; and Azjol-Nerub at levels 17-20. There's also the Scarlet Monastery, which is designed for levels 5-10 or 11-16 depending on which wing you're exploring, and Onyxia's Lair, designed for characters level 17-20 and centered around a boss fight with the dragon who dwells within. Yes, this is a dungeon with a dragon; as I said, the crossover is a natural fit. Dungeon layouts are pretty loyal to their MMO counterparts, though Haeck mentions they've added new secrets and shortcuts to some, which will be a surprise even to players intimately familiar with the maps. And while most problems in World of Warcraft are solved with combat, the D&D adaptation allows for alternate solutions, like stealth and negotiation, where it makes sense. Still, if you want to go in guns blazing, you're more than welcome to. Some dungeons include random combat encounter tables for that very purpose.The foes on those tables are a mixture of newly designed Warcraft monsters and existing D&D monsters, some of which are re-skinned to fit the Warcraft universe. For example, there's already a stat block for a water elemental in the 2025 Monster Manual, but if you want players to face off against a Murloc, the book includes rules to convert a table of humanoid stat blocks by adding the Amphibious trait and a swim speed. Meanwhile, characters like Jaina Proudmoore, Sylvanas Windrunner, and the Lich King Arthas each get their own unique stats, rated at a challenge rating of 22, 23, and 25, respectively. And while there are plenty of dragons in D&D, their abilities don't line up one-to-one with those in the Warcraft universe, so the crossover will include Green, Blue, Red, Black, and Bronze Dragonflight Dragons to square up against.While the presentation was more DM-focused, it ended with some player-centric mechanics. Tool proficiencies (which can fall to the wayside in many D&D games) are repurposed to correspond to World of Warcraft's professions. There's guidance on how gathering professions can be used to collect resources and how production professions can be used to craft new items.Finally, we saw some of the game's factions, which parties can gain affinity with using D&D's existing Renown system, found in the Dungeon Master's Guide. There's the Alliance and the Horde, of course, but the book includes "more than 20" factions total, including the Argent Dawn, the Cenarion Circle, the Earthen Ring, the Explorer's League and Reliquary, the Kirin Tor, the Steamwheedle Cartel, and the Valdrakken Accord. Raising your Renown brings mechanical advantages – allies of the Earthen Ring can use the faction's portals, and the closest allies of the Valdrakken Accord are granted a wish. It's a toybox with plenty of options, and I'm excited to see it all laid out, but it's a pretty daunting project. World of Warcraft has decades of history crafted with a fundamentally different design philosophy than Dungeons & Dragons. Because of that, you could never fit it all into one book, and it could never feel the same as the experience on a PC. The design team knows this, however, and has structured the book accordingly."I've been trying to shift my thinking to talking about [paraphrasing]," project lead designer James Wyatt said in the presentation. "Because we're not doing like a word-for-word translation of any WoW game mechanics. The game you're sitting down at the table to play is still D&D in the setting of World of Warcraft, and with a lot of the trappings of World of Warcraft [...] So the goal is to try to evoke the feeling of playing WoW and let you interact with the characters and creatures and abilities that you have as a WoW character, while also opening up the world for you to explore in a whole new way, because you can talk to anybody that you meet, you can interact with anything you see. Which is the beauty of a tabletop game."For more on the new books coming to Dungeons & Dragons, check out our recap of the publisher's massive Gen Con presentation.

In End of Abyss, you play as a technician who ventures into an abandoned facility alongside soldiers and their captain. The setting is liminal and mysterious – fitting for a survival horror game – where you must seek out old notes and item descriptions if you want narrative context. For a game so light on plot, I found myself instantly sucked into its worldbuilding, cautiously curious to know what was around every corner.It helps that the complex is a joy to explore. End of Abyss is clearly inspired by the Metroid series and borrows some of its best traits. The map constantly winds in on itself, and you're just as likely to discover a shortcut back to a previous area as you are to find a rare resource. Even in the sea of similar indie games, I was consistently impressed by End of Abyss' large-scale level design. There's also a scanner (à la Metroid Prime) that allows you to analyze enemies and search the environment for ways to progress. I don't know what the facility is called, and even after rolling credits, I'm not quite sure what purpose it served, but the setting is one of End of Abyss' most memorable highlights. While End of Abyss is structurally similar to Metroid, it's also a survival horror game with twin-stick shooting. Your pistol has infinite ammo, but you need to craft bullets for other weapons you find (most of which are optional). Every conflict is tense, making my narrow victories satisfying, and the game has just enough enemy variety to keep things interesting, including zombies, bugs, and robots. On more than one occasion, I had to grind for bullets and bombs before returning. I got stuck in walls, encountered invisible walls, and even fell out of bounds at one point. The map would also have glitches, marking unlocked doors as locked or omitting certain landmarks, making backtracking confusing. The bright side is you save frequently to heal, so resets don't erase much progress. End of Abyss has also already received multiple patches during my playtime, and some issues have been resolved, but it affected my experience nonetheless.Despite the glitches, I loved snooping through the cold, industrial hallways of End of Abyss, and the bugs did surprisingly little to affect my overall opinion.
