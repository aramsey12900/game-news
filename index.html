There are data breaches from Meta, AI-generated restaurant menus that make burritos look like they've been shot with a gun, and other evidence that technology is constantly distorting and desecrating our lives. Upcoming Silent Hill-ish horror game Signal Veil understands the weight of that, so its protagonist uses a little flip phone as her weapon. Aki, who could pass as another vapid anime schoolgirl if it weren't for the sleepy bruises under her eyes, looks terrified to be alone in a new trailer revealed during Insider Gaming's 28 Games Later Horror Showcase. But, even when she's being pursued by a fleshy monstrosity, she isn't really by herself -- she has her mom's dainty, vintage cell phone, which players can use to physically attack enemies in a unique combat system.  Developer Time Hunters tells Insider Gaming in an interview, "We grew up loving classic horror games like Resident Evil, but for Signal Veil we wanted to lean much more into psychological horror." So, as the Silent Hill franchise's crackling radio did for many of its main characters, Aki's cell phone shivers and hisses whenever it detects a monster. The sound is enough to make you, the player, paranoid, but Signal Veil's sanity system takes fear further by giving Aki's fraying mind gameplay consequences.  "As her mental state deteriorates, the way she fights becomes more desperate, aggressive, and messy," Signal Veil developers say. Her teeny cell phone can knock enemies' heads around, or even use its camera flash to stun enemies and leave them open to a parry. "We really like games with parry systems, so at some point we thought, 'What if you could use the phone to parry an enemy?'" devs say. "It was one of those ideas that sounded strange at first, but once we tried it, we loved it." After all, technology is a weapon -- if it can hurt us, it should be able to save us, too.  Signal Veil doesn't yet have a release date, but it's available to wishlist on Steam.

How Silent Hill: Townfall’s “Creepy Game Boy” Weaponizes Nostalgia For The BetterNEW
. I have a complicated relationship with the 1990s, an era remembered for its hazy pre-HD tech and early signs of broader connectivity without the mentally taxing standard of an always-online worl...
. I have a complicated relationship with the 1990s, an era remembered for its hazy pre-HD tech and early signs of broader connectivity without the mentally taxing standard of an always-online world. Lately, I've found the aesthetics of that era calling to me in the books I read, the shows I watch, and even in my social media feeds that regularly spit out "remember when?" reels that dredge up imagery of classrooms and malls that I never actually set foot in yet feel familiar. Instagram seems to think I'm simply nostalgic for this stuff, not understanding that I actually find it very eerie, snapshots of a childhood I can hardly recall for complicated reasons. And yet, I'm mesmerized by it. Most recently, I felt that same pull in Screen Burn's Silent Hill: Townfall.  Whereas games like Silent Hill and Silent Hill 2 were set roughly in their respective modern days and are today revered as dreary time capsules, Townfall resurrects similar vibes, in turn revealing which parts stick out most to the team performing that necromancy. In a recent conversation with the studio's co-founder and Townfall's writer, Jon McKellan, he told me how Screen Burn sought to weaponize a particular type of nostalgia through the game's handheld CRTV device, and why the 1990s remain the ideal backdrop for a horror story. GameSpot: When Silent Hill: Townfall was first revealed, I wondered if it would be primarily a "gizmo" kind of game, like Stories Untold. Observation has a lot of physical interaction like that, too. What is it the team likes about that sort of thing in games? Jon McKellan: I think with horror, having something recognisable and tangible just sort of grounds you in the world a little bit more. People might not ever have owned one, but we all can understand the sensation of holding a little screen in our hands that shows us scary things! I’ve always loved analogue video aesthetics, and it’s been a big part of my own art style through my career, so the idea of making an entire core mechanic/device around that aesthetic and experience felt like a great fit, especially when riffing off the static and radio features of previous Silent Hill titles. Was there ever a version of Silent Hill: Townfall that was only going to be that (I'd actually expected something like Killer Frequency), or was it built from the ground up with first-person exploration and combat in mind, too? We always had combat and exploration in mind. One of the earliest conversations I can recall on this project was Graeme and [I] discussing the idea of allowing the player to get up, walk around, explore, and deal with threats while solving those puzzles. It felt like the next big step in the experiences we at Screen Burn were building--to finally face a threat that was more visceral and immediate than we had ever had before in our previous titles.  How did the team land on the physical design and form factor of the device? It kind of reminds me of a haunted Game Boy. I think we did actually refer to it as a "Creepy Game Boy" at one point in concept phases! We looked at a lot of different versions of pocket TVs from the '80s and '90s, and it was a case of trying to find the right shape, size, and style to feel weighty and old-fashioned in the characters’ hands, while giving us a big enough screen size on it to be useful in gameplay.  Some of the original Sony Watchman designs, for example, were very tall with small screens, and that kind of thing wouldn’t work on landscape compositions like a video game in first person, so we took all the best ideas, shapes, colour treatments, and so on from various different models and designed our own from scratch.   What came first: the '90s setting or the CRTV gadget itself? How did one then inform the other?  It all really came together at the same time early on. We knew we wanted to do something new with the concept of the classic Silent Hill radio, as it was a very obvious "Screen Burn" thing for us to play with.  At the same time, when I was exploring concepts for the story, the '90s setting fit for so many different reasons: the state of the world at that time, in places like St. Amelia especially, the rare availability of guns, and the fact that technology was starting to take off without actually being too useful at the time. We had email, internet, and pocket screens, but none were so useful they would get you out of a jam. There was no GPS on your phone, or even things like digitally tuning signals--it had to feel analogue, so it all just meshed together really well with the CRTV concept. Was the CRTV nearly something very different, or perhaps even a traditional Silent Hill radio? The very first sketches were in fact just a radio, yeah, and the idea was that you could simply tune into frequencies rather than passively receive static from enemies or the occasional voice. It felt very basic though and didn’t add much to gameplay, but tuning on the fly made sense. As soon as we latched on to the concept of it being a TV and having a screen that could be used for all sorts of game design uses, story development, and puzzle clues, the opportunities just immediately opened up for it, and there was no turning back. How did you land on its specific functionality, and how it allows for tracking several enemies after scanning for them, but also really only one at a time in a "live" sense, albeit also through walls the way it does? It feels like it must've gone through some revisions before you landed on something you really liked. There was a lot of experimentation for sure! We had tried lots of different versions of the mechanic surrounding enemies, from each ‘type’ of enemy on its own frequency, but multiple on the same one, to not showing their position visually and only showing you video of what type of enemy it was, and so on.  The great thing was that it was really fast to iterate and explore ideas, so we threw a lot of things at it to see what would work in different situations. How would it work with multiple enemies? How frustrating could it be to tune back and forth? What happens in areas with verticality? The last main addition was the quick tune, where once you had tuned into an enemy, you could quickly skip back and forth with the D-pad, making it much easier to track multiple enemies as you move around some more complex spaces.  Is there anything particularly Scottish about the CRTV's design? When playing Silent Hill: Townfall, I felt like I learned about the country's electricity system with that keycard-in-the-closet section. There’s nothing in particular about the CRTV that is Scottish per se; it’s just something that fits that era particularly well. Not everyone would have one--especially not in a working-class island town!--but you maybe knew someone who did, or always wanted one if you were browsing an Argos catalogue in the '90s. It’s just like a portable cassette player--has a connection with that time and place, I guess. Townfall's CRTV is used for stealth, puzzles, and story in different ways. Do you or anyone on the team still collect things like this today? I'm 37, so I'm really into '90s nostalgia right now, and things like Backrooms and Silent Hill: Townfall have warped my Instagram algorithm to show me a lot of that stuff, though I don't own any of it myself. Oh, we have a collection! Pretty much every bit of content you see on the CRTV in-game was actually played through a collection of different real pocket TVs or broadcast monitors. I run the signals from the game engine into these screens, and then generally distort the signal with static, magnets, and deliberately loose cables, and sort of "play" the interference and static like an instrument, varying its intensity or abruptness in time with dialogue or key moments.  I’d have a camera setup pointing at the screen, covered in sheets and filters to remove reflections, and film the "performance" of these scenes. This way, I’d pick up the real static and distortion, not faking it in software. It would also pick up different pixel grids for each type of screen--a 480p broadcast monitor versus a small LCD pocket TV would give you different pixel densities and layouts, which I would use depending on the scene or use case.  So over the years I’ve built up a collection of half-broken TVs and monitors, and still use them in production to this day. It’s one of the most fun parts of the job, and I could talk about it for hours! I'm assuming you agree with me here, and please feel free to disagree if not, but what is it about the '90s that feels so right for horror? I think it’s back to that cusp of technology thing. You can use old PCs to read basic emails, or receive signals that break up convincingly in a cinematic way. The more you move into the 2000s and the digital era comes in, the more you lose some of that. When digital TV breaks up, it usually just stops, which is no fun visually!  The main thing I think, though, is that texture, static, and tangible form factor all ground the player. You can be in an unbelievable situation in a dreamscape, and still feel scared because the thing in your hand is relatable in some way. You can imagine feeling that in your hand subconsciously, so I think that just lowers the barrier to immersion a little. We talk a lot internally about "weaponising nostalgia"--taking that familiar thing that you connect with in an “Oh, I remember this!” way and then making it tell you horrible things!  Silent Hill: Townfall is out now on PC and PS5. Dune: Awakening Review - Only The Strong SurviveNEW
Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S ...
Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Funcom Developer: Funcom Release: June 10, 2025 (PC), September 22, 2026 (PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S) Rating: Mature As much as Dune: Awakening guides you around Arrakis, it can be a hot and unforgiving place. Almost everything around you is trying to kill you, and if not, then you're being used as a pawn to further someone's agenda. Awakening succeeds in making players feel like everyone is out to get them, all while encouraging you to rise to the occasion and come out the other end stronger.Dune: Awakening is set in its own timeline. Here, things play out very differently than in the films and books. Paul Atreides' mother gives birth to a daughter, as she was supposed to. Because of this, House Atreides never falls, and a War of Assassins between House Harkonnen and House Atreides begins. As a result, the Fremen are all but dead. Awakening takes time to explain the ins and outs of the game. With a planet known for being as hot as it is, it only makes sense that the sun's rays are one of the constant threats. Learning how to survive never felt like a chore; it was a necessary sequence to learn. By the end, I truly felt like I was setting myself up to succeed. Exploring the map is a lot of fun. Whenever I saw a crashed ship or a small camp in the distance, I knew there would either be minerals worth mining or a dungeon to run. I never felt I wasted my time whenever I went off the beaten path. You're always encouraged to explore new areas and see what mysteries await you as you get into the desert.If it's by accident or on purpose, one of the best things you can stumble across is the feud between House Harkonnen and House Atreides. Here is where Awakening really starts to home in on the idea of never trusting anyone. As you align yourself with one house or the other, you're always encouraged and given the option to betray the one you've sided with to help the other. Even though it's such a prominent part of the overall story, it never really affects your character's personal journey. Having no real consequences is a missed opportunity when you consider that both Houses have areas you can visit throughout the world. There was never any danger when I walked into Harkonnen territory well after I sided with Atreides. The only hostilities I faced were a few rude remarks and higher prices on certain items.Combat, on the other hand, never falls short, thanks in large part to classes that grant you ample agency in how you approach the game. Throughout most of my playthrough, I prioritized the Mentat, which gave me the ability to drop a turret anywhere, and the Trooper, which allowed me to use a grappling hook and grenades. Combat played out in several different ways for me. If multiple people rushed me, I'd throw a grenade and drop my turret. If there was a sniper off in the distance, I'd use my hook to close the gap. The freedom I had with whatever threat was in front of me made going into combat a joy. If I got bored of one playstyle, I could invest in another skill and try out something new altogether. Each class has its own set of perks and benefits that keep combat fresh and fun.Being able to handle whatever was thrown at me was vital when exploring Awakening's main questline. The first half of the main story does a fantastic job of showcasing the importance of the Fremen while also serving as an extended tutorial. The way the game romanticizes the Fremen is a breath of fresh air compared with the harsh realities of living on Arrakis. By the end of the opening act, you'll feel you learned a lot about how the world used to be before the war began.This contrasts sharply with the game's second half. It serves as a reminder of the daily horrors people endure on the planet, all while making you question your humanity and whether you even have a say in what's going on around you. The end of the base game left me in awe of everything thrown at me and of how far they took an original idea. Another aspect of the story I appreciate is how approachable it was for solo players. Even though there's a single-player option, I chose a multiplayer server and experienced most of the game alone. I never felt like I hit a wall because I didn't have the manpower to deal with whatever was in front of me. If there was, it was simply because I wasn't playing smart. The few times I did play with someone else only made the game more enjoyable. Even though I haven't fully explored the post-game, the time I've spent in the Deep Desert was what I'd hoped it would be. This area can only be reached by Orentogether, which you unlock toward the endgame, and can be experienced in PvE or PvP. Regardless of how you tackle it, the Deep Desert seems to go on forever and resets once a week, so fully exploring it is a task in itself. However, Deep Desert also boasts some of the best loot and dungeons the game has to offer.If you want to take it a step further, you can also try the Landsraad. This feature pits both Houses against one another and tasks them with completing a specific goal. Whichever side comes out on top is rewarded with loot on par with what you'd get in the Deep Desert. While it doesn’t add much to the story, it aptly supports the fantasy of truly being part of the ongoing conflict.Even if you know nothing about Dune, the game does a great job of explaining the significance of each house and why you should care about everything around you. Dune: Awakening has everything it needs to remain true to the franchise: endless deserts, war and espionage on all fronts, and of course giant sand worms. It takes a lot of creative freedom and hits many of the important marks, all while reminding you of the brutal reality of living on Arrakis.  Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
