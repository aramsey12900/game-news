Over the last few years, we lost sight of that, and there have been real consequences. We lost trust, we lost incredible colleagues, and we lost sight of what we should be prioritizing most, our players.As a new leadership team, we must own that.We aren’t just listening to feedback, we understand your disappointment. The studio acknowledges Marathon "did not reach as many players as we had hoped" and that it needs to evolve. It also states it still has many things planned for the October Nightfall Refresh and December Symbiosis updates. More updates regarding Marathon will be shared later this week. 2026 has been a challenging year for Bungie. Marathon arrived in March to a generally positive critical reception (here's our review), but as Bungie acknowledges, it has not retained the player numbers the studio hoped for. This has only intensified following the end of Destiny 2, as some fans blamed Marathon's existence for the studio moving on from Destiny 2. To make matters worse, with Destiny 2 coming to an end, Bungie laid off most of that game's development team. With Bungie addressing these realities head-on, it will be interesting to see if these proposed updates bring back lapsed fans of both games.

Valor Mortis Preview: Uneven Difficulty Balance Mars An Otherwise Great Soulslike So FarNEW

Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Lyrical Games, One More Level Developer: One More Level Release: October 13, 2026 Rating: Mature Valor Mortis is the next game from One More Level, the studio behind the Ghostrunner series. As a massive fan of those two cyberpunk parkour-action games, I've had my eye on Valor Mortis, the team's first-person take on the Soulslike formula, since its reveal last August. When I played it last year, I was shocked by how well OML's trademark first-person action translates into a slower, more deliberate Soulslike, given that the best action games in this sub-genre are typically played from a third-person perspective. Now, I've put more than 15 hours into a preview build of Valor Mortis, playing through its first four chapters, dying a lot, defeating bosses, meeting new characters, and learning more about why the deceased Napoleon Bonaparte is in my head talking to me while I acquire more and more supernatural powers. I've enjoyed most of what I've played, but – and this is unfortunately a big but – the difficulty balance in Valor Mortis is incredibly uneven, and key moments, like chapter-ending boss fights, are spoiling what should otherwise be fun climactic bouts. There's no rhyme or reason to the balance of Valor Mortis, and it made for an often-frustrating experience in a game I can tell has the Soulslike juice. This boss killed me probably 30 times After dying in the game's opening, protagonist William wakes up, unsure of what's going on or why Napoleon is talking to him, seemingly telepathically. He marches forward, looking for answers amongst the decrepit trenches, battlefields, abandoned buildings, and torched villages as to what's going on, and along the way, he sucks up mysterious powers that imbue him with flames, time-freeze abilities, and more in his right hand. These powers are especially helpful because many enemies are weak to fire, and you can easily stun them by filling up their burn gauge; the same can be said for William's time-freezing power, which allows him to hit an enemy for massive damage when all their freeze gauges are filled. It's an enjoyable BioShock-esque set of powers that complements his ranged guns and various swords. I like that the saber is great for general damage and blocking, while the rapier uses stabs to target weak points with heightened parry stagger (at the cost of not being able to block), and I'm excited to see what other weapons will fill the weapon wheel that teases me with multiple unused spots. The environmental design is smart and beautiful, with just enough explorable side passages to keep moving through these large spaces enjoyable – quick puzzles along the way keep my brain engaged outside of Valor Mortis extra-difficult combat. While the French countryside and post-battle battlefields aren't anything unique in video games, One More Level has painted these otherwise unremarkable settings with otherworldly growths, formations, and more. The result is a Remedy Entertainment-esque spin on 18th-Century France that remained a delight throughout this demo's 15-hour runtime. And, given the preview demo ran me roughly 15 hours, I suspect there's a lot more game left in the final build of Valor Mortis.  Despite loving what I was playing, the spectre of uneven balance hung heavy over everything, perhaps a testament to the bones of Valor Mortis. I recognize this is a Soulslike, but the difficulty seems way off. I'm no Soulslike scrub, either; I quite enjoy the genre (and if we're talking credentials, I have the Platinum trophy in Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice, and trophy hunters know that is no easy feat). But when a boss can wipe out basically 60 percent of my HP, even after upgrading my health as much as possible at this point in the game, what should be fun tests of combat prowess become a demand for perfection, and that's a problem. Soulslike fights are about learning the enemy, recognizing patterns, and countering with your own moves – but these bosses often hit so hard that actually surviving long enough to learn how they move, react, and counter felt impossible. I probably died more than 100 times to bosses in this 15-hour demo alone.  That difficulty imbalance extends to some mob enemies in the game, too, and I dreaded seeing certain enemies in my path because, despite being a nameless regular ol' enemy, I knew I had what was basically a mini-boss ahead of me. I like mini-bosses, but I don't like seven of them in one chapter.  Still, though, even when getting absolutely walloped by enemies, I kept pressing continue at the You Have Died screen. Valor Mortis is shaping up to be a great game thanks to its strong foundation and mechanical bones, One More Level's unique visual design, and an interesting narrative-forward adventure atypical for the Soulslike genre. However, I worry that without some last-minute touch-ups to the game's difficulty balance, Valor Mortis could prove to be more annoying and frustrating to play than anything else, marring what might be a genuinely great action game. Valor Mortis launches October 13, so there's still time for tuning, and I hope it happens.

With Friday the 13th, Halloween, and other horror franchises getting video game adaptations in recent years, you might wonder when it'll be Freddy Krueger's turn, but according to Illfonic, the team behind those games, the task is harder than you might think. To do it right, one aspect of the films creates a big roadblock. "There are plenty of IPs that people are like, 'Why aren't you doing this?' and 'Why aren't you doing that?' As much as I love Elm Street, [those movies are] about these big over-the-top kills," Illfonic's chief creative officer Jared Gerritzen told me in a Zoom call. "Same with Terrifier, where it's like these big, over-the-top kills that you would expect that you would have to do [in order] to do it right. That's the most costly thing, in some cases. You'd have to do hundreds of those to build that basis. You'd have to do all these other layers, and then it just becomes like, 'Well, shit.' Like, there are all these quagmires that we can't go into, and so for us, we're very diligent about the IPs that we choose." With Freddy's dream world seeming like a great fit for a game, I'd long envisioned Elm Street as an obvious candidate for an asymmetrical multiplayer horror game akin to Illfonic's other titles, but listening to Gerritzen speak on the pitfalls, it occurred to me that he's right. It's hard to imagine a game creating enough elaborate set-piece kills to keep them feeling fresh for players who may play for dozens or hundreds of hours, but neither does it make sense to rinse and repeat the same standout moments like Tina's nightmare that saw her crawling on the ceiling, or the way Glen's bed swallows him whole and erupts with a geyser of blood.  Freddy doesn't usually catch up to someone and finish them off with his iconic glove in a way that maps well onto multiplayer. He makes a meal out of them, and that's hard to translate into a game built on replayability. Gerritzen explained that his team has worked on so many games of this sort that, by now, it gives them something like a running start. "We've been through this so much that when we start on a game, we already know what the pitfalls are. [...] And so we start off with a pretty good skeleton, and then we add those layers of the project that we love. We turn down a lot of stuff because it's just like, 'Hey, to do this, it doesn't make sense in a video game form.'" Beyond those gameplay pitfalls, there seems to be a broader rights issue tying up the Elm Street franchise, too. Though Gerritzen didn't comment on that aspect, it's recently been reported that competing Elm Street movies may soon be in the works--one for US audiences and one for international audiences--because the ownership of the intellectual property is currently split. That sort of rights quagmire probably scares off some creators, following what happened to Friday the 13th. Does this mean we'll never get a Freddy game? I don't think that's what Gerritzen meant, though it may mean a team other than Illfonic is the one to make it, or it could be that Illfonic finds a way to make it work in the future.  For now, the team is focused on supporting Halloween, which launched on September 8 and has surpassed a million copies sold, alongside its cabinet of other asymmetrical multiplayer games, Predator: Hunting Grounds, Ghostbusters: Spirits Unleashed, and Killer Klowns from Outer Space. The Blood Of Dawnwalker Wants You To Eat The Rich–LiterallyNEW
The Blood of Dawnwalker’s hero, Coen, begins with a predictably humble origin story common to many fantasy heroes. Outside the small matter of a looming blood mass, where villagers feed on their vampi...
The Blood of Dawnwalker’s hero, Coen, begins with a predictably humble origin story common to many fantasy heroes. Outside the small matter of a looming blood mass, where villagers feed on their vampiric overlords’ blood to ward off the plague, you meet him on just another day in the unremarkable life of an impoverished former-sellsword’s son. He starts out chopping wood, checking traps for hares, playing tag with his energetic siblings, and haggling with the local herbalist over his mother’s desperately needed medicine.  I assumed this inauspicious beginning would become a distant memory once I, playing as Coen, imbibed the supernatural vampiric powers of a Dawnwalker. Surely I’m destined to become rich, powerful, and respected, I thought. I’ll become a willing participant to political machinations within gilded halls of renown. Maybe I’ll even get on the property ladder like in The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, and start my own IKEA collection. No such thing happened. Instead, I was delighted to find Dawnwalker rejects that played-out routine of upward mobility. I was never wooed and welcomed into the halls of the vrakhiri; the game's vampire ruling class reviled me to the last. I firmly remained a peasant, an upstart subject challenging their feudal vampiric hierarchy. Dawnwalker may take place in the 14th Century, but the sense of class solidarity I saw makes for a very modern fantasy. From Gareth the Arthurian knight to Oliver Twist, there’s a long line of fictional characters who treat poverty and precarity as a state to overcome and entirely forget once they’ve acquired  wealth and power. Games aren’t immune: My Dragon Age: Origins City Elf playthrough started in the slums but invariably ended in the throne room; And, for all Atlus’s nods to more democratic governance models, Metaphor: ReFantazio’s protagonist still sets aside his humble Elda roots for the Euchronia highlife. And we can’t forget Geralt, the protagonist of The Witcher series and perhaps the defining hero of the fantasy RPG genre. With Rebel Wolves being founded by several CD Projekt staff, including The Witcher 3 director Konrad Tomaszkiewicz, The White Wolf is perhaps the most interesting comparison of all. Witchers are treated lower than peasants, yet, despite his claim to eke out a living from patchy contracts, Geralt still gets swept into the ruling class’s orbit. He treats with royals and enjoys their hospitality--even their bathtubs. Remember Geralt’s famous scrubdown? It comes right before his audience with the Emperor of Nilfgaard. Put it this way: If you toss a coin to Geralt, he likely spends it at the tavern or smithy. If you toss a coin to Coen, that poor lad desperately needs it to feed his impoverished family. That defiant attitude toward those who think they’re Coen’s betters shapes Dawnwalker’s worldview of power, class, and politics. It extends far beyond Coen; even accounting for branching decisions, it’s etched across its worldbuilding and systems.  It’s seen in its vampires, who aren’t the kind to inspire class envy like the stylish, seductive, and sophisticated modern vampire. Brutish, cruel, and repulsively ugly, the vrakhiri hew closer to the original Eastern European folk tales that first inspired Bram Stoker’s Dracula. For Brencis and his boyars, eternal life doesn’t come with eternal beauty. But it doesn’t matter when power comes first. Wealth is a welcome, but secondary, consideration. And it’s equally visible across the extent of the vrakhiri's blood-tithe regime, which imposes industrialized cruelty across the valley. Peasants wade into fetid swamps as they farm for leeches, fed on their blood and stored for vrakhiri snacks. Handpicked maidens are trafficked under guard across Vale Sangora for unknown sinister ends. And boyar Bakir runs bloodthirsty battle royales where volunteers fight for his favor. That feudal system of extortion is mapped out across the open world and through the game’s reputation system. You accrue notoriety, not political influence. That metasystem places Coen as a destructive interruption to an exploitative machine, well-oiled with peasant blood. And yet, for all the vrakhiri's monstrousness, Rebel Wolves draws a complicated and unresolvable comparison between the vrakhiri and the valley’s former ruler. For many peasants, the new vampiric rulers are little different to the previous knyaz. Their new lords at least offer protection from the plague that’s taken the lives of millions across the rest of Europe, in exchange for serving as a living factory farm prone to ruthless displays of violence.  After upending the social order and reducing nobles and knights to peasants, the vrakhiri can genuinely be seen as an equalizing force. Life was already poor, nasty, brutish, and short for the peasantfolk. Now that the underclass has expanded to everyone else, peasants rejoice in seeing one-time nobles now play jesters or serve as a moveable feast for the vrakhiri court.  When commoners now play key roles in boyar courts, their loyalties are divided to the point many don’t need to be coerced by fear or propaganda to fight Coen to preserve the new status quo. For the peasants working towards Bakir’s inexhaustible taste for alcohol, or filling barrels full of (former peasant) Ambrus’s blood-fattened leeches, consistent pay beats the hardship of subsistence farming. And when either variation on society treats humanity as livestock to exploit, your choice of farmer can still make a difference. That choice between familiar or supernatural oppression is the moral conflict behind many of Coen’s decisions.  Nevertheless, everyday life continues under the nobles’ shadow. And when you spend time in the communities you encounter, rather than chasing mystical swords, sabotaging boyars, or marshalling allies, it holds weight due to the ongoing countdown to your family’s slaughter. When the game invites you to help your father’s friend Volk--an aged, blind, and disconcertingly horny man--there’s a tangible cost: the lost time. Yet still, I fix his windows and slay the wolves roaming nearby, all for scant reward. When I ask Volk if he needs anything else, the ungrateful codger cheekily tries his luck for a footrub!  The pattern repeats when I pursue a quest in Svartrau City, wherein the only way I can proceed is by spending hours helping nuns tend to the sick and dressing their wounds. These unwanted duties could feel like a waste of time, but I do them gladly. These acts feel earned, like they're more than just RPG busywork; these scenarios replicate communities surviving atrocity through mutual care and favours.  Coen isn’t the only peasant who dreams of taking matters into his own hands. The careless, reckless Manumits share the same intention of taking down the vrakhiri. Drawing their support from the populace, or at least those their rash actions haven’t angered, Crake and his forces don’t easily draw my sympathies or allegiance. For him, peasants are collateral damage. Crake’s win-at-all-costs attitude even tempts him to consider calling for aid from nobles outside the valley. When that act risks recreating the same tyranny that made Brencis the more palatable despot, his ending felt the worst option to me by far--only slightly better than the one in which Coen simply abandons Vale Sangora by leaving the valley forever through a forgotten mountain pass. Dawnwalker’s vampiric metaphor for the ruling classes resonates all the more today, when working-class precarity has heightened to the stage it’s been compared to neo-feudalism: Hypercapitalism extracts labor from our bodies for ever-dwindling rewards, while big tech extracts the expression of our emotions and intellect, just to sell it back to us. Pitted against such overwhelming forces, those old fantasies of joining the elite or effecting change as a benevolent ruler don’t cut it anymore. I’m reminded each time I defeat one of Brencis’s boyars, plunging my fangs into a defeated adversary’s neck, that peasant power is where my class allegiances truly lie. When the monsters and their insatiable greed are real, The Blood of Dawnwalker knows the solution isn’t to join them: It’s to eat the rich.

Silent Hill: Townfall Review - Addressing The TraumaNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: Konami, Annapurna Interactive Developer: Screen Burn Release: September 24, 2026 Rating: Mature Much like the franchise itself, for years Silent Hill existed as an opaque figure embedded in fog, with only crackling radio static confirming its existence. Konami wasn’t sure what to do with it beyond Pyramid Head cameos in kart racers and Bomberman party games. But, like a repressed memory that suddenly manifests as a monster compelling you to address your unresolved trauma, Silent Hill is back, and Townfall, the third Silent Hill game in as many years, continues the impressive return-to-form streak. Simon Ordell’s journey through the Scottish town of St. Amelia in 1996 is not without its shortcomings, but the experience is an affecting one buoyed by the first-person perspective. Silent Hill has dabbled in first-person before with Silent Hill 4: The Room, 2024's experimental The Short Message, and the whole P.T. debacle. We also, of course, all definitely played and remember the on-rails shooter, Silent Hill: The Arcade. Townfall, however, is the first time the franchise has fully embraced the perspective. The view makes the game unique within the Silent Hill oeuvre. Through Simon’s eyes, St. Amelia is overbearing and imposing in all the intended ways. That the game looks great also helps. The claustrophobic interiors are full of details that reward inspection, and you can practically taste the fog while exploring outdoors. The peeking mechanic is also expertly integrated and makes it feel like you truly are cowering and exposing as little of yourself as possible while staying quiet and keeping an eye on monsters.The broad story, at least structurally, will be familiar to Silent Hill players, which I offer as a compliment. Townfall may take place in St. Amelia, but there is no question that this is a Silent Hill game. Simon and others are confronting their inner demons in a foggy town, and what exactly is happening or why the streets are littered with monsters is never truly clear. You do know, however, that something is off and the only way out is through. Uncharacteristically, compared to past Silent Hills, the individual plots that have led to the emotional distress of Townfall’s cast are more transparent. There are fewer questions about why characters feel what they’re feeling. It eliminates some of the dreamlike qualities that make these games ethereal and mysterious, but I am not opposed to more clarity, as sometimes information is withheld unnecessarily. I like the narrative balance Townfall strikes.Simon has an era-appropriate portable television that lets him track monsters through the screen’s static, and it does a good job of letting you monitor creatures’ whereabouts while maintaining intensity. Combat is heavily discouraged, with perhaps the fewest number of bullets in any Silent Hill game (that feature guns, anyway) and melee weapons that expire quickly. As a result, Townfall is a stealth game, but I struggled with understanding what alerted enemies to my presence. Sometimes crouching and moving slowly behind cover was enough. Sometimes enemies saw me anyway. The inconsistency made me reload saves often, which diminished the mood of the encounters. I got particularly frustrated with one invincible, instantly-kill-you-if-caught monster who never seemed to walk away no matter how long I tracked him on my TV while hiding in a closet. In these moments, I was bored and annoyed instead of scared.What I have enjoyed so much about Konami’s renewed efforts with Silent Hill is how it is finding developers like Screen Burn Interactive that understand what makes Silent Hill so intriguing and are managing to produce unique experiences within an admittedly undefined structure. The franchise is about loneliness and trauma and how that affects your emotional state of mind. Townfall presents these emotions well, and my only frustrations arose when I was forced to grapple with some of its unbalanced video game mechanics. Score: 8.25 About Game Informer's review system
