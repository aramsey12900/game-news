Here’s The Great New Tactics Game You Should Be Playing–And I Don’t Mean Fire Emblem Or Zero CompanyNEW
I've been loving Star Wars Zero Company, especially for how it builds on my favorite story arc from The Clone Wars animated series. But whereas I was positive that Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave would b...
I've been loving Star Wars Zero Company, especially for how it builds on my favorite story arc from The Clone Wars animated series. But whereas I was positive that Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave would be the title to finally pull me away from Zero Company, it's another tactics game that has captured my attention: Ex Sanguis. It's no real surprise why I'm enjoying Lightbulb Crew's Ex Sanguis. While it's not a direct sequel to the studio's debut title, Othercide (one of my favorite games of 2020), Ex Sanguis has the same memorable visual style consisting of simple grayscale and striking crimson, a party of heroes who are all elegant women, and turn-based bloodbaths that satisfyingly reward triggering chain events. Ex Sanguis just feels like more Othercide, much in the same way Hollow Knight Silksong and Marvel's Spider-Man 2 feel like extensions to their respective predecessors. Ex Sanguis has enraptured me so completely with how it differs from Othercide, however. Both tactics games have roguelike elements, but Ex Sanguis really leans into that genre's trappings to offer a snappier gameplay loop. You can boot up the game, play for 20-30 minutes, and feel good for accomplishing a few small victories that will benefit your future attempts. That's not really the case for Othercide, which is designed first and foremost as a brutally challenging tactics game. Othercide sees you commanding an army of your daughters. Each day, you send a squad of three on a mission in hopes of unlocking a node at the end of the week, leveling up your units to unlock more permanent upgrades. If one dies, she and her perks are gone for good and the only way to replenish one daughter's health is to sacrifice another. Birthing new daughters costs valuable resources. This culminates in a loop of sacrificing units (some of which you've put a lot of time into) for the good of the mission. It's like playing a game like XCOM or Zero Company with permadeath on and purposively leaning into the tragedy of losing a beloved unit. So while it's very much a tactics game, Othercide has a roguelike feel--from the jump, it's clear that you're not going to do well on your first time trying to unlock a node and face a boss, and you'll need to slowly accrue enough permanent upgrades to enough units to get strong enough to even have a chance at success. And if all your daughters die (which will surely happen more than once), then you're done for and have to start again. Othercide is a long and arduous grind, one soothed by the intriguing lore that twists between each level and drip-feeds answers to the mystery of what your daughters are and why you're hellbent on sacrificing them to carve and shoot your way to whatever is at the end of all this violence. The game is perfect for folks who like to min-max, but even your best-laid plans can fail if the random nature of the changing levels between each failed attempt screws you over while you're trying to build up an ideal team for the boss at the end. And that can be really frustrating, so it's also a game for those with either a ton of time or a lot of patience. Ex Sanguis, meanwhile, leans further into the roguelike formula by streamlining everything to make the overall gameplay easier. Each run is substantially shorter than those in Othercide, so getting to some sort of checkpoint is less intense, and it's easier to say, "Okay, one more run" after a failed attempt. The warriors under your command (they're called stillae, rather than daughters, in this game) heal between each mission, and new stillae spawn in automatically between missions if one falls in battle. And each stillae has a choice of one upgrade from three possible options after every mission instead of two, increasing your chances of replicating your favorite builds (and a limited-use re-roll feature to get rid of any options you absolutely hate raises those chances even more). Ex Sanguis puts a greater onus on completing runs quickly to build up your party and then tackle a boss, while Othercide is all about perfecting how you play a tactics game (and scrapping a run partway through if the randomness of the roguelike elements screw you over on the permadeath mechanic) to have any chance of even holding your own against a boss. Ex Sanguis encourages you to earn a bunch of small victories on your way to each of the big victories, while Othercide is just all about those big victories and being okay with failing a whole bunch between each one.  Structurally, Ex Sanguis still pretty closely copies Othercide, but I'm no longer drowning in despair at 3 AM because I bungled my latest run, lost my strongest daughters, and now I'm basically starting over from square one. And like Othercide, Ex Sanguis is a fantastic tactics game. Time is the major factor in every battle. Each action that one of your stillae can take consumes a set number of points from the total pool they have--moving a great distance costs more points than only a few steps, for example, and weaker, fast attacks tend to be cheaper than stronger ones. If a stillae only uses half her total points on her turn, her turn ends as normal. However, you can command each of your warriors to go beyond her limits and use the second half of her points to perform extra actions. Doing so puts the stillae in a bad way, pushing her next turn further back on the overall timeline of the battle as she needs longer to recover.  Success in a fight is often only rewarded by carefully considering how often to risk pushing any of your stillae in this way, as well as by stacking specific effects together. Certain environmental hazards can trigger explosive chain reactions, for instance, and some stillae can be equipped with abilities that don't act immediately on the overall timeline or only occur if a specific trigger happens, like an enemy wandering into melee range or a nearby stillae reducing a foe's health below a specific threshold. This encourages you to not only think through what you plan on doing next but how each of the various enemies are going to move and attack. It's a complex series of considerations to keep in mind that only grows as new enemy types are introduced and you unlock more stillae abilities. But setting everything up in just the right way to pinball bounce an enemy into oblivion is so deeply satisfying, much in the same way I've adored Creature in the Well and Marvel's Midnight Suns. That isn't to say Ex Sanguis is a universal improvement upon Othercide--for as much as this game improves Othercide's gameplay, it drops the ball on narrative. Othercide drips with a horrific gothic atmosphere. Its dark and foreboding story of a mother desperately attempting to utilize her womanhood to save a broken world suffering under a growing fracture in reality is deliciously cryptic--the type of convoluted mystique that's such a delight for theorists to unravel via soothing narration in a YouTube lore video. You piece the story together through snippets of lore dropped in dialogue and by unlocking codex pages, and even then the final result is up to interpretation. It's a challenging game to understand, and subsequently the difficulty of clawing your way through its gameplay emulates that struggle for understanding. Othercide's vibes are perfect. In comparison, Ex Sanguis doesn't really have a story. There's very little lore provided and no codex to be seen. And while the narrator implies that the stillae are struggling against something, that challenge isn't reflected in the gameplay. I can feel the existence of a narrative, but nothing in the game is focusing it into some sort of recognizable tale. Admittedly, that all could come later. Ex Sanguis launched into early access with only the first three bosses available and an initial handful of difficulty settings (I've done everything in about six hours), so the full game might tell a more interesting story. Regardless, if you like tactics games, you should play Ex Sanguis. Well... you should play Othercide first and wonder how you missed it back in 2020. And then you should play Ex Sanguis--maybe it will have gotten a story update by then!

Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave Review - Let The Games BeginNEW
Reviewed on: Switch 2 ...
Reviewed on: Switch 2 Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Intelligent Systems Release: September 17, 2026 Fire Emblem’s scale has been escalating for some time. From three Fates, to Three Houses, to numerous rings and now, a massive three-act entry for the Switch 2. Those ever-escalating aspirations have led to impressive new heights and ballooning scope. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave is easily one of my favorite settings and stories in the series' history, with memorable battles and characters, even when repetition and overabundance weigh it down.Fortune's Weave starts at the end of all things. After a quick character creator for a mysterious Pale Raven and an in medias res run through a world crumbling and haunted by the living dead, the player's avatar and Fortuna – a goddess of fate – are some of the only survivors. To save the world, they'll need to defeat the encroaching lord of the underworld, Balor, and they'll need help to do it.So, you retire to a chamber outside of time and zip back through history to the lives of four Flame Lords of Dagda: Cai, Theodora, Dietrich, and Leda. All have found their way to this continent's capital in Dagsion, where Divine Emperor Solel is hosting the Heroic Games, with a promise that any wish will be granted to the ultimate victor. Correct the causality of these fallen heroes, and they may survive and rally under your banner in the changed future for the final fight against Balor. You could go challenge the big bad at any moment, but the more lords you assemble, the easier time you'll have. Showing up ready for the finale probably means bringing at least two lords, and their respective recruits, to the final campaign, and in my case, I wanted to get as many through as I could.Fortune's Weave takes on a three-part structure, with each lord getting an individual story through the Heroic Games. They each have their own reasons for fighting: the young Cai wishes to rescue his father from prison and imminent execution; Saraminian queen Theodora wants to reclaim land important to her people; songstress Leda has murder and revenge against her father's killer on her mind; and Dietrich is just in it for the love of battle.It's a little overwrought to explain, but it lends itself well to the size of the tale Fortune's Weave is telling. All four face trials and strife throughout the course of the games. Faustian bargains, shadowy organizations plotting in the background, and old secrets brought to light by the influx of every interesting and notable person in Dagda to its capital. It's not required to play any of the routes, as you could go fight Balor on your own if you wish. But each lord's story adds more context, nuance, and character to this huge tale. I loved moments where I'd see Dietrich and his spell-casting pal Fabio mention something during Cai's path, and then get to see the events from their perspective when I played through them in Dietrich's.Playing through each route is a big task, though. They're made up of chapters, which each have a big battle or two, with free time baked in for you to spend as you'd like in and outside of the capital. The scope starts small, with shared meals to build rapport within your unit, recruiting others to your team, or training skills at the arena. It soon expands out to encompass the entire continent, though, as you can roam around, fight overworld battles, explore dungeons, and use lord-specific mechanics like Theodora's battalion recruitment or Leda's tavern performances to gain resources and power.This is where Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave starts to weigh itself down. Dungeon exploration, a returning mechanic from Echoes: Shadows of Valentia, turns Fire Emblem into a classic RPG dungeon-crawl with a small party, strike-to-engage exploration, and turn-based combat. It feels like a microcosm of big strategy battles, only they get repetitive and too frequent; I engaged with them for the first lord's route, but moved to auto-battle soon after, as I was just going through the same motions for each fight in every dungeon.Free time was most interesting when I was pressed against the clock to finish certain quests, acquire resources, and resolve paralogues before the deadline, but keeping up with the numerous weekly activities and checking in at home for any random events – often only signified by an inconspicuous ripple on my map in Dagsion – meant I was usually just going through the motions. During some periods I'd be stressed out, trying to build up support levels for a new Archer recruit while finishing enough side quests to get the renown level I'd need to get them on my team for a big battle with flying foes coming up. Other cycles, I was just in a loop: train, eat, auto-activities, repeat.When it finally comes back to the bread-and-butter strategy of Fire Emblem, Fortune's Weave is excellent. Overworld map fights are fairly straightforward, but the bookending fights of chapters are highlights, especially when they take place in Dagsion's arena. Developer Intelligent Systems concocted some brilliant coliseum battles that have all the theme and flair you'd hope for: charging chariots, puzzling mechanisms, and special combat arenas that push you to utilize your units as best you can.There's no shortage of options for combat, as each unit archetype can go down interesting upgrade lines. In Leda's path, I had the aspiring mercenary Mu train up into a deadly sword-wielder who could cut through anything with a strong critical strike. In Cai's path, I secretly trained the lord in magic alongside one of his childhood friends, Thialla, so he could become a spell-slinging mounted Caldarius for the final fights of the Heroic Games. Fortune's Weave eschews the weapon triangle to place more emphasis on what weapons can handle individually, like magic against armor or lances against horses, and a greater emphasis on Combat Arts makes training within a fighter's specific strengths rewarding. Lords also pack their own Blaze Arts, like Leda's spellsongs or Dietrich's shadowstep, giving them fun tools to enable strategies and close out maps.The opening act is great, and yet somehow only one part of the pie, as Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave still has two more acts to play out. After leading a lord to the end of their path in the Heroic Games, a second section unlocks, exploring their story in a war that breaks out not long after the Games' conclusion. See them through that, and the lord – and any units they recruited – can join you for the final campaign against Balor, which has its own cycle of days, activities, and an extra layer of grand strategy over the top. Pull it all together, and Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave is weighty to see through to the end; it took me over 130 hours to see credits, and I still missed some scenes and events. The payoffs are excellent, and certain story beats getting set up in Part One and paid off in Part Three were incredible. But doing so requires a lot of playtime, investment, and repetition. Dungeons never get more interesting or challenging, collecting specific resources can feel cumbersome, and even exploring the map and managing units starts to slow Fortune's Weave down in its final hours.What kept me going was the world and its characters. The Romanesque vision of gladiatorial combat, the plucking strings of vihuelas, and a world where gods walk among men all kept me enthralled, start to finish. The story of Fortune's Weave starts small and grows massive over its runtime, but at its heart, this Fire Emblem asks what our mortality means for us. How do we build our legacies and histories, build up for our future generations, and what do we live for with our allotted time? And what is worth defending and protecting with that time we've been given?Fortune's Weave has an amazing soundtrack and gorgeous visual designs for its cast and the various classes they can take on. Coliseum fights never failed to excite, and some of them now rank among my favorite all-time Fire Emblem maps. Warriors like Fabio, Lilian, Mu, Anatolia, Dante, Talimun, and Orchel all grew on me through their support and banter, or just developed their own emergent stories in battle.Support conversations and side quests dig deep into the world's lore, as well. In Theodora's route, characters explored the difficulty of abiding by their patron god Yu Phas' command to never write down their histories, and whether adhering to their long-held practices would cause them to erode over time. Leda grapples with the vices of indulging revenge and what music can bring to those who have lost as much as she has, while Dietrich stumbles into fascinating bits about the wider world and its long-held mysteries.The scope of Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave allows it to take massive swings at surprising, cool concepts and mechanics that feel fresh and interesting for the series. Packing them all into a single game might have made this the most intimidating entry to jump into since Fates, but putting it all in a single package with some excellent quality-of-life options, like cross-path progression boosts and side quest clears, helps smooth out the road for those who want to see everything. I've no doubt that those who crave a slow-burn epic and can stomach the repetition for the payoffs will love this world and its cast of characters. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave will easily eat up all your time for months to come, and it's worthwhile to see these Heroic Games through to their conclusion.
