Everything New In Wardogs Season 2NEW
Wardogs is about to get shaken up with Season 2. The tactical shooter will get a fresh arsenal, new vehicles, a round of balance changes, and more ways to fortify the battlefield. The update will b...
Wardogs is about to get shaken up with Season 2. The tactical shooter will get a fresh arsenal, new vehicles, a round of balance changes, and more ways to fortify the battlefield. The update will be available at 9:30 AM UTC on October 15, with the community already discussing its potential impact. Some players have been enthusiastic about the scale of the changes, although the new economy and logistics adjustments have raised concerns among players who rely more heavily on those systems. Here are the 10 changes that will have the biggest impact on Wardogs, followed by everything new in Wardogs Season 2. Blue Team Bribery Wardogs is trying something unusual to solve a problem money alone can't usually fix: bribing players to choose the team they don't want to play. Lonestar--or the blue team--has struggled with lower win rates and earned itself quite the reputation as the cursed faction in the game. Bulkhead says that could be down to uniform visibility or spawn locations, but weird player superstitions are probably the real reason people avoid the team. Season 2 introduces extra cash and XP for Lonestar victories, plus additional rewards for certain teamplay actions. Players on any faction that's at least 40 points behind the team in second place will also receive a 20% vendor discount to encourage them to equip their best loadouts and try to recoup the loss. This is being treated as a Season 2 experiment rather than a permanent balance solution, but it remains to be seen whether the rewards are enough to change players' preference. Four New Weapons Season 2 adds four new weapons, giving players more options across multiple combat roles: TCX-SPR: An assault rifle chambered in 7.62x39mm. PP-19 Bizon: A 9x19mm submachine gun. Evo-3 Ultra: A .308 Winchester light machine gun. M14: A .308 Winchester marksman rifle. The new weapons arrive alongside additional optics, magazines, muzzles, and bipods. They also tie into the revised progression system, so players will have new equipment to work towards as they level up. With weapon damage, recoil, and suppression also being reworked, the effectiveness of different weapon classes could shift considerably throughout Season 2. Four New Vehicles The roster of available vehicles is expanding too, with four new options designed for different jobs: Wolf-110: A fast, lightweight transport. Wolf-110 with M2 Browning: An armed version with a heavy machine gun. T-72B3: A battle tank that's slower than the L2A6, but cheaper and more fuel efficient. Flakpanzer Gepard: A dedicated anti-aircraft vehicle. The new vehicles give drivers more choices before unlocking the game's more expensive hardware. The T-72B3 offers a different cost-versus-performance trade-off, while the Gepard adds another specialist option for dealing with airborne threats. Vehicle progression has been adjusted, with the Wolf-110, Wolf-110 with M2 Browning, T-72B3, and Gepard appearing in the Driver track at levels 8, 16, 20, and 25 respectively. Rain Is Important The new rain weather preset is one of the more interesting changes because it affects far more than just the scenery. Rain visibly wets characters, vehicles, and surfaces. Its sound changes depending on whether you're outside, indoors, in a vehicle, or under cover. If a building's roof is destroyed, rain can fall through the opening. GTA 6 who? The tactical consequences are even more interesting. Rain reduces spotting range and weakens Molotov flames. During heavier weather, thunder is synchronized across the server, with everyone hearing the same strike from the same direction. Thunder can mask footsteps, potentially giving players a brief window to reposition or approach without being heard. A storm could become an active part of your tactical approach rather than just a dramatic overhaul. New Meta It might sound like we're stating the obvious here, but with Bulkhead making significant adjustments to damage across weapon classes, the meta will shift more dramatically than in the new season changes you're used to in other tactical shooters. The aim of the overhaul is to give each category a clearer purpose. High-caliber ammunition was delivering too much damage in some assault rifles, DMRs, and light machine guns. Those weapons could get close to sniper-rifle levels of damage while firing much faster. The new base damage reductions are: .308 Winchester: Down 25% 7.62x54mmR: Down 20% 7.62x39mm: Down 19% .50 Cal: Down 16% Assault rifles will also deal less damage to the body, while LMGs will match assault-rifle body damage and rely on sustained fire and larger magazines more. DMR damage has been adjusted too, while sniper rifles receive increased hit location damage to ensure they're still a viable choice. Progression Is Much Faster Grinding a role was a major commitment in Season 1. Bulkhead says most players stalled between levels 10 and 20 before reaching the most valuable equipment for each role. Season 2 dramatically cuts the XP required for those levels. Level 10 will require 29% less XP, level 15 will require 50% less, and level 20 will require 55% less XP. That means you'll only need 111,500 XP to reach level 20 instead of 250,000. The highest listed role track level, 50, is around 23% less expensive in terms of XP. Unlocks have been shuffled across all of the role tracks. Some equipment arrives earlier, while other weapons or attachments move to different roles or become more expensive. The result is a less punishing grind for players who want to experiment with different roles, although not every unlock is getting easier. The full list of changes is detailed at the bottom of this article. Building Is Getting More Expensive If you're anything like me, as soon as a match starts turning against my team, I become a builder. There's something satisfying about hammering away at structures under the guise of "fortifying the base" when actually I'm just hiding from the bullies on the battlefield that keep wiping out our uncoordinated team. However, some of the structures were getting utterly ridiculous, and Bulkhead says it wants them to feel more like considered investments than items players can spam without much thought. Yet it also dips deep into the series' wealth of mechanics and themes – wingmen, huge mission areas, memorable rivals – to forge what feels like the ace of aces, and one of the series' best entries.In the wake of an invasion of your homeland, you go down in a dogfight with Sotoa's elite Shadow squadron alongside the "Wings of Theve," a folk hero for the FCU that you discover is a little more tall tale than living legend. You don his identity and become the new "Wings of Theve" to help keep the war effort alive. Though Ace Combat is certainly no stranger to stories about war, politics, and their effect on the world at large, Wings of Theve takes it to a new height thanks to its focus on your wingmen and the crew of your carrier, the Endurance. Over 30 missions, you build a small band of surviving fighters aboard your meager fleet, all hoping to make it to the conflict's resolution. From your Joker Squadron and sister Queen Squadron to enemy aces and crew aboard the Endurance, the cast is both packed and memorable.Though there are still some odd story wrinkles, specifically when asking for an arbitrary button press to advance the scene, and the voice of the former Wings pilot Cope speaking to you from the beyond lays on a little thick at the start, Ace Combat 8's story soars thanks to its new first-person perspective and focus on the squadron around you and other members of the Endurance. Big moments in missions hit that much harder when you've spent time around these pilots, seen their struggles up close, and then have to desperately battle alongside them for your life in the skies above. Each mission is a brilliantly laid-out box of objectives and tasks, an impressive feat given the variety of what Bandai's Aces team asks you to accomplish. In one, you soar across a vast desert, taking out strategic reserves of power, ammo, and hangars full of planes to whittle down an invasion force's resources. In another, you jet toward an island at high speeds and low altitudes to avoid radar detection; then, several missions later, you're trailing massive cargo planes in the sky, following their contrails because radar jamming masks their exact position.A wealth of planes and weapons are available, and all feel fantastic to fly. The jets feel a little heavier than in Ace Combat 7, but it's a good heft; in the midst of a high-G turn, it appropriately feels like you're wresting the nose of a giant mass of steel at sonic speeds. You can also pack two special weapons at a time, which lets you specialize more and opens up more options in your hangar for each outing. I still found myself beelining straight for the F-35C, but enjoyed several of the other options as well; one special section involving an A-10 bomber was a particularly fun break from the whiplash-inducing missile rodeo of other missions. It's not just missiles the enemy will throw at you, either. Get used to hearing "Rail Gun Warning" in your headphones, as Sotoa's fleet of land battleships and other terra-naval units make ground forces especially troublesome in Ace Combat 8. Sotoa's Lux Lunae, or the Shadows as your squad calls them, are also a highlight. From their sleek black paint jobs and open-comms chanting as they enter the battlefield to their mid-air maneuvers, the Shadows continually push you to the edge and beyond. Besting the Lux Lunae's aces was a consistent source of improvement and achievement as a virtual pilot.Multiplayer pushes even further, with modes for those who just like to chase high scores and those who want to dogfight other human pilots. Modes where your four-flier squad compete for score, either alongside or against an enemy squadron, make up the bulk of multiplayer playtime. The star attraction is the emergency sorties: special missions that fire up every so often for all players, pitting them against Ace Combat superweapons like the Arsenal Bird. It's a solid base with room to grow for future support, though a lot of menuing, grinding for specific plane upgrade needs, and cosmetic monetization options put speed bumps on my playtime more often than I'd have liked.Ace Combat 8: Wings of Theve perfectly represents why fliers have stuck with this series. A fantastic campaign, filled with missions I want to replay over and over again, is buoyed by a compelling cast and story, excellent flight controls, explosive dogfights, and encounters you can't wait to scream through at supersonic speeds. The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) Preview - A Beautiful Step Back In TimeNEW

Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Nintendo Release: November 5, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ For nearly 20 years, The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time was my favorite game. While it was eventually supplanted by The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, Ocarina of Time remains a formative, treasured gaming experience that laid the foundation for the wider 3D action genre. However, I am the first to admit that while the original Nintendo 64 version – the only one available on Switch and Switch 2 – has aged better than most games of its era, much like its iconic hero, it doesn't take long to realize it's from a bygone era. For years, I wanted Nintendo to bring the 2011 3D remaster to Switch, but it's doing me one better: a full, from-the-ground-up remake with lush visuals, several quality-of-life improvements, and even voice-acted cutscenes.  We are just weeks away from the launch of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time on Nintendo Switch 2, and ahead of its November 5 release, I spent over an hour exploring Hyrule as both Young and Adult Link. My time begins the moment I take control of Link after Navi wakes him up. Following a brief conversation with Navi and Saria, who also ran up to Link's treehouse, it's time to retrieve the Deku Shield and Kokiri Sword so Mido will let me past to see the Great Deku Tree. As increasingly endearing as Saria is thanks to her improved facial and body animations, as well as her more fleshed-out dialogue, Mido is just as increasingly obnoxious; I was definitely happy to make him eat his words after he mocked Link for previously being the only one without a fairy.Running around Kokiri Forest is nearly enough to sell me on this remake. The visuals are absolutely stunning. The new jump button and ability to sprint out of a roll make it feel like an all-new game. The freely controlled camera adds to this feeling, even with some relatively basic level design. And while I never had a problem with how Young Link looks in this remake, I'm even more impressed and comforted by seeing him in action in the actual game. In fact, everything surrounding Link also looks great; Kokiri Forest is much more bustling than its previous forms, light fog dissipates as you run through it, and swinging your sword looks better than ever before. Though I love Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom's stylish anime-inspired art style, the sheer amount of detail present in each and every character, enemy, and environment accentuates the masterfully executed maximalist approach Nintendo took with this remake. After finding 40 rupees to buy a Deku Shield, I crawl through the tunnel to the area where you get the Kokiri Sword. This time, however, there's added verticality as you dodge the rolling boulders to reach a ladder, which you climb and then jump across a gap to reach the chest that contains the blade. This is indicative of small changes dedicated fans will find scattered throughout; I didn't uncover anything monumentally different from the original game, but eagle-eyed viewers of last month's Nintendo Direct will notice that Lon Lon Ranch is now on level with the rest of Hyrule Field rather than up on a plateau, and that Kakariko Village is now slightly rearranged. Outside of some small upgrades to the diversity and placement of foliage, I didn't notice many more differences, but it's also been several years since I last fired up either the N64 or 3DS version of Ocarina of Time, so it's entirely likely they were subtle enough that I didn't notice. After entering the Great Deku Tree and completing the first part where you jump off the ledge and break through the spiderweb, I must jump to the next part of the demo. Still as Young Link, I watch as Saria gifts Link the Fairy Ocarina through a touching, fully voiced (outside of Link, obviously) cutscene. From there, I'm dumped into Hyrule Field. It doesn't feel nearly as impressively endless as it felt in 1998, but it is definitely way more beautiful. After a brief conversation with the owl, Kaepora Gaebora, I sprint across Hyrule Field, catching passing yet impressive glimpses of landmarks like Lon Lon Ranch and Death Mountain. One new addition is apparent as I navigate the menus: The Threads of Time. Here, a tapestry tells the story of the Hero of Time in real time; I can look back at what has already happened in Link's adventure, as well as see how much is still on the horizon. Additionally, if you get lost, the tapestry can give hints for where you're supposed to go. After a brief exploration of Hyrule Castle Town, including a short tour of the Temple of Time, it's time to jump ahead to the final segment of my demo: the Forest Temple. This section lets me try out Adult Link, who is equipped with the hookshot, and later, the Fairy Bow. This was the one place where my memory felt fuzziest, as I struggled to speedrun the puzzles that I last played through more than a decade ago.Fighting Wolfos, Stalfos, and Skulltulas is more straightforward than most modern third-person fights, which may be the one area that fell slightly short of my admittedly lofty expectations. Using the lock-on targeting allows Link to dodge and counterattack, but I didn't find a simple way to swap targets when you're fighting multiple enemies at once. Instead, I had to release the left trigger, move the camera, and squeeze the trigger again. I get that this is how it worked in the original game, but I would have liked switching to the next target without going through that. Maybe there is an easy way to accomplish that, but I didn't find it in my nearly 90 minutes fighting through Hyrule. Regardless of that one reservation, there is no doubt in my mind that The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time will consume my life when it comes out. And with October already halfway in the rearview, we really don't have long before a whole new generation gets to experience what could very well be Link's greatest adventure. This remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time comes to Switch 2 on November 5.

Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S ...
Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: Konami Developer: Konami, Evil Empire Release: October 15, 2026 Rating: Teen Castlevania has been a dormant franchise for years, but Belmont's Curse revives the series with fiery passion. There are hundreds of indies inspired by Konami's gothic vampire-hunting games, putting a lot of pressure on the publisher to live up to its far-reaching legacy. Konami's surprising, yet effective solution is to bring in a new developer: Evil Empire, best known for its work on the Castlevania-inspired Dead Cells. The resulting game has a fresh perspective, a brand-new protagonist, and whip-smart execution. Rose Belmont and her father Trevor have been summoned to Paris, a city under supernatural siege. Rose is eager to prove her worth, partially to impress her father, who killed Dracula 23 years ago, and partially because she wants revenge for her bewitched arm, a curse from Dracula, passed down from her late mother. It's a solid premise, and while the narrative is not particularly complex, Rose's character arc has standout moments that will stick with me.For newcomers, Belmont's Curse stands alone perfectly fine, though it is technically a sequel to Castlevania III, which the first two seasons of Netflix's Castlevania are based on. Arguably, it's a better sequel to the show than the game; the art style and character designs have clear anime inspirations, and some voice actors reprise their roles. I enjoyed seeing them in a new light, especially as they passed the torch to Rose. The best example of this torch-passing is a deck of tarot cards, gifted to Rose by her late mother, Sypha. It's my favorite mechanic in Belmont's Curse. Each is a specially crafted vessel to hold the power of Paris' most powerful figures, friend or foe. As you approach each target, Rose will gather clues from her surroundings to aid in hunting them down or besting them in battle. This investigation ranges from just-okay, linear moments in the early game to cryptic, map-spanning missions in the late game. Creatively designed quests to chase down the last handful of tarot cards were my favorite moments in the whole game.Once you find your targets, you absorb their souls into a tarot card, allowing Rose to speak with them. What starts as a lonely journey becomes almost like a road trip, as Rose has a packed backseat of antagonists and allies who can chime in whenever they see fit.Each card also unlocks a spell, ranging from fireballs and a magic cross to a blast turning foes to stone. They also come with a list of Works of Mercy: achievements that upgrade your spells upon completion. These range from "Use This Spell 20 Times" to "Kill 44 Skeletons" or "Find All The Rooms In The Clock Tower." It provides a fun reason to use abilities you might not otherwise, which is especially nice when you become overwhelmed by options in the game's closing hours. I was swayed on more than one spell after achievement-hunting for upgrades. Speaking of combat, the action in Belmont's Curse is magnificent. Rose is light on her feet and only becomes lighter as she gains new abilities, becoming a whirlwind of death by the game's conclusion. Between six weapon categories, a variety of perk-granting relics, and your deck of cards, players are flooded with choice in how to approach encounters. I constantly switched up my loadout, often mid-fight, to adapt to the enemies that appeared. Fights were tough enough to keep me on my toes but never insurmountable, and a forgiving checkpoint system keeps repeated deaths from becoming frustrating. Inventive and intense boss battles against Dracula's coterie of minions are also a major highlight.After 16 hours, I ended my playthrough with 92 percent completion. I just couldn't resist doubling back to earlier zones to see the new areas I could access with my newfound traversal abilities, and Belmont's Curse never failed to reward me. Between two dozen weapons to find, upgrades to health and mana, and even some huge, optional areas, I'm excited for the satisfaction that awaits me as I dive back in to reach 100 percent.Belmont's Curse is a strong reminder of why the Castlevania series is so influential, with a rich world, tense combat, and a map chock-full of secrets. While this latest entry certainly pays homage to the Castlevanias of yore, it also breathes fresh life into the franchise, making me excited for its future.
