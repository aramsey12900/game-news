The Best Tabletop Role-Playing Games To Play That Aren’t Dungeons & DragonsNEW
Tabletop role-playing games have reached an all-time high in popularity. Turns out, there’s a certain appeal about rounding up a group of friends together to go through an adventure while chucking dic...
Tabletop role-playing games have reached an all-time high in popularity. Turns out, there’s a certain appeal about rounding up a group of friends together to go through an adventure while chucking dice in the process. For over half a century, the juggernaut of the genre has been none other than Dungeons & Dragons, designed by Gary Gygax and made super-popular through Critical Role, Stranger Things, multiple feature films, and countless other media. While the latest iteration of the fantastic game might be the best it's been in years, you're missing out if you haven't stopped to consider its alternatives. Whether you're looking for a lighter rules systems, different settings, or an entirely different concept, there has never been a better time to explore other RPG systems. Here’s a quick rundown of a few of the best out there. Mork Borg Have you ever felt that D&D was getting a bit too cozy? Maybe the sometimes-crunchy rules for combat drag down the flow of the game. Maybe you just want to roll up a complete scumbag of a character and D&D’s ruleset don’t really facilitate that. Well, Mork Borg just might be for you! A rules-lite system that owes more to the Old School Renaissance (OSR) movement of RPG design that aims to recreate rules similar to the original editions of D&D, Mork Borg puts a unique spin on setting and atmosphere. Inspired by the extreme tone of Doom Metal album covers and music, the game has players roll up a scumbag of a character with little hope of surviving in a doomed plane. I'd highly recommend it to fans of the dark fantasy genre, Darkest Dungeon, and other grim games. However, if fantasy's not your bag, give its spin-off Cy_Borg a try, as it amps up the violence and chaos of a game like Cyberpunk 2077 to 11. AMC’s The Walking Dead: The Role-Playing Game Okay, hear me out. I know the years haven’t been kind to The Walking Dead television series or its ungodly amount of spin-offs, but I found myself thoroughly impressed with this rule system. Rather than try to adapt stories of the show, this game puts players into the world of it and tasks them with survival. The game also uses a unique dice system that makes success rolls harder to obtain the more risks you take, and features a great mechanic for tracking how big and dangerous the walker horde is. If you’re a fan of zombie games, The Last of Us, or games where you’re forced to make hard decisions, this game just might surprise you, too. Dread What if you don’t want to use dice at all? What if your success was based on your physical dexterity and you were able to pull off impossible tasks because you were sure you could pull it off… or watch it all come crashing down. That’s the concept behind Dread, a game that ditches dice chucking entirely and has players use a tower of Jenga blocks for success checks. Designed for bleak horror settings, Dread uses its tower to great effect, adding a physical skill-based element to role-playing that can’t quite be recreated with math modifiers. The fact the game also allows for horror storytelling in any setting means you’re surely able to find a variant to you and your playgroup's liking. This is a fun one to play around Halloween and at parties. The One Ring (and, to an extent, The Lord of the Rings 5E) Let's be frank: J.R.R. Tolkien crafted quite possibly the greatest fantasy setting ever when he created Middle-earth for his novels The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings. They’re timeless, they have amazing films, and they’re pop-culture mainstays. So they’re primed for a TTRPG, and thankfully, an amazing one exists. The One Ring is a role-playing take on Tolkien’s universe that stays true to the lore and world that he created. This means that unlike D&D, there’s no “superpowered” player characters or copious uses of magic, but instead very thoughtful and almost cozy atmospheric storytelling that is sure to please any fan of Middle-earth. Hell, one of the starter sets even has players take on the role of Hobbits in The Shire doing the tasks that are associated with the halfling creatures. For those who truly don’t want to ditch the D&D 5E system, there’s also a conversion of The One Ring into the rules system that’s titled Lord of the Rings 5E, which is a fantastic supplement (and the one that hits my table the most). Final Fantasy XIV TTRPG Who would’ve thought that a tabletop RPG based on one of the coolest MMORPGs to ever exist would be so good? Go figure. What makes the tabletop adaptation of Final Fantasy XIV stand out though is its fantastic adaptation of XIV's iconic job system, which cranks up role-playing classes to the max. Dungeon exploring while using the various jobs from Final Fantasy are sure to please any gamer and mix things up a lot from your usual fare of fighters, wizards, rogues, etc. Players who tried D&D will also be able to jump in pretty easily as the game uses a standard d20 system.

Suliman, Exodus' Bad Boy Companion, Is Intentionally 'Self-Important And Arrogant'NEW
Exodus is an ambitious sci-fi RPG from Archetype Entertainment that shares some DNA with Mass Effect. It's evident if you know the studio's history (many of its higher-ranking developers used...
Exodus is an ambitious sci-fi RPG from Archetype Entertainment that shares some DNA with Mass Effect. It's evident if you know the studio's history (many of its higher-ranking developers used to work at BioWare), but you can also tell just by looking; it's nearly impossible to make a story-driven sci-fi game without those comparisons being made. One of the easiest parallels comes with Exodus' companions, and when I played the game at Archetype's Austin offices, the dark-haired companion Suliman left quite an impression. "Suliman is one of the leaders of the Tenjin Dynasty, which is a very powerful faction on Lidon," Exodus narrative director Drew Karpyshyn told me. "The thing about the Tenjin Dynasty is they are very closely tied in with the Awakened. Suliman [...] has a special relationship with them, and is basically a master assassin and spy who you need to ally with to help you."Awakened animals are essentially larger, smarter, longer-living versions of their standard Earth counterparts. Each has a translator chip so humans can understand them. Suliman is the human representative of a Dynasty that is mostly Awakened, and you need his help in an early mission. As such, he's generally accompanied by ravens, who have a knack for spying and collecting sensitive information."Suliman was essentially – we don't want to spoil too much of his backstory – raised by the ravens, because they saw him as a useful tool," Karpyshyn said. "[Suliman] became sort of the face of the Dynasty to make it easier to work with humans. Him being smart and intelligent, he has learned to leverage his position to become more than just a figurehead and become an actual important player in the dynasty itself." Suliman is confident, with dark hair, a pretty-boy vibe (the developers mention they used some K-Pop celebrities as a reference), and a know-it-all attitude, probably because intel from his raven companions means he does know quite a bit. He speaks to everyone as if he's the one in charge, unconcerned with the opinions of others and completely self-assured in his position. He's not quite rude, though, and even though he's not really my cup of tea, game director Chris King says they adjusted Suliman over time to be a little less abrasive."It's [a] case where, especially with the Dynasty characters, we were experimenting with a little bit of like, you don't necessarily want to like them right off the bat, and you're kind of doing this just because of the job at hand," King said. "At one point, he was kind of like a rich, spoiled kid that you were meant to hate. But we sort of softened that down a little bit, I would say. And still, he's maybe a little off-putting when you first meet him. But I think as you get to develop that relationship with him further, people start to like him."Karpyshyn adds, "Yeah, He's pretty self-important and arrogant. But then once you see him in a level and you see his abilities, you're like, 'Oh, okay, yeah, he walks the walk, doesn't just talk the talk.'" I had this exact experience in my demo. Suliman guides Jun, the protagonist, on a mission to Khonsu, a space station that orbits your homeworld, Lidon. It's a dangerous situation, as the orbital platform is crawling with both enemy troops and Ghosts (essentially ancient security robots), but Suliman makes the whole ordeal much easier. And that's especially true if you're trying to move stealthily.Combat in Exodus has you play as Jun to shoot enemies in third-person, but pressing one of the bumpers allows you to command your companions as well. Suliman's basic ability, Shadow Step, has him turn invisible and essentially teleport behind an enemy to take them out. It reminds me of the recruits in Assassin's Creed Brotherhood and Revelations, where you can tap a button and summon an ally from a nearby bush to take out an opponent. Suliman spawns in, deals a bunch of damage, and retreats without being seen. Meanwhile, his Ultimate Ability, Vendetta, has him deal damage to three enemies and apply a corrosive effect. You can use that to trigger a synergy, one of the main ways you interact with companions in combat, by throwing a thruster grenade and spreading the corrosion even farther. "If you're not into stealth, Suliman still comes in handy though," King tells me. "All [of Suliman's moves] are valid in normal, regular, action combat, but I think that's sort of his area of expertise. [...] If you like sneaking around, he can really get you some quick kills off the bat."I will likely not romance Suliman (though that is an option), but I wouldn't be surprised if he finds his way into my regular adventuring party purely due to his cool combat abilities. I also can't help but sympathize with his empathy for Awakened – it's hard to side against a guy telling people not to point guns at giant frogs, my favorite faction in the game.Exodus launches next year on PS5, Xbox Series X/S, and PC. Blood Dungeon Is A Survivors-Like For People Who Don’t Like Vampire SurvivorsNEW
Not everybody bought into Vampire Survivors. The 2021 game popularized the idea of "bullet heaven," a reversal of elements normally seen in "bullet hell" games, and made such a huge impact on the indu...
Not everybody bought into Vampire Survivors. The 2021 game popularized the idea of "bullet heaven," a reversal of elements normally seen in "bullet hell" games, and made such a huge impact on the industry that some call this subgenre "Survivors-likes." But while Vampire Survivors was a giant leap, it wasn't a wholly satisfying one for some. Blood Dungeon, a new "bullet hell" from Nidhogg and Wheel World developer Messhof, runs with some of the ideas that made Survivors a hit, fleshing them out and helping define the future of the genre Vampire Survivors sired. The chief hallmarks of a "Survivors-like" are automatic combat, hordes of enemies that get stronger with every wave, and the kind of randomized upgrades found in roguelikes. You see it in Deep Rock Galactic: Survivor, Brotato, and 20 Minutes Till Dawn, and there's even a Breakout-inspired brick-busting riff called Ball X Pit. The RPG-style progression acts like a hook to keep you playing, but the fundamental passivity of their indirect combat, and the inherent unpredictability and impermanence of those upgrades, can be off-putting. And Vampire Survivors itself has an aesthetic that overtly evokes Castlevania, with very similar artwork and environments, which makes it look a little uninspired. Sorry, pal. But you're never beating the Castlevania accusations. I know not everybody bought into Vampire Survivors, because I didn't really myself. I had a fixation with it for a short spell in 2022, and still find it preferable to many of the games inspired by it, but ultimately it's about as hollow as its biggest critics allege. If any game can be called a "walking simulator," it's this one, as walking is all you do--moving left, right, up, and down, with no tactility or physical friction--just endless swarms of monsters desperate to mulch you up as you glide around its wide-open, top-down levels. Its whole personality feels canned, prefab, as interchangeable as its Konami-aping art. Good for its developer that they struck gold with their work, but it never really hooked me, personally. But Blood Dungeon has hooked me, and deep. It's been all I've played for a couple of weeks now, without it even starting to get old. Blood Dungeon shows that, with a little bit of ingenuity, a strong identity, and a sense of physicality, a Survivors-like can be more than just a self-operating machine inside your TV that you barely engage with. Instead of trudging around with only enemies providing friction, Blood Dungeon drops this style of auto-combat into a traditional platformer. You might not have to press a button to trigger your attacks, but you do have to tap to jump from ledge to ledge. This kind of platforming is crucial not just for navigating the game's Pac-Man-homage mazes, but for avoiding the enemies that are constantly rushing toward you.  Satyrs and giant worms coexist in this hellishly fun time. By making motion a little more complicated Messhof makes Blood Dungeon more interactive than the almost purely reactive play this genre's known for. Add in extra wrinkles, like free-climbing on any vertical surface, and watery sections with a depleting oxygen bar, and you've got a game that's simply more interesting to play than most bullet heavens. The most important thing elevating Blood Dungeon, though, is its personality, as relayed by its art style and sense of humor. The game bears a hand-drawn aesthetic, as if these are doodles in a middle schooler's notebook come to life, with characters ripped from sources that a 12-year-old would be familiar with--nature, pop culture, and mythology, to name the most common inspirations. It's a game where sirens and centaurs coexist with mummies, vampires, rats, and big rushing dudes in helmets who look a little like football players if you squint your eyes.  The sense of being hand-made by a would-be tween delinquent with heavy metal posters on his bedroom wall courses throughout Blood Dungeon. At least two of the playable characters are always fully nude. One unlockable character is an ominous red-cloaked skeleton whose greatest weapon is lethal farts--complete with a loud BRRP every time he lets one rip. (I honestly turn the volume down when I use that weapon, because c'mon, I don't want to hear that every couple of seconds.) It doesn't rely on an instantly recognizable homage as shorthand, but creates its own whimsical style that perfectly fits the seventh-grade goofiness of the name Blood Dungeon. And that makes it as much of a joy to watch as it does play. How could you not want to play a game with this guy in it? Even if you don't necessarily like Vampire Survivors or the games inspired by it, you shouldn't dismiss Blood Dungeon. It does something games in this genre often don't: It gives you reasons to play beyond simply watching the numbers go up. It's resolutely focused on establishing its own well-defined voice, which gives it an unmistakable humanity not always found among Vampire Survivors' imitators. And in these increasingly artificial times, it's that humanity that I appreciate the most about Blood Dungeon.

It’s been a busy year for games so far. A new Nintendo console just launched. I haven’t yet beaten Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 or Doom: The Dark Ages, despite truly loving what I’ve played, and I’m at least three games behind on the new releases I want to check out. And yet, these past few weeks, whenever I’ve sat down to play a game in my spare time, it’s been 2017’s The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild.Now, outside of it being my favorite game of all time, it also recently received a big performance upgrade through the Nintendo Switch 2 version, making for an improved experience in the year 2025. Still, I haven’t been able to shake a persistent feeling of guilt each time I paraglide back into Hyrule.Whenever I’ve fired up Breath of the Wild, a voice in the back of my head screams, “Shouldn’t you play something else? Something new?!” Part of that is thanks to my job as a member of games media, where I feel professionally obligated to stay abreast of the latest releases in any given year. But as I’ve reflected on my complicated feelings surrounding replaying my favorite games, I’ve realized there’s more to it than just my career.In the age of social media, we are constantly pushed to experience the latest form of entertainment, whether it’s the current blockbuster film, the newest streaming sensation, or, yes, the most recent hit video game. Aside from people obnoxiously telling you, “Well, you should have stayed off social media if you didn’t want to be spoiled,” participating in the discourse surrounding the latest cultural zeitgeist has become an event unto itself. I didn’t see Sinners until a few weeks after it hit theaters, and as a result, I missed out on the most rewarding conversations taking place across the social platforms, and after I finally caught it, I was kicking myself for not seeing it sooner. The fear of missing out is exacerbated when the conversation dominates the platform that serves as the only way you communicate with your long-distance friends.But it’s even more than that. Maybe it stems from growing up and constantly being told I should be doing something other than playing video games with my spare time, but that same guilt has transferred to whenever I consume entertainment I have before. A few years ago, I rewatched the entire Marvel Cinematic Universe, one of my favorite entertainment franchises ever. That is an absurd amount of time to devote to something I’ve already seen, but when that guilty feeling popped up, I talked myself down from letting it overtake me and dissuade me from my plan.How did I pull that off? By reminding myself that time spent doing something you love isn’t time wasted. I think we should all travel to places we’ve never been, try foods we’ve never eaten, attempt a new hobby we’ve never done, and maintain a healthy level of physical activity and outdoor time, but if something brings you joy and it’s not hurting anyone, why deny yourself that pleasure? Yes, there’s abundant value in cooking a new cuisine, but sometimes, that same dish you’ve made over the last 15 years just hits right.Comfort-food gaming has been a staple of my entire life – I’ve replayed Super Mario World, Final Fantasy X, and Pokémon Blue more times than I can count – and I would argue that with the tumult and uncertainty of the 2025 news cycle, we need it now more than ever. If gaming is your most prominent hobby, as it is mine, don’t deny yourself the joy and escapism that re-embarking on your favorite adventure grants you. So, plan that movie-rewatch marathon, fire up Switch Online’s retro libraries, and do your best to not count how many games you bought in the most recent Steam sale that are still sitting at “0 minutes played.” I’ve been trying to treat my hobby more like a hobby rather than a checklist to complete, and I’m getting more enjoyment out of it as a result.  This article originally appeared in issue 370 of Game Informer in July 2025.

Welcome to September 2026, which will forever be known as pre-pre-GTA VI month. Alternatively, it might be known as the month when every single video game came out. We're already feeling the weight of the September gaming waterfall, but the water is invigorating, if a bit intense. Well, guess what: it’s back. Onimusha: Way of the Sword is the first new Onimusha game from Capcom in over two decades, and after covering it all month long for Game Informer’s cover story hub, I’m happy to report that the final game is absolutely worth your time and money. The combat is incredible – challenging, intricate, complex, and constantly building on itself. The characters and setting are good fun, too, and though it has more repetition in its combat arena design than I’d prefer, you still get to kill stuff with protagonist Musashi’s sword… and that’s always a great time. It’s out today, and you can read my Onimusha: Way of the Sword review while it’s downloading. Oh, and if you’re worried about difficulty, don’t be: Onimusha: Way of the Sword has an incredible easy mode. The Blood Of Dawnwalker Charles Harte I was optimistic about The Blood of Dawnwalker after reading my coworker Brian Shea's articles from his cover story trip, but I was still very surprised by how much I like the game. You play as Coen, a Dawnwalker (which means you're a vampire by night and a human by day) who has 30 days to save his family from an evil vampire lord. Each quest you complete advances the clock by a little, and you only have a limited amount of time before the game concludes, which means you have to pick and choose your missions carefully. What I didn't realize until playing, however, is that there's no main quest – you choose what feels important to you in your journey to save your loved ones. It's an incredibly empowering combination, and I really feel like my decisions matter when I focus on a quest to reforge an ancient sword instead of buddying up with some revolutionaries. I also enjoy the directional combat, the different mechanics in the night and daytimes, and the general atmosphere. Our reviewer Diego was a little less enthusiastic than I, so your mileage may vary, but if you're a fan of games with choice-based stories, I highly recommend The Blood of Dawnwalker.Game Informer's The Blood of Dawnwalker review Orbitals Charles Harte I like Orbitals, but I don't love it. It's an absolutely gorgeous game with visuals inspired by 80s anime and a wicked soundtrack to match. The characters are fun and charming, and even the menus are expressive and unique. The vibes are immaculate, but the actual gameplay is just okay. It's co-op only, and you'll spend its 6-hour runtime collaborating with a partner to solve puzzles and save your home space station. I just wasn't impressed with the game itself – it has some good ideas, but fails to meaningfully stand out the way similar co-op-only games do. I also don't love the story, but it's admittedly not much of a hurdle until the final moments. Despite those gripes, it's still such a joyous experience that I recommend it nonetheless. If the art style draws you in, you're in for a pretty good weekend with a friend. For my full thoughts, you can read my review here. Halloween: The Game Wesley LeBlanc Friday the 13th: The Game developer IllFonic has finally made the asymmetrical horror game I’ve been wanting since playing as Jason and camp counselors, and it’s called Halloween: The Game and is out now. Kind of. IllFonic is doing the whole “pay for the more expensive edition and get early access to it” thing I hate (because it preys on FOMO and is just generally lame), so you can technically hop in right now. But if you decide to wait for the real launch, that’s next week, on September 8. Either way, I recently wrapped up its single-player campaign (IllFonic’s first) and am starting my multiplayer journey ahead of reviewing the game sometime next week. If you’re a fan of John Carpenter’s Halloween, this single-player campaign is a passionate love letter to it… but it’s also only a couple of hours long and basically a glorified tutorial for actually playing as Michael Myers in the game’s primary multiplayer experience. So, would I recommend picking this game up for just its campaign? No, absolutely not. But if you’re a fan of asymmetrical horror games, or if you’ve never tried an IllFonic joint (they’re good fun, with or without friends to join you), Halloween: The Game is shaping up to be great. Crimson Moon Marcus Stewart Crimson Moon has Souls-inspired combat, but it isn’t a true Soulslike. It’s actually more of a roguelike thanks to its run-based blueprint, where you embark on missions as a nephlim (a human-angel hybrid) to slay a bevy of grotesque and dangerous demons. I’m not sure entirely why – I’m sure it benefits whatever version of Earth this is – but you’ll likely not care too much because of the solid combat, a more forgiving run-based format thanks to having multiple lives, and an impressive presentation for a $20 game. While it’s not perfect, Crimson Moon offers a pretty good time, likely even more so if you rope in a friend for two-player co-op. I played the first two hours of the game on a stream this week, so be sure to check out that VOD if you want a better look at Crimson Moon in action.  NBA 2K27 Brian Shea The NBA 2K series is perpetually the most frustrating sports game franchise. It possesses quite possibly the best gameplay, visuals, and Franchise mode in all of sports gaming, but so many of its other offerings are riddled with microtransactions in ways that feel, at times, more aggressive than many free-to-play titles. This year, developer Visual Concepts seems to have dialed back the microtransactions in MyCareer, the single-player focused mode, allowing you to largely avoid the live-service billboard that is The City in favor of focusing on your created player's career, but the elements are still there if you step outside the comfort of your player's apartment. However, now you can choose whether you want to play through a narrative prologue where your character rehabilitates from a career-threatening injury to make it to the NBA, skip straight to the NBA action, or even play in a different era of the NBA; playing at a time when titans like MJ and Shaq were dominating the courts is a really nice touch. And that time-travel ability continues to extend to the still-excellent Franchise mode, making this a solid entry in the long-running king of the court. Marsupilami 2: Salsa Palombia Matt Miller This under-the-radar title could be easily overlooked, but for fans of classic 2D platformers (especially in the vein of Donkey Kong Country) that would be a mistake. This is a charming and approachable platform adventure that can be played either solo or with a partner, in which you happily bounce and swing through jungle (and other) environments, collecting pick-ups, uncovering secrets, and mastering a surprisingly nuanced control scheme. While the game starts out very approachable, the levels gradually ramp up to offer significant challenge, demanding you master the three marsupilami characters' distinct movesets as you switch between them on the fly.Catchy music, vibrant visuals, and finely tuned gameplay all help this game hit some impressive notes. There's also a fun unlockable "Salsa Mode" that uses the in-game music to ramp you up into a combo of pick-ups before your meter vanishes, leading to a fun and replayable twist on the already-played levels. There's barely any real story here in the game, so don't worry that the "2" in the title is going to leave you confused. The game is an outgrowth of a long-running Belgian comic book character, but rest assured, the taut and enjoyable gameplay of this particular project requires no special French connection to enjoy. Find the game on PS5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, or PC. Tabletop Pick – Fibbage Matt Miller Longtime fans of Jackbox Games' many excellent party video game releases likely need no introduction to the concept of Fibbage. It's been a popular and included option in various iterations of the series since 2014, and playing the video game version remains a most amusing time. But we all enjoy a break from our screens from time to time, and the popularity of the video game version has driven Jackbox to release a physical card game that perfectly captures the same fun while gathered around a table.In this version, players take turns reading a card with an unusual piece of trivia upon it, such as "In March 2006, a burglar in Osaka, Japan, knew he screwed up when he broke into a building that contained 20 ____. The answer (in this case "Sumo Wrestler") is written on a whiteboard by the player who revealed and read the card, while everyone else writes down their own pretend answer. Players then vote on which answer is actually true, with points for the player who tricks the most people, and an additional bonus point for the funniest answer. It's all quite lighthearted and silly, and this card game version ably stands by its original digital counterpart as an ideal pick for your next get-together with friends.
