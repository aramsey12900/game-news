Dragon’s Dogma 2: Dark Arisen Is Great, But At a CostNEW
Capcom choosing the exact same subtitle for two separate expansions in two different games makes a lot of sense. Dragon's Dogma 2: Dark Arisen is not only a thematically relevant title, but thanks to ...
Capcom choosing the exact same subtitle for two separate expansions in two different games makes a lot of sense. Dragon's Dogma 2: Dark Arisen is not only a thematically relevant title, but thanks to the first game's expansion of the same name, it also conveys to longtime fans what to expect: namely, a new gameplay cycle focused on reworked loot and plenty of dungeon crawling. The key difference this time around is that instead of heading into the labyrinthine depths of a cursed island, you've got an entirely new region to explore.   As someone who loved Dragon's Dogma 2, I couldn't really ask for much more. Yet changes to some of the game's core systems also dilute what made it so unique when it launched back in 2024. I know it's always been a fairly divisive game, and these changes are intended to make the experience more approachable, but they also strip away some of its magic in an effort to appeal to more people. Dark Arisen is still a great expansion, transporting you to a fascinating new region with more loot to earn, fantastical beasts to slay, and stories to behold, but it also shifts Dragon's Dogma 2 in a slightly different direction.  To start with, Dark Arisen alleviates some of the game's intentional friction by reducing how often you need to travel on foot. The Eternal Ferrystone was already added to the game a few months ago, letting you freely fast travel to any portcrystal without expending a scarce resource. Those same portcrystals are located at every key location in Dark Arisen's new Norgan region. You still have to reach them on foot first to activate them, but once you do, you can simply teleport from one side of the map to the other.  Using the Eternal Ferrystone is entirely optional; you can still traverse almost all of Norgan on foot if you desire, so the friction is still there if you want it. However, Dark Arisen also feels designed with unlimited fast travel in mind. The first settlement you come across, for instance, acts as a hub of sorts, letting you manage your gear, rest at the inn, and pick up a fair few side quests, so you're intended to return here often.  Meanwhile, many quests require you to visit the same locations over and over again to speak with specific characters. Heading off on foot might be an option, but it's never incentivized and feels antithetical to how it wants you to play.  I'm not a fan of this change. I can acknowledge that giving players more choice is a positive, but the back-and-forth quest design and reliance on fast travel means you never really feel like you have options when traversing Norgan. This has a fairly significant knock-on effect, too: Without embarking on foot more often, I rarely stumbled into the emergent moments that made the base game so thrilling. And without that palpable sense of adventure, Dark Arisen feels less like Dragon's Dogma and more like any number of action-RPGs, rendering this a fundamental change to its identity, rather than an improvement. When Dragon's Dogma 2 launched, traveling the regions of Vermund and Battahl felt treacherous, especially in the black of night. The world was as hazardous as the enemies you encountered, and campsites provided a welcome respite on your weary travels. Now, those feelings of everpresent danger and welcome safety are mostly gone. There's no risk when you can freely fast travel out of danger or skip a journey entirely. The game's uncompromising design has been softened to broaden its appeal.   The same goes for changes to its save system. By letting you roll back to a manual "Interim Save" at any time, there's no longer any risk involved in your decision-making. You won't have to live with the consequences of accidentally angering a bunch of guards in Battahl's jail or fret over when to use the Unmaking Arrow. This one-hit-kill item still triggers an autosave as soon as it's fired, creating tension the moment you decide to draw back the bowstring, but the ability to select an earlier save robs it of its finality. If these changes were strictly tied to Dark Arisen, they wouldn't be quite as detrimental. Yet this sanding down of its intentional friction affects the base game, too. By changing these systems, Capcom has fundamentally altered what made Dragon's Dogma 2 so fantastic. The emergent moments that happened on the road, the interplay between its systems and physics engine, and the need to deal with the consequences of your actions--all of this combined to make the base game an unpredictable and powerful adventure that stood apart from other similar games. I enjoyed its resolute design because it forced me to tackle the world on its own terms, providing a challenge that captured a rousing sense of adventure. I still thoroughly enjoyed Dark Arisen, despite these changes, but there's also this nagging feeling that something's missing.   Norgan, for its part, has moments where that classic Dragon's Dogma abrasiveness returns. The frigid region offers a nice change of pace from the verdant forests and arid deserts explored previously, with jagged, snow-capped cliffs; frozen rivers; icy tundras; and dilapidated, long-abandoned castles.  The hyperboreal climate doesn't just provide a visual shift, either; the weather can be both an ally and a foe. Sudden blizzards blanket the screen in heavy snowfall, obscuring your view within a few feet and taking the mini-map out of action. You also can't fast travel during these intense whiteouts, which restores the feeling that you're battling the elements just as much as the ghouls and monsters waiting to ambush you. On the flip side, you can also use the environment to your advantage, knocking down giant overhead icicles to crush your foes, throwing and skewering them on pointy shards of ice, and rolling boulders downhill that grow larger as they pick up more and more snow. You'll appreciate the extra help when facing down demonic goatmen and abominable snowman-esque monstrosities with gnarly mouths that cover their entire torsos.    At first glance, Norgan's map might seem fairly small, but there's a sprawling verticality both above and below the surface that hides its true density. New climbing hooks and grapple points have been introduced to help you tackle the elevation changes, while interconnected dungeons and caves reveal shortcuts amid expansive ancient ruins and the piles of loot therein.  Speaking of which, Dark Arisen entirely reworks the game's loot system with the addition of Relics. You'll unearth this generic gear in chests and on the bodies of defeated enemies, and can then take it to one of the new vendor characters for appraisal, revealing what kind of equipment you've found, its raw stats, and, on occasion, whether it has any special attributes that enhance weapon skills or grant passive abilities.  This creates a Monster Hunter-esque loop as you explore and defeat powerful foes to gather more Relics, before returning to the appraiser to reveal what you've found and upgrade your character. An unseen dice roll determines the type and quality, and no single piece of gear will remain in use for too long before you'll invariably find something better, but this is still a marked improvement over the base game's uninspired loot system--where I think I purchased more usable weapons and armor than I actually found.  The base game's map has also received a similar gameplay loop of its own, with the addition of 12 repeatable dungeons known as the Lost Rite Trials. While you can easily travel back and forth between Norgan and Vermund/Battahl, these new dungeons are designed to be tackled before venturing into the snowy north, setting you up with more powerful gear to make the transition easier, with repeat visits netting more and more loot.  Some of these dungeons are fairly straightforward (reach the end and kill everything along the way), while others rely on different mechanics or require prerequisites before you can enter. One such trial, which asks you to enter as either an Archer or Magick Archer, features rooms filled with support pillars you can shoot down to topple the terrain and wipe out groups of enemies.  Aside from better gear, completing each Lost Rite Trial nets you an Offering Flame. These can be used at various Obelisks scattered across the frigid Norgan landscape to unlock new vocation skills and stat buffs that boost health, damage, and so on.  The new skills are welcome, though bumping up the amount of skills you can equip at one time from four to six is a more notable improvement. Having more options at your disposal opens up the game's combat a tad more, and somewhat makes up for the lack of new vocations. As someone who's already maxed out most of the game's classes, though, I realize I might notice the dearth of new ones more than most. One of the more surprising things about Dark Arisen is how much I enjoyed its self-contained story. Okay, maybe "enjoyed" is the wrong word, but I was definitely more invested in seeing how things would unfold than I was in the base game. It's a solid-enough vehicle for getting you from point A to B, even if it's still just remixing themes from the first game. More characters are involved, and you spend significant time with them, so there's at least something to latch onto. The writing still has little time for subtlety or nuance, but it's appropriately broody, and the story eventually leads you to some fantastic set pieces and locations.  At over 20 hours long, Dragon's Dogma 2: Dark Arisen is the kind of hefty expansion we don't see very often. I might like this softened version of the game a little less than I did before, but even if some of its prickly nature has been smoothed over, Dark Arisen still offers a whole bunch more of what I loved about Dragon's Dogma 2 in the first place. For first-time players, and those returning with new hopes, it's a more approachable game, and I hope this helps it reach a broader audience. Whichever side of the divide you fall on, there's something here for both. That might sound blasphemous to some, and its vision isn't as unflinching as it was once before, but Dark Arisen--and Dragon's Dogma 2 on the whole--are still well worth your time and attention.

RuneScape: Dragonwilds Review - An Adventure In BusyworkNEW
Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Jagex Developer: Jagex Games Studios Release: September 15, 2026 Rating: Teen RuneScape: Dragonwilds strikes a respectable balance between a fantasy RPG and survival crafting game. The variety of environments and hidden lore for those who want it give depth to an otherwise nondescript setting and offer many hours of content for those who can tolerate its crafting system. But, without having an attachment to the RuneScape series, or a love for chopping wood or mining ore, it’s difficult to enjoy past the opening chapter. Though there is fun to be had, I wish certain parts of it didn’t feel as tedious or locked behind a grind for materials.Dragonwilds expands the RuneScape world and brings back familiar faces to ground players in the dragon-infested continent of Ashenfall. It revolves around the history of Ashenfall’s early settlers and the takedown of Dragon Queen Kuldra, told indirectly through glowing blue books and side quests. Those stories of grief and greed are interesting, but mostly a backdrop for common tasks like plundering temples and stabbing rats. Most activities tie back to the crafting system, whether that’s foraging for food or smithing swords. You can build tools, weapons, armor, jewelry, furniture, structures, and more. Dragonwilds lets you spend as much time as you want farming materials, though some, like ores, are more necessary than others. Magic spells can speed up some of the monotony, but that also requires leveling up your skills enough to learn those spells. Even with crafting at its core, Dragonwilds is bloated with workstations and items that are more laborious than fun to collect. I blame the increasingly high number of materials needed to craft higher-level weapons and armor, and the multiple station types that could have been combined into one. As you get stronger, the number of materials you need for your gear increases, too. For example, my gear now needs Fine Cloth woven from threads and gold bars. By the time I reached the halfway point of the game, I had separate stations for tools and weapons, armor, jewelry, bows and arrows, and magical weapons like wands.Classes don’t really exist in Dragonwilds. Instead of explicitly choosing a class like in a traditional RPG, you swap between roles by switching your gear – like swapping from armor to robes to switch from melee to magic. Changing classes felt pointless because I could force my way through most challenges with a sword, and bows and wands are less effective given the circumstances. Many confrontations involve flanking from multiple sides, so long-range combat isn’t ideal. Magic isn’t bad, but not many creatures have magical weaknesses and melee can easily do more damage with attack speed and buffs.Dragonwilds is massive. Just the first arc took 20 to 30 hours to finish before I slew my first dragon. Fellhollow, the second arc, took about the same time to complete its story quests, and it didn’t make me any more excited to reach Dowdun Reach because I adapted so quickly to expectations. At the start of each region, I expect to grind for materials for three to five hours before I could expect to survive an “expedition” from my base, let alone bungling around uncharted territory to complete a quest with vague instructions. Thankfully, this steep on-ramp can be smoothed over through a setting that eases the experience grind, but the base way to play encourages the tedium to set in. To its credit, Dragonwilds spices up exploration in each region with gimmicks like “soulrifting” in Fellholllow, which unlocks alternate planes and invisible bridges.Ideally, you avoid dying in any game, but Dragonwilds twists the knife as much as a Soulslike; when your health depletes, you drop your entire inventory and teleport back to the last place you camped. To avoid the inconvenience of refilling your bag, it’s easier to run back to the gravestone to retrieve your inventory. I’m grateful that I don’t just lose all the items, and that Dragonwilds spawns gravestones outside of dungeons instead of making me go back inside to claim them. Still, it’s annoying to be unexpectedly overpowered and then need to dive back into dangerous territory just to avoid another crafting spree. You can turn this annoying convention off in the settings, but playing the game as intended leads to cumbersome situations. For better or worse, Dragonwilds brings back the “old school” feeling of discovering what to do without overt handholding. An NPC may suggest a general direction, but won’t tell you that you need an Iron Pickaxe to mine a dragon's teeth to complete your quest. Players who have trouble understanding these hints could spend hours trying to decipher exactly how to find the items they need to progress. Dragonwilds prioritizes the journey of completing quests over how easy they are to finish. After all, that means more time in the crafting system and discovering more about your environment, like rocks you didn’t know you could mine or hidden lore from a side quest you might have missed. Alternatively, it could lead to hours of repetitive grinding for materials, trying to unlock new clues or power-ups without getting any closer to the next objective.RuneScape: Dragonwilds freshened my perspective on survival-crafting games with base-building and farming under dragon-dominated skies. I floundered for answers on how to progress to the next story quest without clear direction, and while I could explore in the meantime, other games in similar genres can offer the same thing with more entertainment along the way. In a genre full of compelling modern classics like Valheim, RuneScape: Dragonwilds is a difficult game to recommend. Score: 6 About Game Informer's review system

Phantom Blade Zero May Have The Best Ladder Climbing In All Of Video Games, But I Promise This Hands-On Preview Talks About More Than ThatNEW

Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: S-Game Developer: S-Game Release: October 29, 2026 I played Phantom Blade Zero at Gamescom in Cologne, Germany, back in August, which, at the speed of the internet, means it feels like I had my hands on the controller about five years ago. The memory is vague and dreamlike, but it’s a strong one that left an undeniable impression. I also made sure to take thorough notes because I knew it would be some time before I could write about it.For my Phantom Blade Zero play session two months ago, developer S-Game Studio offered up the opportunity to basically play from the beginning of the game, encouraging a long play session. I played through the intro and initial tutorials, meeting protagonist Soul, and learned some of his backstory.  A major selling point for Phantom Blade Zero since its reveal has been its use of motion capture for its animation. When our own Wesley LeBlanc visited the studio in China last year, they put him in the motion capture rig to showcase how important the motion capture process was to the game’s development. My admittedly dismissive reaction to this selling point has always been… all modern triple-A games use motion capture for gameplay and cutscenes. I have seen video game performers like Troy Baker dressed in grey leotards more than I have seen him dressed in clothes whenever behind-the-scenes footage of game development is shared. What’s the big deal?I am not fully removed from that camp, if I am being honest, but I admit that getting my hands on Phantom Blade Zero and controlling the movement and action did highlight the overall smoothness of playing what is essentially a Chinese martial arts film. It really does feel like you’re playing a game that borrows the beauty of human movement showcased in movies like Ang Lee’s Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon. Acrobatic dance applied to the art of swinging a sword. After completing an opening combat encounter, it is revealed that Soul suffered a heart wound and a healer cryptically named The Flesh Sculptor gave him a replacement: a tainted heart that will only let him live for 66 days. And then I am whisked away to a high-speed carriage ride where the charismatic old man driver warns me that no one visits my destination because of a plague.Enemies with chain and claw weapons ambush the carriage and Soul ejects the driver from the cart before things get too dangerous, as the driver's yell of, “You still owe me for the ride!” echoes in the distance. This whole sequence is very "style over substance." Pulling off counters and delivering sword slices to enemies as they leap from the nearby cliff face walls isn’t particularly challenging, but it looks impressive while feeling closer to a series of QTE moments. There is no QTE, however, to save the horses as the carriage careens off a cliff and crashes in a valley. When Soul comes to following the crash, I am surprised to see the dead horses hanging from the trees. A gruesome image now etched onto the map screen presumably serves as a memorable visual reminder of this location, but I can’t help but feel bad for the poor animals. The whole sequence impressively sets up a chance to finally explore without training wheels, and I am happy to report that the level design is interesting and circuitous. With such a focus on combat and combat choreography, I was worried the action would be a series of arena fights. But Soul can sneak up on enemies, open shortcuts, and find characters that need help and offer missions. I hesitate to place the game in the Souls-like genre – it seems generally more forgiving – but the level layout recalls that structure in a way I like.Soul may also be the fastest ladder climber I have ever controlled in a video game. Ladder climbing may be an odd mechanic to highlight, but I have climbed up and down thousands of ladders in my video game career and they always bring pacing to a standstill. You push up on the control stick and wait. If you’re lucky, sometimes you can press the sprint button to make your character climb a little faster, or slide down. That’s been the best-case scenario for decades. Soul, however, bounds up ladders like Spider-Man sprinting up the side of a building. Much like the player eager to get to the next memorable moment, Soul doesn’t have time to gingerly step on or grab each rung one by one. The UI loudly proclaimed that Soul officially only has 65 days to live once he woke up from the crash. There is no time. And speaking of no time, mine was also literally limited during my Gamescom appointment, so S-Game Studio fast-forwarded me to the incredibly impressive Lion Dance boss. The Lion Dance is a traditional Chinese dance where two performers wear a joined lion costume and perform acrobatic feats often on high pillars. Here’s a good example. Seeing the real-world version of the dance is entrancing, and for Phantom Blade Zero’s Lion Dance boss, the team motion-captured real performers.Just seeing the technically-two characters move around in the boss arena is impressive, but the second stage of the boss fight (which you jump to if you die – no need to replay the first stage of the boss fight) is where it gets really impressive. Pillars appear from the ground and the Lion dances among the tops of them while shooting fireballs at you. And while that is happening, small lion head minions appear to distract you. However, defeating the last minion means you can grab a lion mask, jump on the pillars yourself, and shoot your own fireballs at the boss. The reports from everyone who has had a chance to play Phantom Blade Zero have basically been universally positive, but I still went into the demo expecting a potentially forgettable, but well-executed action game. It ended up being one of my favorite hands-on experiences at Gamescom, despite the long wait to talk about it. The game feels great to play; I am eager to explore the levels; and the Lion Dance boss, in particular, showed me that the boss encounters (unless the Lion Dance is the notable exception) will be full of unexpected surprises and impressive animation.
