The Aeons of Honkai Star Rail have always been far beyond ordinary mortals. They are conceptual deities whose actions have the potential to reshape entire galaxies--so putting one into a player's four...
The Aeons of Honkai Star Rail have always been far beyond ordinary mortals. They are conceptual deities whose actions have the potential to reshape entire galaxies--so putting one into a player's four-person team was always going to be a huge design challenge for the HoYoverse team.  Ahead of the game's Version 4.7 update, we spoke to lead game designer Chengan An about why Aha, The Aeon of Elation, was chosen as the game's first playable Aeon. According to Chengan, the decision to bring Aha into the fold came down to their uniquely human disposition, as well as the development team's unwillingness to break the game's battle rules. Aha is meant to focus on collaborative synergy rather than racking up extreme damage numbers. Chengan also noted that Aha's personality is what makes them the most logical Aeon to bring to the battlefield at this point in the game's life cycle. "The game's system is based on the concept of Paths. For Aeons, they are quite abstract concepts for most of the people in the world, but Aha is the most human-like one." Chengan continued: "I think our [game's] battle is way too tiny for Aeons to initiate a war. For Aha, we picked some of the most important factors to bring into the combat design. We certainly don't want them to destroy the battle with the power of an Aeon, and that kind of design does not fit Aha's personality as an Aeon of Elation." HoYoverse viewed the Path of Elation as a foundation for Aha, one that's been built up over months of prior updates. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rMcFUTECbMQ "For the Path of Elation, we do not intend to simply bring a character with super-strong skills to overcome all the existing characters when we have already designed so many previous Paths. We aim to bring the Aeon of Elation to interact with our other characters, and that's why we have already presented so many characters that can interact with Aha. We think this kind of design can bring a fresh experience to our players when they see such an Aeon," Chengan explained. Chengan confirmed that Aha was never envisioned as an offensive carry, and that the decision to make them a supportive unit rather than a damage dealer was an easy one. "We've always designed Aha that way," he said. "Aha is closely connected to other characters, and from Aha's instance you can see lots of performances. The Path of Elation is about bringing happiness and all these emotions. Aha is more suitable to be a supportive character than a damage-carrying one." Playing alongside Aha is supposed to mirror stepping onto a grand stage together, reframing the battlefield into an immersive performance. "All the characters together with Aha perform on the cosmic stage, and different characters have their own moments on that stage." Chengan said.  By keeping Aha in a supportive role, HoYoverse avoids rendering previous characters obsolete. Instead of ending the fight single-handedly with absurd damage output, the Aeon of Elation works alongside every squad member, giving them a chance to show their strengths. This is the best choice HoYoverse could have made when introducing a playable Aeon. Investing years of play time into a game only to have your hard work nullified by introducing an overpowered character that's capable of wiping the floor with any opponent would have put many players, myself included, off the game. However, if this is the approach the team is taking with Aha, I don't foresee any possible ways to add more playable Aeons in the future, unless they exclusively take on support roles, and that could be a missed opportunity. It remains to be seen whether there are any tricks up HoYoverse's sleeve to get around the limitations they've created for themselves, but for now I'm looking forward to bringing Aha into my team and feeling what it's like to play as an actual deity.

How Honkai Star Rail Took Inspiration From Sports Cars For Something UnexpectedNEW
Turn-based RPGs can face difficulties during the design process when figuring out how to convey physical momentum. In an environment where actions are decided by button taps and menus rather than real...
Turn-based RPGs can face difficulties during the design process when figuring out how to convey physical momentum. In an environment where actions are decided by button taps and menus rather than real-time physics, the connection to physical movement can feel inauthentic. When HoYoverse designed Honkai Star Rail's massive fire-breathing dragon that accompanies Castorice, lead game designer Chengan An says the team looked beyond typical fantasy tropes for inspiration. Instead of looking at other mythical creatures or cinematic moments, the development team found a surprising inspiration in high-performance sports cars. Speaking to GameSpot during HoYoverse's Media Tour in Seoul, South Korea, Chengan An mentioned that Castorice and her colossal dragon are some of his favorite characters. While a giant dragon spraying flames across the battlefield looks undeniably striking, the developers realized early on that visual fidelity alone doesn't capture the sensation of actually commanding a dragon. "Let's take Castorice, for example. When we designed the giant dragon, beyond the cool visual presentation and some special effects, we also have detailed design in terms of the player's controls."  The team realized that there's no basis in humans' physical memory when it comes to commanding a dragon. Players understand what a dragon attack looks like because they have spent decades watching them in movies, but that's done passively. "When it comes to a giant dragon bursting fire, actually no one has ever experienced it in person, right?" Chengan said. "That's actually an experience based on films or in dramas. However, in films or dramas you can only watch what the directors have already shot for you. You still cannot control the dragon to burst into fire, like first-hand." https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0FPvfC8g6-Q In many action games, dragon attacks could be controlled with a reticle or a trigger. In a turn-based title, however, players are more restricted with their options, having to stick to straightforward commands. "You only need to tap the button to burst into fire," Chengan confirmed. "We get inspiration from our real life, that is, the engine of a sports car." "When we drive or listen to the sound of a sports car, and you speed up a gear, and the engine is riding, then when you switch the gear, you can hear a very powerful impact of sound. We're inspired by this phenomenon," he noted. Chengan An wanted to replicate that distinct mechanical cadence. As the RPMs climb through one gear, there's a sudden release at the shift point and then an immediate surge in the next gear. That loop became the inspiration for Castorice's dragon flame.  "When you switch the gear, you can feel the super-strong impact of speeding up. This is our inspiration. We turned it into the dragon, so you can experience it [breathing] the fire and you can see the gradual process. Each time you play Castorice, you can feel the gradual process and instant burst of attack, and then players look forward to playing it again and again," he explained. For HoYoverse, the engine metaphor represents a wider approach to character and combat development. While the game takes place across space, Chengan An noted that the best design ideas come from outside fantasy media. "You can see inspiration can be not only from games or dramas, but also drawn from your everyday life." The fantastical turn-based ability being tied to the grounded, real-world kinetic escalation of a sports-car engine is just one way that HoYoverse turns a standard input prompt into an attack that players feel more connected to, despite Honkai Star Rail's limited possibilities given the genre of the game. I don't necessarily feel the sports car influence when playing as Castorice, but with Chengan An's contextual comments, it's easy to see how real-world moments are shaping every corner of Honkai Star Rail, even if we don't always realize it as players.

Australia Did It: End-of-the-World Railway Defense Business Review - Delivering Where It MattersNEW

Reviewed on: PC Platform: PC Publisher: Mystic Forge Developer: Rami Ismail, Aesthetician Labs Release: November 10, 2026 Australia Did It: End-of-the-world Railway Defense Business is a game I catch myself playing in my head long after I shut it off. Though it uses turn-based strategy as its base, it expands beyond the genre’s regular confines, blending elements from roguelikes, puzzle games, bullet-heaven titles, and the tower-defense genre. The result is an experience that demands wide-scale experimentation and rewards improvisation. In a post-apocalyptic world where the Atlantic Ocean has dried up, you risk your life to ensure your shipment reaches its destination. Riding from station to station by train, you must build your team amid your many missions as various mutated creatures, malicious machines, and coordinated soldiers attack your convoy. Lore is doled out piecemeal and serves mostly as background dressing, but each time I learned more about the world, I became more curious.To survive the harshness of the wasteland, you must use cindermint, an in-game resource gained for surviving turns and killing enemies, to spawn defenders, with three base classes to choose from. The revolver-wielding Gunslinger is your primary damage dealer, while the Radshot applies corrosive radiation damage, and the Nomad offers a close-range shotgun blast with knockback.  Those base-level characters offer solid variety upon which to build. And that’s where the main hook for Australia Did It comes in: experimentation through combination. In the midst of battle, you can merge two units of the same level to create new, more powerful defenders, influenced by the traits of those used in the merge. At the onset, you can merge two Nomads to create a level 2 Nomad with increased damage and knockback, but the real fun comes in combining disparate units to create completely new characters. Even now, more than 15 hours into my gameplay, I’m still concocting new combinations I want to try on my next run.By the end of my first successful run, I had multiple level 6 characters, including a railgun-toting heavy, mages who steal health from enemies and redistribute it to nearby allies, a drone that can move beyond the walls of the station to attack enemies directly, and, my personal favorite, a dual-shotgun-wielding one-man wrecking crew. However, many of these higher-level units also possess drawbacks to consider, like the railgun scorching all adjacent tiles or the drone running out of battery if it’s outside the walls for too long. Though I started keeping notes in my head about which combinations led to my favorite results, I was grateful for a comprehensive compendium that tracked the tens of unit types I unlocked through this rewarding experimentation. The meat of the experience places your squad on a 25-tile square board with enemies spawning from the four principal directions. Thoughtfulness and positioning are crucial, as each unit on your squad possesses different traits and movement efficiency, as do the encroaching enemies. Basic enemies often make a beeline toward the station, while others may take a weaving route or keep their distance and blast you at range. Improvising and adjusting your strategy on the fly is the most engaging part of Australia Did It, and when you perfectly adapt in the moment, or escape the scenario by the skin of your teeth, there are few better feelings I’ve experienced in a game this year. Your goal in this phase is to simply defend the station and gather resources to strengthen your squad before evacuating and moving on to the next area. Considering each unit's strengths, weaknesses, and capabilities while strategically positioning them around the station takes some getting used to, especially during the more chaotic stages. I fist-pumped as I watched the train pull out of a station that was about to crumble, and I lamented the loss of high-level units I had to leave behind to ensure the survival of the convoy. The process is ceaselessly thrilling, even as you begin to see how the gears work under the hood. I just wish I could say the same about the intervening train sections.Once you escape the turn-based strategy portion, your surviving squad members hop aboard the train and chart their route. Using a basic map, you choose your next stop, with factors like enemy types and modifiers shown to you ahead of time. The top-down train segments between station-defense stages are largely passive; the units you evacuated from the previous station automatically attack swarming enemies with the same traits and perks they have in the main gameplay areas. You can aim a focus cone to instruct your units, but you’re at the mercy of the units you’ve brought. You can trigger an ultimate ability to send your defenders into overdrive, but I rarely needed it.While I love the idea of intentionally crafting a team that’s good at both the turn-based strategy and bullet-heaven transport sections, I was never fully engaged getting from point A to point B. Instead, I often caught myself checking how much longer before my train arrived at the next station; these sections can be completed in a minute or two, but that doesn’t alleviate the feeling that it’s a step down from the other gameplay. These novel portions aren’t bad by any means; they’re merely an average part of an otherwise excellent experience.Looking back on the hours I poured into Australia Did It, the forgettable train segments will occupy only a small part of my memories, just as they do in each run. Instead, what will stick with me for months to come are the creative combination mechanics, the outstanding turn-based defense system, and the thrill of uncovering the mysteries of this bleak, yet engaging world.
