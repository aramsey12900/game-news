Nintendo’s Customer Appreciation Sale Flubbed The Goodwill Gesture AttemptNEW
I love Nintendo. It was a formative part of my video game history, and to this day I think it makes some of the most consistently great games in the industry. But you know what I love more? Deals. For...
I love Nintendo. It was a formative part of my video game history, and to this day I think it makes some of the most consistently great games in the industry. But you know what I love more? Deals. For proof just look at my Steam backlog, which is full of games I stupidly bought on the basis of their being cheap. Do I have time to play this? No, but it was so cheap!  This is somewhat of a contradiction in my gaming tastes. Nintendo isn't known for offering sales. In fact, it's notoriously stingy with discounts, offering them rarely, and with only modest savings. So I was cautiously optimistic about the Customer Appreciation Sale, which Nintendo promoted as a larger scale sale than usual. The sale is now ongoing and, well, I should have trusted my instincts. These deals are not good. The sale prices are relegated primarily to older games, and while 30% is a larger discount than usual for Nintendo games on the eShop, it still barely makes a dent in the full list price.  Since Nintendo games keep their sticker price for years, this sale boasts discounts on years-old games like Donkey Kong Tropical Freeze and Mario Tennis Aces, both from 2018, for a lean $42. Those hoping that newer games would be included are likely left disappointed. A handful of 2025 releases are included--Metroid Prime 4, the Mario Galaxy two-pack, Xenoblade Chronicles Definitive Edition, and Donkey Kong Returns HD. Everything else is 2024 and before. So it's mostly older games, and most of the newest games are ports of older games. And in many cases these are the Switch 1 versions of games, meaning that if you want an optional Switch 2 upgrade, you have to make two purchases, and end up with less than a 30% discount as a result. What a savings So the deals are unremarkable. But so what? I've gone this long without a copy of Astral Chain; I don't need to buy it now for $42. What makes this sale such an unforced error, though, is how Nintendo positioned the sale as innately tied to tariffs.  When President Trump came into office, he instituted a strict tariff regime on all kinds of imported goods from a wide range of countries. Lots of consumer goods manufacturers raised their prices in response, passing the cost onto the consumer. This all happened to coincide with the time that Nintendo was preparing to launch the Switch 2. Nintendo delayed US preorders to assess the impact of tariffs just after announcing its price--and while the price of the console itself didn't increase until just recently, the price of controllers, other accessories, and physical games did increase across the board. You probably know the rest of the story. Many of Trump's tariffs were struck down by the Supreme Court, roughly a year after he instituted them. The US government began issuing refunds to companies who had paid the import fees. Some companies like Playdate in turn offered tariff refunds to customers who bought their products at tariff-inflated prices. Consumers have also started to sue, saying they were impacted by the tariffs and if the companies are being refunded, it's only fair that the consumers who ate the costs get their money back. This does not appear to be going well so far, but in the long term, who knows. Nintendo, meanwhile, made strong profits. Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze It's in this environment that Nintendo announced its Customer Appreciation Sale, and in the process, it explicitly mentioned tariffs as a factor: "The Customer Appreciation Sale is our way of saying thank you to Nintendo players for their continued support and is made possible in part by tariff-related refunds," the company said in its announcement. "While Nintendo absorbed most tariff-related costs, the refunds helped make promotions like this one possible." And that is what makes this sale so galling. Tying the sale to tariffs, rhetorically or otherwise, was completely unnecessary. Nintendo likely doesn't owe us anything from a legal perspective. Sony and Microsoft have already argued as much in court and Nintendo has too. The gaming hardware manufacturers are united on this message. No one forced anyone to buy a Switch 2, its accessories, or any games. Those of us who have bought Nintendo products over the last year saw the price and decided it was worth it, regardless of whether that price was partly affected by Trump's tariffs.  What Nintendo has on its hands, instead, is a PR problem. Nintendo is getting a refund for whatever it paid in tariffs, which means that it's getting paid twice-over: once from consumers who bought their products at inflated prices, and again when the government pays it back for its import fees. That risks making consumers feel like they're getting a raw deal. Holding a big sale event and telling users it's made possible by the tariff refunds is meant to smooth over any bad feelings we as customers might have. Astral Chain As many pointed out right away, this is already a silly proposition. Nintendo is thanking us by allowing us to give them more money. Even at sale prices, they're not going to take a loss. But an appropriately generous sale with much deeper discounts on older games, and including more recent games, would at least feel like we're getting some benefit out of the arrangement. Nintendo's traditionally very conservative sale prices could cut a little deeper, just this once, and we'd all leave feeling better about the whole tariff debacle.  But the goodwill gesture goes from silly to downright insulting when the prices themselves are this mediocre. Instead of clearing any lingering resentments over the inflated price, we're left looking at this sale and saying: that's the best you can do? Instead of resolving the problem, it rubs salt in the wound. What's more, it's an unnecessary own-goal. Nintendo could have held the same sale, with the same prices, and never mentioned that it was intended as a make-good for tariffs, and many Nintendo fans wouldn't have noticed anything amiss. There was a time in relatively recent history when Nintendo actually did feel its hand was forced to offer a big magnanimous gesture to soothe fans who felt burned. When the 3DS stumbled out of the gate, Nintendo quickly slashed the price in 2011. But sensing that those who bought it at the higher price might feel burned, it offered the 3DS Ambassador Program. This gave early adopters a special certificate with optional exclusive notifications, as well as 20 free games from the NES and GBA libraries that could be downloaded to the 3DS system. The GBA downloads remained exclusive to Ambassadors through the lifetime of the 3DS. A 3DS "Ambassador Certificate" These situations are not perfectly comparable. The Switch 2 price has not been slashed and so early adopters haven't been left holding the bag. The price of a Switch 2 has already gone up, and if anything, it's more likely to increase further as global RAM prices remain unstable due to the advent of data centers. And a set of older emulated games would certainly be less appealing now that emulation is everywhere, and Nintendo offers a much larger library as part of the Switch Online subscription. But it's the type of outside-the-box thinking that we know Nintendo is capable of when its back is against the wall and it feels obligated to make things right for its loyal customers, who are often full-blown fans of the company and its franchises. Compared to that, the Customer Appreciation Sale feels sadly lacking. This could have been an opportunity to smooth over a rocky moment for customers. Instead, it feels half-hearted and non-committal.

Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave Review - Let The Games BeginNEW
Reviewed on: Switch 2 ...
Reviewed on: Switch 2 Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Intelligent Systems Release: September 17, 2026 Fire Emblem’s scale has been escalating for some time. From three Fates, to Three Houses, to numerous rings and now, a massive three-act entry for the Switch 2. Those ever-escalating aspirations have led to impressive new heights and ballooning scope. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave is easily one of my favorite settings and stories in the series' history, with memorable battles and characters, even when repetition and overabundance weigh it down.Fortune's Weave starts at the end of all things. After a quick character creator for a mysterious Pale Raven and an in medias res run through a world crumbling and haunted by the living dead, the player's avatar and Fortuna – a goddess of fate – are some of the only survivors. To save the world, they'll need to defeat the encroaching lord of the underworld, Balor, and they'll need help to do it.So, you retire to a chamber outside of time and zip back through history to the lives of four Flame Lords of Dagda: Cai, Theodora, Dietrich, and Leda. All have found their way to this continent's capital in Dagsion, where Divine Emperor Solel is hosting the Heroic Games, with a promise that any wish will be granted to the ultimate victor. Correct the causality of these fallen heroes, and they may survive and rally under your banner in the changed future for the final fight against Balor. You could go challenge the big bad at any moment, but the more lords you assemble, the easier time you'll have. Showing up ready for the finale probably means bringing at least two lords, and their respective recruits, to the final campaign, and in my case, I wanted to get as many through as I could.Fortune's Weave takes on a three-part structure, with each lord getting an individual story through the Heroic Games. They each have their own reasons for fighting: the young Cai wishes to rescue his father from prison and imminent execution; Saraminian queen Theodora wants to reclaim land important to her people; songstress Leda has murder and revenge against her father's killer on her mind; and Dietrich is just in it for the love of battle.It's a little overwrought to explain, but it lends itself well to the size of the tale Fortune's Weave is telling. All four face trials and strife throughout the course of the games. Faustian bargains, shadowy organizations plotting in the background, and old secrets brought to light by the influx of every interesting and notable person in Dagda to its capital. It's not required to play any of the routes, as you could go fight Balor on your own if you wish. But each lord's story adds more context, nuance, and character to this huge tale. I loved moments where I'd see Dietrich and his spell-casting pal Fabio mention something during Cai's path, and then get to see the events from their perspective when I played through them in Dietrich's.Playing through each route is a big task, though. They're made up of chapters, which each have a big battle or two, with free time baked in for you to spend as you'd like in and outside of the capital. The scope starts small, with shared meals to build rapport within your unit, recruiting others to your team, or training skills at the arena. It soon expands out to encompass the entire continent, though, as you can roam around, fight overworld battles, explore dungeons, and use lord-specific mechanics like Theodora's battalion recruitment or Leda's tavern performances to gain resources and power.This is where Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave starts to weigh itself down. Dungeon exploration, a returning mechanic from Echoes: Shadows of Valentia, turns Fire Emblem into a classic RPG dungeon-crawl with a small party, strike-to-engage exploration, and turn-based combat. It feels like a microcosm of big strategy battles, only they get repetitive and too frequent; I engaged with them for the first lord's route, but moved to auto-battle soon after, as I was just going through the same motions for each fight in every dungeon.Free time was most interesting when I was pressed against the clock to finish certain quests, acquire resources, and resolve paralogues before the deadline, but keeping up with the numerous weekly activities and checking in at home for any random events – often only signified by an inconspicuous ripple on my map in Dagsion – meant I was usually just going through the motions. During some periods I'd be stressed out, trying to build up support levels for a new Archer recruit while finishing enough side quests to get the renown level I'd need to get them on my team for a big battle with flying foes coming up. Other cycles, I was just in a loop: train, eat, auto-activities, repeat.When it finally comes back to the bread-and-butter strategy of Fire Emblem, Fortune's Weave is excellent. Overworld map fights are fairly straightforward, but the bookending fights of chapters are highlights, especially when they take place in Dagsion's arena. Developer Intelligent Systems concocted some brilliant coliseum battles that have all the theme and flair you'd hope for: charging chariots, puzzling mechanisms, and special combat arenas that push you to utilize your units as best you can.There's no shortage of options for combat, as each unit archetype can go down interesting upgrade lines. In Leda's path, I had the aspiring mercenary Mu train up into a deadly sword-wielder who could cut through anything with a strong critical strike. In Cai's path, I secretly trained the lord in magic alongside one of his childhood friends, Thialla, so he could become a spell-slinging mounted Caldarius for the final fights of the Heroic Games. Fortune's Weave eschews the weapon triangle to place more emphasis on what weapons can handle individually, like magic against armor or lances against horses, and a greater emphasis on Combat Arts makes training within a fighter's specific strengths rewarding. Lords also pack their own Blaze Arts, like Leda's spellsongs or Dietrich's shadowstep, giving them fun tools to enable strategies and close out maps.The opening act is great, and yet somehow only one part of the pie, as Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave still has two more acts to play out. After leading a lord to the end of their path in the Heroic Games, a second section unlocks, exploring their story in a war that breaks out not long after the Games' conclusion. See them through that, and the lord – and any units they recruited – can join you for the final campaign against Balor, which has its own cycle of days, activities, and an extra layer of grand strategy over the top. Pull it all together, and Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave is weighty to see through to the end; it took me over 130 hours to see credits, and I still missed some scenes and events. The payoffs are excellent, and certain story beats getting set up in Part One and paid off in Part Three were incredible. But doing so requires a lot of playtime, investment, and repetition. Dungeons never get more interesting or challenging, collecting specific resources can feel cumbersome, and even exploring the map and managing units starts to slow Fortune's Weave down in its final hours.What kept me going was the world and its characters. The Romanesque vision of gladiatorial combat, the plucking strings of vihuelas, and a world where gods walk among men all kept me enthralled, start to finish. The story of Fortune's Weave starts small and grows massive over its runtime, but at its heart, this Fire Emblem asks what our mortality means for us. How do we build our legacies and histories, build up for our future generations, and what do we live for with our allotted time? And what is worth defending and protecting with that time we've been given?Fortune's Weave has an amazing soundtrack and gorgeous visual designs for its cast and the various classes they can take on. Coliseum fights never failed to excite, and some of them now rank among my favorite all-time Fire Emblem maps. Warriors like Fabio, Lilian, Mu, Anatolia, Dante, Talimun, and Orchel all grew on me through their support and banter, or just developed their own emergent stories in battle.Support conversations and side quests dig deep into the world's lore, as well. In Theodora's route, characters explored the difficulty of abiding by their patron god Yu Phas' command to never write down their histories, and whether adhering to their long-held practices would cause them to erode over time. Leda grapples with the vices of indulging revenge and what music can bring to those who have lost as much as she has, while Dietrich stumbles into fascinating bits about the wider world and its long-held mysteries.The scope of Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave allows it to take massive swings at surprising, cool concepts and mechanics that feel fresh and interesting for the series. Packing them all into a single game might have made this the most intimidating entry to jump into since Fates, but putting it all in a single package with some excellent quality-of-life options, like cross-path progression boosts and side quest clears, helps smooth out the road for those who want to see everything. I've no doubt that those who crave a slow-burn epic and can stomach the repetition for the payoffs will love this world and its cast of characters. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave will easily eat up all your time for months to come, and it's worthwhile to see these Heroic Games through to their conclusion. Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Switch, PC Publisher: Nihon Falcom, Gungho Online Entertainment, Clouded Leopard Entertainment Developer: Nihon Falcom Release: September 17, 2026 Rating: Teen Coming-of-age stories are a dime a dozen in video games, especially if you’re talking about the heyday of RPGs in the late ‘90s and early 2000s. But after 80 hours with the Trails in the Sky 2nd Chapter remake, I’m convinced there’s not a single coming-of-age story that holds up as well as Estelle Bright’s journey of self-discovery. Building on the already-great remake of the first game, 2nd Chapter masterfully updates a timeless story, accentuating it with visual enhancements and phenomenal voice acting. And while the gameplay additions are few, they are meaningful, adding up to what is now the Trails series’ defining entry, and one of the best RPGs of the year. 2nd Chapter is, essentially, the second half of the story of Trails in the Sky, picking up quite literally where 1st Chapter left off. Although this has long become tradition for the Trails series, this sequel deliberately builds on the events, locations, and systems of the first, ultimately feeling more like an upgrade than a drastically different sequel. Senior Bracer Estelle Bright, in the wake of a coup to overthrow Liberl’s Queen, now finds herself lost and alone after her adopted brother (and the man she loves), Joshua, reveals his checkered past and shocks everyone with an unceremonious departure. Reeling from multiple revelations, Estelle sets out on a journey to combat the heinous experiments of a shadowy organization called Ouroboros, while discovering her own reason to live on. Because this game directly and deliberately builds on 1st Chapter, playing through the first game is basically a requirement; but for all intents and purposes, 2nd Chapter is where the big narrative swings come into play. And boy do they pay off. It may sound a bit hammy, but 2nd Chapter is truly a game about the power of positivity and human connection. Baked into the fabric of this story are strong messages of the power of human choice – the importance of leaning on others and not giving in to despair. Despite 2nd Chapter being a nearly two-decade-old game, its overall message is one that feels more relevant than ever in the modern world: a strong reminder of the importance of community and caring for everyone, even if the systems governing society don’t. These ideas are expertly woven into every facet of the game, illustrated through side quests, the arcs of party members, and especially Estelle’s personal journey. More than anything, Estelle herself remains the biggest highlight of this game as a richly layered and complex protagonist.It’s honestly a shame that, 20 years later, it still feels so rare to get a brilliantly written female protagonist in RPGs like this, but Estelle’s complex character arc is so smartly explored; from her learning to stand on her own, without Joshua and her father’s help, to her endless optimism. So much of why Estelle works so well is because she’s initially built up as that cheerful, overly optimistic RPG hero stereotype, before that trope is then subverted multiple times throughout the game. This is a story that’s deeply affecting and emotional, with a delightfully slow build-up.The overall narrative of 2nd Chapter remake is exactly the same as the original game, although it’s been very slightly tweaked here and there and enhanced with some new content. In particular, smart story adjustments help this remake line up with later entries in the franchise, providing extra context on villains and story arcs that have been playing out across a dozen games. And everything in the game is heightened by genuinely fantastic voice acting across the board. 2nd Chapter has some big emotional scenes, and the voice cast pulls them off with gusto. Ray Chase’s Kevin Graham is a particular highlight here as a warrior priest who puts on a bumbling act, hiding an icy personality underneath.In every regard, the story and writing have been spruced up; the additions here are additive in the best way. That same sentiment then applies to combat and the overall gameplay experience. The hybrid combat system is identical to 1st Chapter, letting you fight enemies in an action system on the map, and then enter a turn-based battle at the press of a button. While the foundation is the same, each character has new arts and crafts. A Brave Rush ability also lets you execute a deadly team strike while fighting enemies in the action system on the map.  On other fronts, 2nd Chapter’s fishing minigame has been completely overhauled and is now surprisingly complex in how you must use different baits and rods at different fishing spots. The enhanced map system and quest tracking also work wonders for finding what were hidden, incredibly easy-to-miss quests in the original game. Again, the intent here is clearly additive, with new elements layered on top. It’s like spreading that extra delicious layer of frosting on top of a cake. It’s not often that a game succeeds on pretty much every level, but 2nd Chapter is a rare one where it’s hard to find nearly any fault. The only real downside is that occasionally dated RPG design rears its head, in the form of dungeons that can feel just a tad too long. Some minuscule trimming of the original’s game’s content could have benefitted the overall experience, and in that regard, it is perhaps the singular misstep of this remake. The biggest question any remake has to answer is, why? What about this experience justifies remaking, or reimagining the original? Trails in the Sky 2nd Chapter now feels like one of the definitive answers to that question. It’s a game that so succinctly and expertly enhances everything about the original, from a lavish visual presentation that fills the world with extra color and detail, to a perfectly tuned enhancement of the original game’s dramatic storytelling. The original 2nd Chapter helped establish the Trails series as something special, and it truly feels like Nihon Falcom’s series has now come full circle, folding everything the studio has learned back in on itself. And in the process, it’s created an RPG powerhouse that can stand alongside the genre’s greats. Score: 9.5 About Game Informer's review system
