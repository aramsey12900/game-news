Remedy Explains Control Resonant's Most Memorable, Unexpected Sequence And Its [REDACTED] InspirationsNEW

Please allow the first sentence here to warn you that this interview discusses a portion of Control Resonant that takes place near the end of the game and is arguably the most unexpected and surprising sequence. I encourage you to experience it without spoilers. So if you haven't beaten the game yet, please do, and then come back to this page.However, if you have beaten the game, or curiosity has driven you to learn what game has been redacted from the headline and why (it's the 1995 Super Nintendo RPG, EarthBound, by the way), then please read on.FULL SPOILERS AHEADIn the late-game of Control Resonant – basically right before you tackle the final confrontation – the game suddenly shifts timeframe and genre to become a 16-bit RPG. The nostalgic lens turns the game into something that feels like 1995's EarthBound, complete with turn-based battles, in order to explain the trauma that Jesse and Dylan went through at a young age after they came across an object of power: the slide projector. The whole sequence is funny, heartbreaking, weird, and importantly effective at making you feel you are seeing a part of their past. It was particularly effective on me as I have fond memories of playing EarthBound and games like it when I was young. To see and experience Dylan and Jesse's childhood this way felt accurate, as absurd as that sounds.I reached out to Remedy to specifically discuss that portion of the game. You can find the full e-mail interview below with the team members who contributed to its creation. The big RPG backstory sequence for Jesse and Dylan – why approach the storytelling that way?Lead level designer, Anne-Marie Grönroos: The basis for the whole sequence is that the Hedron attacks Dylan's mind, breaks his reality apart, and he has to rebuild himself. We wanted the reality-breaking part to affect the game itself and have the player feel some of the confusion Dylan is feeling by breaking the fourth wall.Who pitched that sequence, how did they pitch it, and was it difficult to sell to the larger team?Grönroos: At Remedy, ideas rarely come from a single person or team, and we rarely do formal pitches, but rather discuss and workshop ideas cross-craft until they start taking the shape of something we like. The initial idea from the writer was for this to be a text-based adventure, to return to a very early form of a video game to show that the Patterning is devolving him, but an Alan Wake 2 DLC had already done that, and we wanted to find something else.Independently, some months ago a level designer had suggested a sidequest with JRPG pixel graphics, but that had gone nowhere. I remembered that and suggested doing an old Final Fantasy-style JRPG here, which also felt more fitting since that’s the type of game Dylan and Jesse would have played in their childhood, and not a text-based adventure. People got excited about that, and after some concerns about feasibility, we went forth with it. It helped that the level designer who had originally wanted to do the JRPG side quest is a big fan of these types of games and has made them before. I wouldn’t have dropped something this crazy on someone who wasn’t passionate about it and ready to figure out how to do it with our engine that’s not built for anything like this. Young Jesse and Dylan as they appear in the sequence Is EarthBound (Mother in Japan) the primary inspiration for that sequence? Are there big fans of those games at Remedy?Senior level designer in charge of the JRPG quest, Arhi Makkonen:The whole Quest was born from that childhood feeling I had when playing old JRPGs – Final Fantasy, Breath of Fire, Chrono Trigger, Secret of Mana, Bahamut Lagoon, and yes, Mother. The most important thing for me was capturing that feeling of a time when games felt like magic. I think many people can relate to that nostalgia, and now Jesse and Dylan share that feeling in the game.Visually, Mother was a major inspiration of course. Juuso Anttila is the concept artist who did all the splendid pixel art for Rebuild quest. Mother's style fits the mysterious Americana small town vibe perfectly. We also couldn't pass up the pun about a story revolving around "Not-Mother" that was made in the style of Mother.Was it difficult to implement the sequence into the game? Did Remedy's Northlight Engine need significant work to account for the sequence?Makkonen: The Northlight engine was designed to work perfectly for third-person action games. Well, Rebuild Quest is pretty much the opposite of that. Northlight is a specialized tool rather than a general-purpose one, so we had to come up with some rather creative solutions to create a 2D JRPG.First of all, we’re faking the 2D look. In reality, the entire JRPG Ordinary exists within a 3D world that the camera simply views from directly overhead. It occupies a tiny patch in a remote corner of the game world—a space where 3D Dylan would barely have room to stand. The entire game-within-a-game is written in Lua; our incredibly versatile game designer, Tommi Saalasti, created the underlying system, upon which I was then able to build the story.Since we didn't really have the tools to create a game like this, I had to hand-code every single detail. Dylan takes one step up, Dylan turns left (where Jesse hopefully is at that moment), text box 1, text box 2. The Lua script for the Rebuild quest alone probably contains as many lines of code as all the other quests in the game combined. And then there were things like animations were restricted to a maximum of two frames, and some UI elements were created using static particle effects.So yes, we faced all sorts of challenges and compromises, but at the heart of it all was a wonderful story – simply told in a somewhat unconventional way. We trusted that the narrative would make up for some of the imperfections. Everyone's favorite FBC janitor Ahti has a cameo appearance in the sequence Remedy has a reputation for big unexpected and often crazy singular moments. What are the conversations like around delivering those? In pre-production, are there conversations like, "What is the big moment for this one?" Or do they happen pretty naturally?Grönroos: They both happen naturally and we also have conversations about what the big moments should be. Sometimes we like a section so much that we decide to put even more resources and importance to it, even if it wasn’t supposed to be all that important from the get-go. The Into the Sinkhole quest, for instance, is a quest that wasn’t planned to be a “tentpole moment” from the start, but we kept putting more effort into it when it was shaping up to be something very special.But most setpieces in the game were planned to be such from the early stages of pre-production. In Resonant, we wanted to have multiple standout moments instead of a single sequence like the Ashtray Maze overshadowing everything else, and I’m happy to see that players have different favorites.And a different approach from Control is that several of these memorable moments (Sinkhole, Underpass Fault, the JRPG section) are more emotional and subdued instead of bombastic action (although we also have big action setpieces), which was an interesting challenge itself.Was there a fear that the visuals would limit the emotional impact of the sequence?Grönroos: Yes, but we decided to trust the writing. Several other games have managed to deliver a similar emotional impact despite pixel graphics (and sometimes because of them, since the level [of] abstraction can make characters more relatable and up to the player’s interpretation), so we weren’t too concerned. Was there a fear that action game fans would be frustrated by the RPG sequence?Grönroos: Yes, this was the main concern about the sequence. It was a very polarizing sequence in the playtests: people either loved or hated it. We did discuss shortening it significantly, but in the end decided to keep it as it is but added some fast travel options and make some parts skippable. There were more fans than haters, and we didn’t want to sacrifice something we really enjoyed.How concerned were you about the actual RPG mechanics of that sequence?Makkonen: Since Rebuild doesn't feature a vast number of battles, we didn't need to build particularly deep RPG or progression systems. The combat served as an additional storytelling tool – illustrating how bullies torment Dylan, showing his first fight (against rats, naturally), and depicting his eventual abduction by the FBC.Refining the combat to be simple, yet offer the player meaningful choices required a few iterations. In addition to standard attacks, the children can occasionally use special abilities: Dylan delivers a heavy blow to a single enemy, while Jesse fires at everyone. Enemies deal enough damage that defending becomes necessary when multiple foes target the same character.The lightbulbs in The Underpass mission goes in similarly unexpected, animated directions. How did that mission come about? What was the pitch like for that one?Grönroos: That one started with the idea of doing something comically mundane, switching lightbulbs, and adding a paranatural twist to it. The cartoon man wasn’t in the initial version, but instead we were going to do live action videos and have Dylan get stuck in that video as the end twist. They were supposed to be old-fashioned “work safety” videos explaining how to change lightbulbs, and as the quest went on, it became more and more obvious that the maintenance guy in the videos was too aware of what Dylan was doing. This all came out from a one-hour workshop.There wasn’t really a pitch, but rather a back-and-forth like “could we do a quest about Dylan changing lightbulbs in a ritualistic way? We could have these sentient work safety videos explain how to do it and “Dylan could get trapped inside the video.” Much later, when the live action turned out to be a no-go, we pivoted to the current cartoon instead, and that was even funnier than our initial idea.To read Game Informer's review of Control Resonant, follow the link.

Fangs For The Memories: The Best Vampires In Video GamesNEW

For many of us, the weather is finally becoming that cool autumn crisp, the days are getting shorter, and the season for gorging on candy and horror movies is in full-swing. While I may be in the camp that believes there aren’t nearly enough vampire video games, these creatures of the night are well represented in games. So what better time to pay tribute to our favorite bloodsuckers? Show off those pearly white fangs; these are the best vampires in gaming: Astarion - Baldur’s Gate III One of the best companions of the generational RPG by Larian Studios, Astarion’s charm will win over even the coldest of hearts. Players who choose to engage with his storyline are treated to some of the best storytelling Baldur’s Gate III has to offer. Did I mention he’s a rogue class by default, too? It was incredibly fun speccing his build to stealthily one-shot enemies and retain plenty of action points for his vampiric abilities. Rayne - BloodRayne A protagonist of a bygone era, Rayne was the definition of both sexy and deadly. Featuring an outfit that would fit right in an Underworld film, Rayne uses nimble acrobatics to slay and feast on unsuspecting foes such as Nazis and pesky cult members. While she hasn’t had a title since 2011’s BloodRayne: Betrayal, I pray to the vampiric gods that one day Rayne will return. Blade - Marvel's Midnight Suns This one feels like cheating. We all know how badass Blade is from the 1998 film, but his portrayal in Midnight Suns particularly stands out among vampires in games, adding a whole new side of humanity to the notorious vampire slayer. As the player, you have access to all of Blade’s vampiric abilities, which make short work of foes, but you also have the ability to get to know him personally (including his secret crush on Captain Marvel).  Vincent Valentine - Final Fantasy VII Have you ever just seen a character that is the embodiment of cool? That sums up Vincent Valentine in a nutshell. Hell, when Cloud and company first encounter him, he’s in a literal coffin. Aside from the cool aesthetics, Vincent has one of the best backstories in all of Final Fantasy VII and I can't wait to see how it’s portrayed in the upcoming Final Fantasy VII Revelation. Though not technically a vampire, the fact that he’s a genetically modified immortal human with sick taste in goth clothing qualifies him for this list. Coen - The Blood of Dawnwalker One of the more recent characters to appear on this list, Coen is one of the most fascinating vampire characters I’ve come across in all media. Cursed to be a “Dawnwalker,” Coen lives daytime as a human and nighttime as a bloodthirsty monster who’s willing to kill anyone to feed his bloodlust (if he gets hungry enough). With a story centered on trying to save his family, Coen’s decisions influence his humanity and how others perceive him.  The Blood of Dawnwalker may be one of the newest games on this list, but I can’t wait for a sequel to see more of the human/vampire balancing act that developer Rebel Wolves has created. Alucard - Castlevania: Symphony of the Night Perhaps one of the greatest characters on this list, Alucard is the son of Count Dracula himself. Born to a human mother, Alucard retains his humanity in addition to his vampiric powers and rises to the occasion when his father gets out of line. Symphony of the Night is his most famous adventure and sees him out to stop his father in the wake of Richter Belmont’s disappearance (it's also one of the greatest games of all time). Carmilla - Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse Did you know that, as a literary figure, Carmilla is actually older than Dracula? It's cool that Konami keeps using her in Castlevania games to keep her in public knowledge, and its newest take on the character in Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse might be the best yet. One of the minions who guards Dracula’s castle, her design features her wearing a dress made entirely of human blood and holding a mask based on her original NES appearance. It’s a throwback and modern update that makes her one of the most memorable vampires in recent memory, and her gore-filled boss battle, one of the best in the game. Lady Dimitrescu - Resident Evil Village A vampire created by unconventional means, Lady Dimitrescu is one of the lords of the titular village that Ethan Winters faces off against in the eighth mainline Resident Evil game. Created by the black mold, Lady D grows to gigantic proportions and guards a gothic castle in true vampire fashion. Her design and portrayal broke away from conventional Resident Evil villains and she became a huge hit with internet fandoms. It’s just a shame that Capcom killed her off so soon. Orianna - The Witcher 3: Blood and Wine Orianna is truly one of the most evil vampires on this list. A Bruxa, or vampire lord, that Geralt encountered on his adventures in Toussaint, Orianna had unconventional tastes for a vampire and preferred the taste of children’s blood. To sate her need, she established an orphanage, granting her an endless supply. Her story felt like a Polish fairy-tale take on the tale of Elizabeth Bathory, a real life figure who bathed in the blood of young women in an attempt for endless youth. The Crimson Court - Darkest Dungeon: The Crimson Court I love depictions of vampires that see them as members of the aristocracy, but I also love depictions of them as feral beasts. Thankfully, Darkest Dungeon delivered both in a unique spin. In Darkest Dungeon's The Crimson Court DLC, the bloodsuckers are depicted as noblemen and women who willingly succumbed to vampirism but in their effort for eternal life were transformed into insect-like abominations that the adventuring party must face. It was such a unique take on vampire mythos that it hasn’t left my mind since I’ve played it nearly a decade ago.

Friendslop is everywhere these days. Take one glance at the most recent Steam Next Fest and you’ll be inundated with not only mountains of obnoxious AI slop, but countless games where you and a group of friends can get up to wacky hijinks with cartoonish avatars, over the top physics, and hilarious use of proximity chat. If you’re a developer and your take on this genre goes viral, it has a chance of attracting tens of thousands of players and being a massive financial success--it’s no surprise that friendslop has become so commonplace. But if you fancy gaming on your lonesome for once or sadly don’t have any friends but still want to embrace the chaos of friendslop, then the latest game by Double Fine could be right up your street. Just make sure a bus filled with rowdy passengers doesn’t run you down as you start walking down it.  Thank You Bus Driver is available on Steam for only $5 and it’s a delightfully wacky adventure that does exactly what it says on the tin. You are a bus driver working in a small monochrome city and must drive passengers to their destination. Do so by completing certain objectives like stopping people from eating smelly food or dozing off and you’ll earn a "Thank You" for your troubles. I don’t think falling asleep on the bus is that much of a bad thing (though I suppose it might get you mugged in London).  Your task isn’t just about driving from place to place though, you also need to keep your passengers in line by turning around and smacking them with your freakishly long arms. And if someone refuses to stop, you can easily throw them out the backdoor into oncoming traffic. Lovely stuff. Well, it would be if you didn’t also need to keep driving at the exact same time and reach your next destination before the shift timer runs out. Double Fine has crafted a gameplay formula that is easy to understand yet deceptively hard to master while giving off the very same lunacy I’ve experienced in games like Peak or REPO.  And that's just scratching the surface. Every few stops a creepy dude in a trench coat will come aboard and offer you a selection of gadgets. You can hang an air freshener to eliminate smelly passengers, hang fairy lights or fuzzy dice around the window simply to block your view and cause extra trouble for the added thrill, or down a drink to make your vehicle go even faster--less control, but doing so may allow you to make your destination after suffering a setback. Oh, and be sure to look at your map if you get lost. No, not that long! Keep your eyes on the road as well!  Everything is designed to make your job not only more difficult, but also more fun as you desperately try to adapt to the chaos. I knew Thank You Bus Driver possessed the same infectious charm as my favorite friendslop games when one of my housemates noticed what I was playing and sat right down next to me, laughing in absurd glee as I ran down a cow and let its udders slip down my increasingly overstuffed windscreen. He wasn’t even playing, but not once hesitated to suggest potential shortcuts across meadows or down mountains that could save me a few precious seconds even if my clientele would forever curse the lack of seatbelts on public transportation. Take it up with your local authority, pal.  Friendslop has become such a popular staple for the types of games that friends play together, it can often be easy to forget how, before the genre existed, experiences like Thank You Bus Driver could draw in spectators and make them feel just as involved in the action. Just by being there, those onlookers have an impact and improve the overall experience of playing the game.  I’m eager to keep playing Thank You Bus Driver to earn new town variants, weird unlocks, and passengers who will test my potential for vehicular manslaughter. And I'm sure a few of the people I live with will be just as eager to watch me do it. I doubt I’ll wind up playing this game for a long time, but I’m damn sure having a good one.

Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Activision Developer: Infinity Ward Release: October 23, 2026 Rating: Mature Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 arrives at a crossroads for the series, thanks in large part to the inconsistent quality of recent entries. With a shaky predecessor, Infinity Ward had a lot to repair and conclude, especially with 4 set to bookend the rebooted Modern Warfare series. Fortunately, backed up by a fun campaign, thrilling multiplayer, and loads of new modes and content, Modern Warfare 4 makes up for the past and then some. Modern Warfare 4 continues after the events of 2023’s Modern Warfare 3, with Captain Price hellbent on stopping Makarov and his higher-ups. The story features shifting perspectives, from a new group of ragtag Korean soldiers to familiar characters like Gaz. The character dynamics harken back to the original trilogy, especially in the boots-on-the-ground missions with the South Korean unit, led by Park (played by Beef actor Young Mazino).For a series that, at least recently, has been more spectacle than story, giving the stakes the time they deserve is nice. While it moves away from the stealthy and modernized elements, it makes sense for the matters at hand. The majority of Modern Warfare 4’s attempts at wrapping up the overarching plot of the rebooted series works, but it rushes through some character arcs and story beats in uneven fashion. Furthermore, much of the game’s cast, especially Mads Mikkelson’s Archer and Prisca Kim’s Ri Ju-Young, are underutilized, which is a shame since they both deliver great performances.  Despite the shortcomings of the campaign, Call of Duty's bread and butter, multiplayer, still delivers. While it doesn’t have the fluidity of Black Ops’ newer entries, the customization and design of weapons and maps make it worth digging into. From small, intimate shooting galleries with Mumbai and Nautical to wide free-for-alls like Coal and Reactor U92, the range helps maintain excitement, even if it gets repetitive over time. The variety of multiplayer modes keeps things fresh on the battlefield. Returning modes like standard Team Deathmatch and Kill Confirmed continue that enjoyable competitive flair that made them the stars that they are. However, new modes like Combat Outpost and Hijack fit right into the already great lineup. In particular, Combat Outpost upscales your typical 6v6 player count to 24v24, leading to more action and teamwork. Hanging onto a team’s tank, shooting enemies from afar, and taking back outposts is exhilarating, even if it can all go bad moments later by a well-placed RPG shot.Even after hours of gameplay, the variety of options to craft your perfect gun is impressive. Figuring out the best scope, ammo, stock, and muzzle, as well as the advantages and disadvantages of them, is a fun way of customizing your playstyle. Gunny, a mechanic that helps optimize your gun for certain styles, can help with decision paralysis, but I rarely used it. Not many FPSs go that far and Modern Warfare 4’s commitment to the feature makes it stand out. The newest mode, DMZ, impresses out of the gate. After a few flat attempts in the past, this is the definitive Call of Duty extraction shooter, whether you play solo, or in duos or trios. Immediately, you’re thrust into the wide open world of Hajin and left to fend for yourselves with what you have and what you can gather and extract. Fans of Arc Raiders and Marathon should feel right at home as the gameplay borrows from the typical extraction shooter experience. Intense, action-packed moments keep you coming back after every win or loss. And the excellent gunplay and level design elevate it into something worthwhile, no matter how you play.  Despite not encountering many live players during my time in Hajin in DMZ, it still proves challenging, even for seasoned pros. The world is not your friend, and taking risks can come at a high cost. A good team, as well as a thoughtful loadout and traits, are required to survive. The unpredictability of it all keeps you on your toes, making for an exciting addition to the already strong multiplayer suite.Regardless of the mode, Modern Warfare 4 is one of the best-looking Call of Duty titles to date. New York and Paris are lifelike, and it truly embodies the realistic, modern aesthetic the series has always aimed to achieve. However, I encountered a few technical hiccups, especially in the campaign. Slow framerate plagues some high-octane scenes and texture loading is frequent across the board. These distractions are far from game-breaking, but enough to be noticeable. While the plot execution is a bit shaky, engaging multiplayer options, including DMZ, aptly demonstrate that Infinity Ward can still deliver compelling military-FPS experiences. Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 is a strong return to form that serves as a solid sendoff for the rebooted subseries. Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
