Why Are Fortnite’s Player Numbers Falling So Hard This Season?NEW
Ever since Fortnite started publicly posting player numbers back in 2023, the doomsayers have come out in full force any time there's a decline in concurrent or average player numbers. We're in exactl...
Ever since Fortnite started publicly posting player numbers back in 2023, the doomsayers have come out in full force any time there's a decline in concurrent or average player numbers. We're in exactly one of those situations right now, as player counts for the game's main mode, Battle Royale, have fallen significantly since Chapter 7 Season 4 started on August 14. After the summer season saw a big bump in engagement, thanks to some combination of the sprite-focused gameplay and the nearly entirely new map that Epic dropped at the start of Season 3, player counts have been heading back towards the sort of numbers we saw in Season 1 and 2. In other words: Not great, Bob! According to fortnite.gg, which collates players numbers from the Fortnite API, the average number of players in BR is down 37% in September from August so far. We had the usual big spike of players right at the start of Season 4 in the middle of August, but the falloff has been steady ever since, and the "Fortnite is dead" crowd has emerged once again. But what's going on, really? Is Fortnite dying, or is this just the usual seasonal ebb and flow of player traffic? It's not an easy question to answer from the outside, even with the data we have. To get a better idea of what's going on, we looked at four potential factors. School's back This is one of the more obvious factors we could blame–the kids and college students who had lots of free time to game all day during the summer are now busy with school again. Typically, though, the biggest difference in terms of player numbers when comparing the summer versus. the rest of the year is not that more people are playing, but that they're playing at all hours instead of being concentrated at peak times. For most of the year, there's a big difference in the number of concurrent players between peak hours and the middle of the night, but during the summer, the highs and the lows each day are closer together.  So being back in school doesn't seem to stop people from playing--it just makes them play more in the evening. The data we have for past fall seasons supports this idea, as average numbers largely stayed flat between the summer and fall seasons in 2023, and the averages took a nice jump up for the Marvel-themed Chapter 5 Season 4 in 2024. Player numbers have fallen pretty hard both in Chapter 6 Season 4 and in this year's gaming-focused Chapter 7 Season 4, but it seems like we'll need something other than school schedules to blame for that. Keeping the sprites Fortnite introduced sprites as the main gameplay gimmick in Chapter 7 Season 3, and since player engagement was way up, Epic decided to keep them around in Season 4. This is pretty much unheard of in modern Fortnite, where each season introduces some major new tweak to the Battle Royale formula to keep it fresh, before shuffling it out the door for something new when the season ends. It's not hard to understand the thinking there, however. Fortnite's player counts had been dropping steadily during Chapter 7, to some of the game's lowest numbers ever in Season 2. Then, the sprite-collection gameplay was added for Season 3 alongside a new map, and player numbers went back up. Clearly, the people liked that mechanic, and it was something the powers-that-be could point to as being "something that works."  But there's a reason they basically never keep the same gimmick two seasons in a row–by the end of the season, players are usually over whatever the bit is and ready for something new. That was certainly the case even with sprites as we got to the end of Season 3, but Epic pushed ahead with a new batch of sprites anyway.  Anecdotally, players in my matches rarely seem to engage with the sprite-collecting mechanics in the new season--I rarely hear extractors going off in matches anymore, and my random-fill teammates and I tend to treat the sprites as just another type of loot to find rather than a core mechanic. It wasn't like that at any point during Season 3, even in my overly sweaty matches--folks were grinding those sprites to the very end last season. Too many gimmicks Season 4 has a bit of an identity crisis, because it introduced an entirely new season gimmick while also keeping the one from last season. In a normal season of Fortnite, the new map changes, the override consoles--which let players change the rules of the game mid-match--and the new loot would be more than enough to shake things up. But in Season 4 we have all that and a new set of sprites, each with its own new ability. Having two seasons' worth of gimmicks at the same time has made Season 4 feel harder to comprehend, because it's so unnecessarily busy. With so much going on, it's easy to lose a fight without really understanding what happened or why. It's exactly the kind of situation that overwhelms a lot of players, which then inspires a new generation of Redditors to claim everyone who beats them in a fight is cheating because they're frustrated. And since the sprite-collection gameplay is more appealing to those casual players who are put off by the overly complicated gameplay, Season 4 ends up feeling overstimulating and lacking cohesion.  We saw the same thing happen in the Helldivers-like Chapter 6 Season 4, when the map was filled with alien bugs that made it more difficult to track other players. The numbers fell pretty hard that season, too. They've been relying too much on engagement farming  In 2026, Fortnite has been trying some new techniques for drumming up engagement, the most notable being the Power Hours the game runs in Battle Royale each weekend, which adds some wacky quirks to gameplay, like big head mode, and makes it easier to find and extract sprites. It's essentially a marketing tactic to coerce people to play at a specific time each Saturday so they can get a nice big spike in the concurrent player count, and drag the average player numbers for the rest of the week upward a little bit. But that sort of thing is a bald manipulation, a new layer of FOMO on a game that already has too many of them. At some point, this kind of thing can easily change from being a nice bonus to feeling like an obligation, especially if they continue to center these events around sprites, which--again--was last season's gimmick, instead of coming up with new ideas for them. Maybe we've already reached that point--the August 29 and September 5 Power Hours were the only ones to draw under a million players since the beginning of June, when Epic first started doing them. All this means that the rest of the year will be pivotal for Fortnite. There are several big opportunities for the numbers to recover in the next few months--Fortnitemares in October, the expected mini-season in November, and the start of Chapter 8 in December. The last time Fortnite was able to generate a big, sustained spike in players was in 2023, with the original OG season and the start of Chapter 5. But if Epic wants to pull off that sort of recovery this year, especially with the juggernaut that is GTA 6 looming in November, it'll have to come up with something genuinely special that people actually want to play without having to be coerced into it.

Are you planning to disappear into Vice City when Grand Theft Auto 6 arrives in just over two months? You’re not alone and doing so definitely isn't uncommon. For franchises like Call of Duty, which receives new titles on an annual basis, this theoretically adds up to a lot of time off. GTA games, however, are so infrequent that it’s almost become a generational event for one to arrive. It’s only natural that some players don’t want to wait any longer than necessary to experience it. AAA games are more than just singular luxury purchases. They’re an investment of time that the vast majority of people can’t easily make without cutting into their normal lives. That’s why a growing number of gamers have figured out that one of the best ways to fully experience a title like GTA 6 is to make sure you’ve already carved out some time for yourself to play it. Even if that means clearing it with your job and significant other before picking up a controller. Bud Burruss--a video game streamer and married father of three--spoke with The Wall Street Journal and noted that he’ll be taking a five-day vacation before Thanksgiving to devote to the game. “We’ve got a couple of days where we’re like, ‘Daddy’s very busy,’” Burruss told WSJ. “I’ll have the cave door shut.” Although Burruss noted that he told his wife about his plans a year in advance, his GTA 6 staycation has been mocked by some online. “His wife has four children,” wrote a poster on Facebook. “Our men are children,” added another. If you worked your ass off to afford the time off and squared things with your family or significant other, then why shouldn’t you take some vacation time to play GTA 6? Its $80 price is far from cheap, but it’s a lot less expensive than an actual vacation. And if it’s something you enjoy, then the investment of time and money is worth it. It’s not as if the real world hasn’t noticed all of the excitement surrounding the game. Last month, it was reported that the 9th Engineer Battalion in Fort Stewart, Georgia offered soldiers a reenlistment perk--four days off to play GTA 6--if they re-upped their contracts with the army. Thankfully, most of us don’t have to go to the same extreme to set aside some time off.  Grand Theft Auto 6 may or may not be a once-in-a-lifetime game, but it’s anyone’s guess when another installment in the long-running series will arrive. If GTA 7 is a decade or more away, then there’s an even greater incentive to enjoy the one that’s finally on the verge of release. Regardless of how many players take a time out from their lives in November, we can safely predict that the crime rates in Vice City are going to go way up. We’ll see you on the mean streets.

8 Big Changes That Prove The Ocarina Of Time Remake Is More Than A Link To The PastNEW
Nintendo has finally revealed its The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake with a lengthy gameplay demonstration and luscious release date trailer. This long-awaited revival of the seminal masterpi...
Nintendo has finally revealed its The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake with a lengthy gameplay demonstration and luscious release date trailer. This long-awaited revival of the seminal masterpiece looks incredible, and is poised to be a very faithful take on Link’s first 3D adventure.  But this dedication doesn’t mean that Nintendo isn’t introducing a bunch of new features to the game or updating aspects of the experience so it looks and plays better than ever. Here are eight major changes coming to the Ocarina of Time remake and why they might impact how the game looks, plays, and departs from the 1998 masterpiece. The jump button changes everything  In the original Ocarina of Time, and several iterations of Zelda afterward, there was no dedicated jump button. Instead, Link simply had to run toward a ledge and hopefully use this momentum to land on a nearby platform without plummeting to oblivion.  While the game was ahead of its time several decades ago, the lack of a jump button is awfully frustrating to anyone hoping to revisit it in 2026. Unless the angle was just right or you had wrestled with the camera ahead of time, there was always a chance of Link accidentally yeeting himself in the wrong direction. You could die or end up back at a much earlier point in a dungeon all because the controls refused to play ball.  The remake is going to be much more fun to play in the moment with a jump button that offers more control over Link’s movement, not to mention when trying to reach treasures that sit just out of reach. Heck, I wouldn’t say no to a platforming challenge or two.  Free camera movement transforms our vision of Hyrule While Ocarina of Time didn’t have a completely fixed camera like Resident Evil or Dino Crisis, the original game did limit exactly how the player could view certain locations. It prevented the player from moving around some of its three-dimensional spaces with an absolute sense of freedom, meaning places like Castle Town or the Temple of Time housing the Master Sword were designed to be viewed from one perspective alone.  The remake removes that restriction entirely and gives players the freedom to move the camera around however they like in presumably every single location in the game. This really excites me, and should utterly transform how we approach places like Castle Town, which previously left so much up to the imagination. With any luck, Nintendo will fill these additional nooks and crannies with secrets that weren’t present in the original game.  Threads of Time adds more depth to Link's journey One of the major new features introduced by Nintendo for the remake is Threads of Time, which on the surface kind of sounds like a glorified quest log. It’s described as a means of figuring out where to go next and checking how far you’ve progressed in the story. Ocarina of Time isn’t the most complicated game in the world, but it also didn’t tell you where to go outright back on the Nintendo 64, so it makes sense to introduce something that does.  We’re used to AAA games holding our hands and chances are this remake will be the first time millions of players have experienced the classic, so why not modernise it? The minimap is also significantly more detailed this time around, making it easy for Threads of Time to pop down a pin and show players exactly where their next objective is. I’m most excited for some of the additional flavour text alongside the visually arresting tapestry that slowly builds out as you progress. If you’re a lore nerd, this is one new feature worth paying attention to.  Castle town will invite more exploration than ever before I’ve always considered Castle Town to be the core location in Ocarina of Time. It's a hub Link will return to again and again as the plot progresses in order to see exactly what has changed as he picks up new items, meets new characters, and watches how the passage of time comes to ravage a once bustling bastion of civilization. But the aforementioned fixed camera angles made it difficult to appreciate sometimes, not to mention the size of Castle Town was also a smidge limited.  In the remake however, Nintendo has confirmed that it will be bigger than ever. Parts of town that were once pre-rendered backdrops are now living, breathing spaces with new faces and dialogue that add an extra layer of depth to what was already one of the game’s densest locations. It’s described as a place where you can take a break during your adventures, and to me this suggests that the game will actively encourage you to revisit Castle Town several times to see exactly what’s changed and whether new avenues of exploration have opened up.  Listening to gossip and playing minigames gives the game more life Speaking of Castle Town, the expanded dialogue in the remake also extends to the NPCs there, who will often possess new gossip for Link to listen in on or minigames that weren’t present in the original game. We don’t yet know what these are, but Ocarina of Time loves forcing Link to catch chickens or jump into a shooting range, so expect more things along those lines.  I’m also willing to bet this improved world building will also extend to the Goron and Zora towns as well. Can we expect expanded map design and additional supporting characters there too, with a smattering of gossip and minigames to discover that will offer those who have played through Ocarina of Time countless times over the years a reason to care? It would make a whole lot of sense.  Voice acting is going to have a massive impact on the narrative As expected, Link will not have a voice in the Ocarina of Time remake that goes beyond excessive grunts and triumphant cheers, but the same can’t be said for everyone else. In quite a surprising move, it seems the majority of characters will be fully voiced and come with updated dialogue to boot. We only hear a handful of performances in the gameplay demonstration and reveal trailer, but the delivery seems quite similar to Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom. Navi sounds endearing with the potential to become incredibly grating, while Zelda has the adorable naivety of a monarch in way over her head. It feels like a modern anime dub with the enthusiasm of a Saturday-morning cartoon. With the original game lacking voice acting entirely, it goes without saying that the remake adopting it so passionately is going to bring substantial pacing changes to the narrative. Perhaps we’ll spend more time with some of the characters or even receive greater depth and context to certain events?  Dashing could risk making its open world feel small While it blew our socks off back in 1998, compared to modern games, the open world in Ocarina of Time isn’t very big. You can ride across the entirety of Hyrule Field on Epona in just a few minutes, while there isn’t a whole lot to discover on it beyond random enemies and the occasional item drops. It was a means of connecting Castle Town, the neighbouring civilizations, and dungeons in a way that made Hyrule feel real. But having to march across it again and again in order to explore became a bit of a slog, which explains why Nintendo is introducing a dash option for Link. You don’t just run at a relatively slow speed now--you can rush across Hyrule Field with urgency, leaving all the monsters chasing you in your dust. One of my fears is that this will risk making the open world feel small, especially to new players who will come in expecting more.  Maybe it would have been wise to slow down the movement and let Link smell the roses ... Loading screen have changed, but aren't gone Loading screens are here to stay in the Ocarina of Time remake because Nintendo wants to keep the spirit of the original game intact. I understand this approach, since it would be a bit strange going straight from Kakariko Village into the Lost Woods and right onto Hyrule Field. In my head I always imagined there were other locations across Hyrule we simply didn’t see as the player, leaving my imagination to fill in the gaps. By removing loading screens, you’re confirming this illusion never existed.  But the loading screens have been removed in some instances, with Link going straight into local shops in the starting village without opening a door or vanishing into a black void. The world has been seamlessly connected in ways that make sense for the experience without sacrificing immersion. Removing the time it takes to step through a door in a dungeon or go into a building in Castle Town is a smart evolution of the original game that doesn’t change Ocarina into something unrecognisable.

How Exodus Makes Its Choices MatterNEW

The moment one mentions a narrative-driven game, one question springs to the mind of any interested player: Do my choices matter? Video games of all genres are filled with illusory choices meant to make the player feel like they're impacting the world, even when major moments and mechanics are scripted, and that's often not a big deal. But in a game like Exodus, a choice-driven RPG that aims to follow in Mass Effect's footsteps, impactful choices can make or break someone's experience.  I played a few hours of Exodus at developer Archetype Entertainment's Austin offices and came away with a good sense of how the studio intends to give players agency. The systems are mostly what you'd expect from a game like this, but there's one major wrinkle: time dilation. It's a real scientific principle, and the backbone of Exodus' worldbuilding. Essentially, the closer you travel to the speed of light, the slower you experience time. As Jun Aslan, Exodus' protagonist, you'll embark on multiple lightspeed journeys (fittingly called an Exodus) that last months, years, or decades and only experience a few hours, days, or maybe weeks. However, the people back home on the world of Lidon experience time normally, so when you return, a lot will have changed. Your decisions in Exodus can have major ramifications for civilization down the line, and you must live with the consequences of your actions. It's a lens that reframes every decision you make.For example, at the smallest level, you can impact your companions' opinions of you through dialogue. This is pretty standard for RPGs, with pop-ups in the corner of the screen saying "Salt approves" or "Elise disapproves" based on what you choose to say in conversation with them, or with other NPCs while your companions are nearby. You can also choose which companions to bring with you on each mission, giving you more opportunity to raise or lower their opinion of Jun, the protagonist. However, time dilation can impact your relationships in major ways. According to narrative director Drew Karpyshyn, "Not every companion will always be with you at all times [...] And considering what happens when you're gone for years and how these relationships may be maintained is a huge part of the game. It doesn't happen on every mission, but the missions where it does happen – these key Exodus missions that are major points in the story – you have to consider all this."I'm not sure what specific scenarios he might be referring to here, but it's not hard to speculate that there may be decisions where you have to choose which companions to bring with you and which to leave behind. In fact, the damage caused by leaving someone behind like that is the whole pitch of the movie Interstellar, one of Exodus' major influences. It's also possible that you don't get to choose who accompanies you on a journey, and if that's the case, you'll be leaving companions on a planet on the path to a future you've already shaped.To clarify, many major decisions in Exodus involve Remnants, highly advanced pieces of Celestial technology you'll encounter on your adventure. In my playtime, I recovered one called the Logos, which is essentially a highly advanced encyclopedia. Lidon, your home, is a planet built on Celestial technology, and Jun has the choice to either lean into the information in the Logos to build on the planet's foundation or use the Logos to reverse-engineer new tech, making society independent but less advanced. This is what Archetype refers to as a breakpoint decision. You have to pick one of two significant options, and since Jun has some minor precognitive abilities, you can see abstract glimpses of the future before deciding on one or the other. The option to reject the Celestial tech has an image of some dome-like ships, while the option to embrace it shows a preview of some kind of giant snake. Ostensibly, after picking one, I'll see the impact of that choice after an Exodus journey. And if I'm leaving a companion behind, they'll have to live in whichever timeline I've committed to.Jun's precognitive abilities are best seen in the Dynasty tab in the menu, which has a timeline depicting past events and Lidon's ultimate fate. In the early hours we played, Lidon is doomed to be consumed by the Rot, which makes sense – it's the pressure that sets the game in motion. But the screen also shows points earned in three categories, each depicting elements of Lidon's society: green for culture, blue for science, and red for military. Players will face different story outcomes based on each of these levels, and the previews for breakpoint decisions give you a heads-up on which points you'll earn for each choice. All of this is to say that Archetype has injected each breakpoint decision with a lot of variables, and I'm excited about the weight that potentially provides. You're considering the immediate consequences, the long-term story consequences, how it'll impact your Dynasty points, and how it might affect your relationships. It's up to the player to decide which of these factors matter most in the moment, and whether the blowback in other categories might be worth it.However, while my hands-on time gave me a full picture of the factors involved in making decisions, I never saw the long-term results of one. I understand exactly why – Archetype doesn't want to spoil its game's biggest moments months before release – but I can't fully endorse the story's malleability without seeing it for myself.
