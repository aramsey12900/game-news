You know it, I know it, and everyone else knows it. The glut of games coming in Fall 2026 is intimidatingly good. But is that overwhelming wave of exciting titles actually hiding a more gruesome reality? I sure think so. Gaming audiences are no strangers to the rush of fall releases. Even during a slow year, there's almost always an uptick in blockbuster games that launch during this window to best capitalize on the end of the summer and the move toward the winter holidays. This fall is absolutely stuffed with exciting new games around every corner, but it feels more likely for the season to be a bloodbath than a celebration.  In September alone, I suspect all manner of players will feel a bit spoiled. Early in the month, the co-op '80s anime adventure Orbitals will release exclusively on Nintendo Switch 2. On the same day, a very different kind of player will likely dig into The Blood of Dawnwalker, an ambitious vampire RPG with its sights set on dethroning The Witcher series and made by some of its veteran staff. That's some stiff competition just within the first three days of the month. Capcom will launch its return to Onimusha a day later, and yet another asymmetrical multiplayer game--this time starring Michael Myers and centering on the Halloween series--follows soon after. Valheim, an indie survival game that took the world by storm in early access, will drop its big 1.0 update on September 9. A week later, Runescape Dragonwilds--a pretty similar game in all but name--has its big 1.0 release. Uh-oh.  I haven't even gotten to the biggest games of the month. PlayStation will finally release Insomniac's long-awaited Wolverine game about halfway through September, only to be one-upped by what is arguably the largest Nintendo Switch 2 exclusive yet with Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave. In the midst of that, fans of the Legend of Heroes RPGs get the remake of Trails in the Sky 2nd Chapter, and at the very end of the month, a one-two punch of Control Resonant and Silent Hill Townfall will pound lovers of all things scary and deeply weird. Silent Hill: Townfall There are more games of all sorts to come in October, like a new Ace Combat, a Gears of War prequel, a Hellraiser title, Star Wars: Galactic Racer, Modern Warfare 4--not to be confused with Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare--a port of a mobile Final Fantasy, Phantom Blade Zero, and even a collection of Kingdom Hearts games. Hell, we don't have confirmed release dates, but even The Duskbloods and the upcoming remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time are still supposedly due by the end of the year.  Of course, all of these games with actual dates are launching ahead of the biggest title of the year, and maybe even all time: Grand Theft Auto 6, which looks like it'll dominate November, the holidays after, and perhaps the entirety of the next few years.  Much as I want to view this lineup optimistically, it’s hard to see this run of releases as anything but carnage. The games industry has never been more fraught, and now it's about to tear itself apart in a matter of months. Whether a game is good or bad, a hit or a flop, developers and studios are seeing layoffs and even being completely shut down, as parent corporations tighten their belts and watch their stock tickers. All these games are about to go head-to-head vying for a limited number of gamer dollars and eyeballs, which is likely to make all the existing problems in the industry even worse. Grand Theft Auto VI Take, as one example, the fact that Riot Games gave its fighting game 2XKO about nine months before winding down its development. If the support of the publisher behind massive ongoing hits such as League of Legends and Valorant can’t provide a modicum of protection for developers, what is saving any of the studios dropping into this fall's free-for-all? And how are we, as consumers, supposed to reasonably prop up all of that? Now, especially at a time when consumer dollars are being stretched or spent elsewhere, it feels impossible that all of these teams can possibly succeed to the degree they need to in order to survive--or even simply come out of the season unscathed. No matter who wins this fall, it feels like we’ll all lose as a consequence.  Console spending is down due to a worsening global economy and increased corporate spending on AI, which has brought the cost of systems and some games to an all-time high. Anecdotally, most savvy gamers I know--the types to pick up all kinds of games for a breadth of experiences--are budgeting their time and money carefully for the next few months with so many games dropping on top of one another. Even subscriptions such as Xbox Game Pass, which should be a bounty to budget-conscious consumers, are failing to gain any momentum, and in fact seem to be losing their footholds as discourse over ownership and physical media has intensified.  Some of these games are simply going to slip through the cracks, and as a result, the whole space will hurt. Sales forecasts will adjust to account for the most contentious season in years. Some games might perform well or even better than expected. I expect countless more will underperform, though. Publishers and developers will downsize teams as a result, despite the likelihood those teams have put out incredible work. And while massive publishers like Capcom, Square Enix, and Nintendo will be mostly protected from absolute devastation, I suspect smaller developers and publishers--like Remedy, which often skirts the line between AA and AAA--will bear a heavy burden should they come up short at the literal worst of times. And that’s to say nothing of independent studios--teams made up of handfuls of hopefuls trying to make a dent at the same time that Rockstar happens to drop what will likely be one of the most successful entertainment launches of all time. Control Resonant Unfortunately, there seems to be no great solution. Many studios are hemorrhaging as they try to weather this storm the games industry finds itself caught in. Teams can’t simply hold back releases for more opportune release windows, for fear of going under before they can even get their games out the door. Whatever protections may have once existed--lofty publishing deals with Epic Games or contracts to launch titles day one on Game Pass--seem all but gone. It’s everyone for themselves right now, and I fear it's going to bear out in an ugly way come the end of this year. I'd love to be proven wrong. I want everyone to win. But the reality of the situation seems to be that few will. And if the worst does come to pass and the season takes its toll on these talented developers, as it seems has become common, then this bounty of releases will turn into a bloodbath quickly, and all that'll remain at its end is an increasingly broken games industry without a path forward.

Naughty Dog's first effort to adapt The Last of Us for the screen was not as an HBO TV show, but a movie, a fact that recently began doing the rounds again on social media. Like with many Hollywood productions, The Last of Us had a tumultuous, long development period as a movie before it was ultimately abandoned in favor of what is now a hit series.  While not everyone agrees with the creative departures from the source material for the TV series on HBO, it's undeniably a success and one of HBO's biggest hits lately. Let's look back at how The Last of Us originated as a movie and run through the key developments that led to it becoming a TV show. The original movie pitch People across X recently discovered a post from Sony Pictures in 2014 declaring that The Last of Us was "soon to be a major motion picture."  This was a real, studio-sponsored image, not a fan mock-up. There was officially produced key art, showing Ellie wielding a knife. There was a "secret panel" at San Diego Comic-Con that year featuring director Sam Raimi, who was originally attached to produce the movie, having already had huge success at Sony with his Spider-Man films. https://twitter.com/sonypictures/status/492757891229552641?s=46 That The Last of Us was originally going to be a movie is not surprising given how many video game movies came before it and how many have been released since (with varying degrees of success).  This was a year after The Last of Us came out in 2013. It was a hot property, as the game was a critical and a commercial success for Naughty Dog and Sony. With Sony also owning and operating its own film studio, all the synergies were there for Sony to make a Last of Us film. Neil Druckmann, who directed The Last of Us game, confirmed in 2014 that the movie would adapt the story of The Last of Us, as opposed to branching off in some other way. At the time, Druckmann said the movie would "take into account the unique properties of film," but he stressed it was early days and the team hadn't yet mapped out how it would condense the game's story into a two-hour film. In 2015, Druckmann confirmed he had written a second draft of the movie's script, which he said contained "pretty big changes" from the source material. It was also at this time that Druckmann said a table read had already taken place with "a bunch of actors," though he did not name any outright. Game of Thrones star Maisie Williams was said to be a frontrunner to play Ellie, and many people said that casting was perfect. Williams herself confirmed she met with Druckmann and Raimi about it, but remarked at the time that there was no script or director at that point. Williams, who was 17 at the time, also joked at the time that she hoped Sony would make the movie quickly because, "If they make it in 30 years, they can't have a 40-year-old Ellie." Kaitlyn Dever was also considered for the role of Ellie in the film, and, spoiler alert: Dever would go on to play Ellie's adversary, Abby, in the HBO TV show. Development Hell Hollywood productions can take a lot of time to come together, and when the production process goes on for an extended period of time, that's known as "Development Hell." The Last of Us movie was yet another example of this unfortunate phenomenon. In 2016, Druckmann said the movie hadn't progressed for "over a year" at that point. "It kind of entered development hell like these things tend to do," he said. The movie also never had a director signed on, nor was there a cast or a release date. The Last of Us This wouldn't be the last time a Naughty Dog film project was subject to development hell, as the Uncharted movie is a very infamous example of this, but that did eventually come together for release in 2022 after a decade of development. The Last of Us "would have been awful" as a movie Druckmann said in an interview with Sacred Symbols that movie executives told him to "make it bigger, make it more like World War Z," and he pushed back against this. "Go make World War Z. Why make The Last of Us? The Last of Us is not that," he said. Conversely, Druckmann said HBO boss Casey Bloys told him, "'Do as much action as you need to, and no more. Do as much violence as you need to, and no more. Here, we sell drama.'"  Druckmann said he got the opposite notes when The Last of Us was envisioned as a feature film. It's not a stretch to imagine this is part of the reason why Naughty Dog opted for a TV show instead of a film, in addition to there being a more supportive and understanding team at HBO to produce and back the show. Druckmann also said HBO approached the source material with "such reverence and artistry," ensuring Naughty Dog would be involved in the process too. This is not always the case, of course, as the Baldur's Gate TV series at HBO does not involve Larian Studios. Druckmann also mentioned that HBO was supportive of the many changes to the source material that Druckmann and writer/showrunner Craig Mazin made.  Appearing on Logically Speaking in 2024, Druckmann said the movie version of The Last of Us "would have been awful." He said, "It would have been one of those bad adaptations had we finished that one."  "You have two hours, max, and the feedback I got was, 'This will lead it down a bad path,'" he added. In a recent interview with GamesBeat, Druckmann said he changed his mind about how to approach such projects. Whereas in 2014 he said The Last of Us movie would be an adaptation of the game, in 2016, he said Naughty Dog had "evolved our feelings" about adaptations.  "As time goes on, we are not excited by a direct adaptation of a story for a game into a movie,” Druckmann said. “We have told that story well, cinematically. The idea for The Last of Us film was to be a direct adaptation. I don’t want that movie to be made." Bella Ramsey as Ellie. Druckmann indeed got his wish, and that movie was never made. A second season aired in 2025, and was similarly successful, though not quite to the same degree. A third--and potentially final--season is in the works now, but Druckmann dropped out to focus on Intergalactic: The Heretic Prophet and other projects at Naughty Dog (maybe The Last of Us 3?).
