Major League Baseball Is Headed For A Lockout: What Happens To The Video Games?NEW
Major League Baseball's collective bargaining agreement with the Major League Baseball Players Association (MLBPA) ends on December 1, 2026. A new agreement has to be reached prior to the start of the...
Major League Baseball's collective bargaining agreement with the Major League Baseball Players Association (MLBPA) ends on December 1, 2026. A new agreement has to be reached prior to the start of the 2027 season the following spring, and as it stands, there are some very big sticking points in negotiations, particularly around the topic of a salary cap. Should a new agreement not be made prior to the season, we're looking at a lockout, with owners refusing to give players access to their facilities, as we briefly saw back in 2022.  But it feels different now. In 2022, the start of the season was pushed back but a full 162-game schedule was kept intact. This time, the owners (MLB) and the players (MLBPA) seem to be so far apart on the matter of a salary cap that it could cause a reduction in total games for 2027, assuming there's a season at all.  Every year, just prior to the start of the baseball season, Sony releases a new MLB The Show game, bringing with it the latest real-life rosters for all 30 MLB teams. Should there not be a new CBA in place, however, what happens to the baseball video games we expect every year? Will they even release as planned? I wanted answers, not just because I write about games for a living, but because I will undoubtedly be playing hundreds of hours of a baseball game in the event of a lockout that leaves me unable to watch real-life Major League action. Well, to be fair, I would probably do that even without a lockout, but now it's especially important.  MLB The Show 26 So, what could MLB video games look like if they launch before a new agreement can be reached? Last time, the lockout didn't last long enough for it to actually have a tangible effect on The Show, and an agreement was reached to save the baseball season a few weeks before the game's launch.  Sony--honestly, understandably--hasn't responded to my request for comment on its MLB The Show plans in the event of a lockout. These plans could very well still be changing, as the owners and MLBPA continue sending offers and counter-offers back and forth. We can, however, look to another major sports franchise for some insight. I spoke to Matt Bertz, former Game Informer managing editor and current senior VP of Games at consulting firm Magid, to explain how this went in the past. He pointed to a similar situation that happened with NBA 2K12. "That game dropped in 2K's typical October release window, even though the league lockout started in July and extended until December," Bertz said. "The money train needs to stay on the track, and those licenses aren't cheap.  "During that time, updated rosters and rookies were not available until the lockout was resolved," Bertz added. "To help create a draw without having the latest and greatest rosters, [developer] Visual Concepts leaned heavily into NBA history with the NBA's Greatest mode, which replaced the previous release's popular Jordan Challenge. The mode honored the careers of 15 of the league's best players, creating era-specific presentation packages and asking the commentators to wax nostalgic about NBA legends like Magic Johnson and Bill Russell. Given the MLB's storied history, I imagine a similar strategy could be employed by San Diego Studio."  We could be seeing a whole lot of this. There is certainly plenty of historical content that The Show could draw from, as legends of the past--including the Negro Leagues over the last handful of years' games--have been a major part of the experience. But typically, this content exists as a supplement to the live rosters of current players in the MLB rather than the main attraction. Perhaps the game could really lean into the Field of Dreams content, giving baseball fans a chance to see even more greats of yesteryear compete while a never-ending army of fathers and sons plays catch off to the side, but this likely wouldn't have the legs of a traditional full-season content rollout.   As it stands, only Sony knows what San Diego Studio's plans are for the potential lockout. However, even though The Show is the only traditional MLB baseball game on consoles today, it's not the only game with MLB licenses. One major--and very different--alternative is Out of the Park Baseball, a baseball sim that lets you manage your team down to the incredibly fine and minute details. I asked Out of the Park Developments president Rich Grisham back in August about the licensing agreements and the constant changes and uncertainty that come with making an annual sports game, and was a little surprised by the prevailing message: Baseball video game fans shouldn't lose sleep over this, at least not yet.  "I think history is the best guide in terms of video games, and luckily, we've been blessed with Major League Baseball not having a lot of labor issues over the last, you know, 10-15 years. But if you look at history where there have been labor disputes in the past, the video games around the sport, their release schedules didn't change," Grisham told me over a video call. "So I don't think video game baseball fans have any reason to be concerned about their video games. We're not expecting to not be able to make the games or anything like that." Grisham's confidence regarding the 2027 season existing, at least in some form, was a little surprising to me--not just because of the stalemate the MLB and players are in, but because Out of the Park Baseball relies heavily on nitty-gritty details like a new CBA to work authentically. The development team aims to have any changes implemented into the game as soon as possible, but it can't plan for all potential outcomes before the dust is settled.  Out of the Park Baseball 27 "Will there be baseball exactly on time or a little later? You know, that is all up in the air, but there will be baseball," Grisham added. "So, if there is a labor stoppage, you know, we'll do what we do to the best that we can do it, and we'll make sure that the games are as good as they can possibly be, and then, you know, let the chips fall where they may. But we're certainly not going to have two or three different versions of the game." We've seen The Show take an extra year to update all of its modes with rule changes, like when the universal DH was added to Diamond Dynasty one year after it happened in real life, and Grisham says Out of the Park, too, will continue to adapt--just maybe to a greater extent than in other years.  "We're always making adjustments, because even in non-CBA years, there are rule changes, you know? Different taxes come into effect, you know--different rules around the draft. So we're always implementing those in Out of the Park Baseball. As they come out, we then put them in the game."  It will be impossible to immediately implement all the changes that come about from a new CBA, especially if it happens post-lockout and with a shortened MLB season, but existing rules can act as a stopgap measure. "If there's not a CBA come March, then we'll probably just have the rules be the same as they are [now] until we find otherwise. And whatever the new CBA [states], we'll then [implement it] as quickly as possible--within the time allowed, because obviously you've got to put [the rules] in, you've got to test, it, you've got to run a billion simulations to make sure, you know, how it all works. We take it as it comes, is sort of the bottom line."  It's an approach you can see in the most successful baseball teams, weathering the storm of injuries that inevitably crop up over the course of the season, shifting resources and moving around existing players to best compete against the rest of the league. We don't know exactly what the future holds, either for real-life MLB games or their digital counterparts, but we'll have some sort of baseball to enjoy in 2027. Whether that digital version immediately reflects the on-field reality remains to be seen.

Here’s Why Fortnite Sucks This SeasonNEW
There's way too much going on in Fortnite's core Battle Royale mode in Chapter 7 Season 4. With sprites giving everybody a bonus ability, and Overrides fundamentally changing the way the game works in...
There's way too much going on in Fortnite's core Battle Royale mode in Chapter 7 Season 4. With sprites giving everybody a bonus ability, and Overrides fundamentally changing the way the game works in the middle of matches, we've got kind of a large pile of gimmicks that show up in matches at random, if they show up at all. It's made for a disorienting and inconsistent experience that's difficult to strategize for, because there's no real status quo to get used to. It's a big mess, and it seems to be affecting player numbers in a big way, both for the core Battle Royale mode and Fortnite as a whole. The thing about battle royale as a genre is that it's already, by its nature, varied enough from match to match. Loot is random, and the other players are random, and so there's always plenty of variety even if you drop in the same spot every single time. This has been one of the key selling points of the battle royale genre since before Fortnite existed. But at a certain point, adding more randomness doesn't make the game more engaging; it makes it more annoying, because some of these gimmicks change the rules mid-match. Take the Speed Shooter Override, for example, which makes everyone's guns fire much faster and gives Fortnite a lower general time-to-kill (TTK) than it's ever had. You have to change your playstyle when that one is active, and you have to do so on the fly, because it happens in the middle of a match. Making it worse is that Override doesn't come up consistently enough that you can get used to it, unless you play like it's your job. And now that we're deep into the season, and Epic has added lots of other Overrides, Speed Shooter comes up even more rarely, which makes it even harder to adjust to when it does. Heaven help you if your enemies are lucky enough to claim the Squad Speed Shooter, which gives that power-up only to a single team.  While squad Overrides mark the team that has them on the map for everyone to see, those markers don't tell you which Override they took. The only way to find out if they have Speed Shooter is to fight them, and probably die because their TTK is twice as fast as yours is, and you had no way of knowing that in advance. And then you're a little more mad than you usually are when you lose in Fortnite. There are Overrides I like, too, like the Power Pickaxe. That one lets you one-hit-kill anyone with your pickaxe, which I find delightful. But one of the reasons it ends up being so fun for me is that other players don't necessarily adjust their play at all when it comes up. It's an enormous change in the way the game works, and in order to really take advantage of it you'd have to play in a very unintuitive way, so I think a lot of people just don't worry about it. So I may have some easy pickings in those matches, but my victims probably found it just as obnoxious as I find those Overrides that enhance everyone's guns.  These Overrides have all the downsides of the Medallions we saw in past chapters, but once again it's the lack of consistency that makes the current mechanics so much worse than what we had before. Those Medallions were often overpowered, but you knew they were there every single match, and if you defeated somebody who was carrying one, you could loot it off their corpse and take its power for yourself. But neither of those things is true for Overrides, which are a much more capricious mechanic.  Despite these issues, it's not that hard to deduce the rationale Epic was using when it filled Fortnite with all these gimmicks. It's not even that complicated--to a corporate boss or middle manager, "So many different things could happen!!!" sounds abstractly like a great way to keep players coming back. But not all unpredictability is a boon in practice, and this is one of those cases, as players have been making very clear. In September, according to numbers compiled by fortnite.gg, Fortnite's main BR mode saw its second-biggest month-to-month drop (38%) in average players since those numbers became publicly available in 2023. But it's worth pointing out that the record-holder in this case--when we moved from the nostalgia-chasing Chapter 2 Remix in November 2024 into Chapter 6 the following month--the average number of players fell from a very high number (over a million) to a pretty good one (just over 600k).  This time, though, we've gone from August's decent average (537k) to a bad one in September (333k). It's a slightly better percentage drop than we saw in 2024 (38% vs 42%), but a drop like that is a lot worse when it comes from a lower starting point. This mess of gimmicks has had me spending much more time in Reload than in regular BR this season. Even now that Epic has resumed rotating Reload's maps every 20 minutes, Reload is the much more consistent experience. While each of the three maps in rotation (Oasis, Surf City, and Springfield from The Simpsons) has a different loot pool, it's not a big deal because you know which map you'll be playing on when you ready up. You won't be blindsided by a random enemy having some gross unfair advantage that you didn't have the chance to get yourself. You won't have to adjust your playstyle mid-match to account for a fundamental rule change you didn't ask for. Reload is the better battle royale experience, and it's not close. Let me put it a different way: Reload, right now, feels like the real Fortnite, while Battle Royale feels like a desperate, gimmicky sideshow. It's practically a role reversal of the two modes, though I very sincerely doubt it was intended to be.  While Reload is filling the gap for me, that clearly hasn't been the case for everyone. Reload's numbers are way down in September as well, and the average player numbers for Fortnite as a whole are down 28% in September, which is the single biggest month-to-month percentage drop that Fortnite has had since we've been able to track those numbers. Despite the growth of Epic's metaverse, the core BR mode remains Fortnite's beating heart. It's is the reason people install Fortnite, and when those same players delete Fortnite off their drives, it's usually because they got tired of BR. That mode's performance is still a bellwether for Fortnite's overall health. For BR to regain its form in Chapter 8 in December, Epic will need to make some major philosophical changes to its approach, because tossing in as many gimmicks as they can clearly isn't going to cut it anymore.

Irisalis legendary Aniimo Irisalis is the legendary evolution of Iris, and this Aniimo can only be caught during the seasonal Walking with Irisalis event. This is a Nova stage Aniimo with the DPS role and Grass Element type. Irisalis has two skills to use. Irisalis Shadow creates clones of Iris, while Whirling Blossom Rain makes the clones fire floral light beams. Only legendary Aniimo can unlock the transmogrify ability. This allows you to use Radiant Dreamshadow to change the visual appearance of Irisalis. Walking with Irisalis event details Walking with Irisalis event The Walking with Irisalis event is live from now until December 10, giving you over two months to complete the limited-time objectives. This requires collecting Irisalis Petals, crafting a special legendary Aniipod, and defeating Irisalis in battle before you can catch it. How to complete the Walking with Irisalis event The material needed for the Walking with Irisalis event has actually been in the game since launch, so you might already have a good start on this. You'll need a total of 99 Irisalis Petals to craft the legendary Aniipod. If you're just getting started with the game, you'll have a grind ahead of you, but we'll give you some tips on how to get Irisalis Petals easier. How to collect Irisalis Petals There are nine permanent ways you can earn Irisalis Petals: Completing main story objectives (12) Completing Journey Quests (10) Complete Elite Pathfinder challenges (7) Earning Silver Badges (14) Completing Tracing the Trail event quests (5) Alpha First Clears (17) Branch Nurture (18) Completing Aniimo Topics (16) You can get all 99 Irisalis Petals just by completing all the objectives above, but there are additional ways to earn the petals faster. A new weekly rotation of options will be available during the event, allowing you to score several Irisalis Petals quicker. Breezy Mission Letters gift boxes In week one, you can purchase Breezy Mission Letters gift boxes for 428 Glimmer each. The shop currently has a limit of four purchases of this item, but each one will give you an Irisalis Flower. Go to your inventory and select "Use" on the Irisalis Flower. This will give you five petals per flower, giving you a maximum of 25 Irisalis Petals to add to your total count, assuming you buy all five Breezy Mission Letters gift boxes. Irisalis Petals in the Stamp Shop The stamp shop also has Irisalis Petals at a cost of 50 Windborn Stamps each, and you can purchase up to four petals this way. Visit the Walking with Irisalis event tab each week to see what new options are available in the event's weekly rotation. How to craft the legendary Aniipod Crafting a legendary Aniipod for Irisalis Once you have 99 Irisalis Petals, head back to your menu's event tab and select the Walking with Irisalis event. You'll now be able to select the option to craft the legendary Aniipod. You can only craft one of these special event Aniipods, meaning you can only catch Irisalis once. Crafting the legendary Aniipod will unlock the ability to teleport to a rift, and this will take you to Irisalis. You'll now need to defeat Irisalis in battle. Defeating Irisalis Irisalis is a grass-type Aniimo, so you'll want to pick your best team with Dark, Fire, and Wind elements. Also remember this makes her resistant to Earth, Grass, Water. It's worth having a healer in your party as well. Glacy is a water-type Aniimo, but they are a powerful healer to pair with the elementals you'll need. Defeating Irisalis will give you a prompt to catch them with the legendary Aniipod you crafted, and you'll now have the game's very first legendary Aniimo. Few video game openings make the impression City Escape does in Sonic Adventure 2. After jumping out of a helicopter, Sonic uses a piece of debris to snowboard down the city streets as the generational earworm, "Escape from the City" blasts. It was a defining moment for gamers, whether they were playing it on the original Dreamcast in 2001 or GameCube in 2002. It's also one of the best-designed levels in 3D Sonic history. Ahead of its appearance in Sega and iam8bit's 35 Years of Speed: The Sonic Art Exhibit this weekend in Los Angeles, we got ahold of the design document Sonic Team used to lay out just how this iconic level would eventually play out while Sonic Adventure 2 was still in development. Originally codenamed Project GAIA 2, Sonic Adventure 2 had the unenviable task of following up the acclaimed Dreamcast launch title, Sonic Adventure. Not only was that a daunting task on its own, but Sega found itself in dire financial straits as the PlayStation 2 was decimating the Dreamcast in sales. Sega, on the brink of exiting the hardware business, dramatically downsized the development team behind Sonic Adventure 2 and relocated them to San Francisco, California, which heavily inspired the hilly urban design of the game's opening level. "Our team of 120 got pared down to 11, sent to America, and told to go make Sonic Adventure 2," Sonic Team creative officer and Sonic Adventure 2 director Takashi Iizuka told me in 2016. "Part of what we had to resolve was 'How are we going to make the same game with 11 people when we have less than a tenth of the staff on-board?' I was really tasked with trying to make the impossible possible."Regardless of the challenges, Sonic Team, under Iizuka's leadership, was able to clear the hurdles and deliver one of the Dreamcast's most beloved games in just a year and a half of development time. According to several long-tenured members of Sonic Team, including Iizuka, art director Hiroshi Nishiyama, and sound director Jun Senoue, Sonic Adventure 2 was their favorite game they've worked on."It was a small development team so we were able to condense all the good elements from the previous game, and deliver a story and game that was satisfying to players everywhere," Iizuka told me in 2022. Nishiyama followed up with, "I had a lot of new ideas for the game because of all the new stimulus and experiences I got by living in America and was able to make a game with a lot of great moments together with the other members." While Sonic Adventure 2 is often remembered primarily as the game that introduced the world to Shadow the Hedgehog and songs like "Live and Learn," City Escape is up there as one of the most iconic elements of the popular title. The design document showcases various elements of the Hero Side's opening mission, including the aforementioned snowboard sequence, plus the chase sequence involving the massive GUN truck. You can check out the City Escape design document from Sonic Adventure 2 below, and if you need a little accompanying music as you flip through the pages, we recommend hitting play on the "Escape from the City" YouTube video above. If you're in the Los Angeles area the weekend of September 25, you'll be able to see this in person, alongside other historical pieces of art from the Sonic franchise through the 35 Years of Speed: The Sonic Art Exhibit showcase at ARK/8.
