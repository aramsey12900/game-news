A Sentient Snowman Is (Surprisingly) My Favorite Part Of This RPG RemakeNEW
At this year's New York Comic Con, I met with Square Enix to check out two of its upcoming RPG projects. One was Final Fantasy VII Revelation, a game firmly planted in the modern era of video games wi...
At this year's New York Comic Con, I met with Square Enix to check out two of its upcoming RPG projects. One was Final Fantasy VII Revelation, a game firmly planted in the modern era of video games with top-of-the-line visuals and all the trimmings of a AAA, big-budget game.  The other was Romancing Saga 3: Destinies United, a remake of a classic turn-based RPG from the mid-1990s. At first glance, the remake's updated visuals and faster gameplay are easily apparent draws for newer players, but that doesn't change that this game is built around the core of the original Romancing Saga 3, an RPG that calls back to a bygone era for the genre. Remaking a classic like this is a balancing act, but based on my brief time playing the game, it seems like Square Enix is on track to unite the best aspects of the two wildly different eras of RPGs.  Flurry the snowman A Flurry of action The demo takes place in Gwayne's Den, the home of a massive dragon terrorizing the nearby Louv Peninsula. I take control of party leader Khalid and head down the mountain path, where I spot an enemy not paying attention to my approach. By sneaking up to the foe and pressing X, I can stealthily swing my sword at it, and, if I connect, I launch a Preemptive Strike--this starts the ensuing battle with every enemy having lost a bit of their hit points. It's not a massive chunk of health, so I didn't feel too bad if the enemy proved difficult to sneak up on, but the head start always felt good when I did pull it off.  Battles play out in classic turn-based style, with a turn-order indicator situated at the top of the screen. Most of my party is ready for battle, with leader Khalid, fairy Peony, fighter Ellen, and masked swordsman Robin sporting a full kit of weapons and abilities. There's a fifth party member, Flurry the actual snowman, who's underpowered compared to the rest, but it still packs a wallop with its punches.  Also, just to make clear what I said just now: Yes, Flurry is an honest-to-goodness snowman, its body made of a small orb of white snow on top of a larger orb of white snow. It can punch, they can cast spells, and it quickly became one of my favorite parts of the demo. I need to learn more about Flurry. Each party member brought a different battle approach to the table, and it was fun figuring out each character's nuances. Khalid is a standard swordsman with water-based attacks who fared well against the Draconian enemy, while Ellen's electric-charged punches and kicks made her the best option for direct damage. The Glimmer mechanic in action Always a Glimmer a hope One of the unique traits to this battle system is the Glimmer mechanic, which sometimes replaces a chosen ability with a similar and more powerful ability. For example, I chose the basic Spear Attack ability for Peony, only for a lightbulb symbol to appear over her head, followed by a new Giant Swing attack. What's more, I gained access to Giant Swing for the remainder of the demo, and it proved valuable in the later boss fight.  While the idea of randomly receiving buffed abilities through Glimmers isn't always convenient, I do like the concept, as a well-timed Glimmer can help turn the tide of a lost battle with the new ability it unlocks. Also, when the light bulb does appear, the jolt of excitement in the discovery of a new ability is pretty cool. Romancing Saga 3 also uses a formation system, where party members can be positioned in particular ways. Placement on the battlefield matters during a fight, as some attacks can score multiple hits by moving in a straight horizontal or vertical line, while others are area-of-effect attacks that damage everyone standing in the danger zone.  I like this wrinkle to the turn-based scheme--not only does it deepen the complexity of combat, but it gives me an extra bit of agency in terms of how to position my characters best for battle and rewards me for thinking beyond just how my party can attack or defend. This means that the remake doesn't follow the traditional "weaker characters in the back, stronger in the front" format seen in a lot of RPGs either, as the vertical-line attacks can reach the back row with no issue. It's not exactly novel, but it helps the remake stand out against its modern contemporaries. The demo offered a few formation examples, from a straight horizontal line to one resembling the five sides of a six-sided die, and I wonder what other formations the game will have in store.  Formations Gwayne Over Eventually, I get to the bottom of the pit, and I find Gwayne, a black dragon with massive wings laying on top of a pile of gold and treasures. He's a Smaug … sorry, smug individual, goading my team with insults until it's time for battle. Gwayne is one tough customer, with attacks that hit multiple party members at a time, along with an energy bolt move that damaged Khalid for one-third of his total HP.  However, I had one more trick up my sleeve: Overdrives, which pair two of my party members together for a single attack that inflicts big damage. The Overdrive meter at the bottom of the screen charges as I deal damage, and eventually I can activate it to send two of my party members in for an attack at once.  I saw Khalid pair with Ellen, Ellen with Peony, and Robin with Flurry before the battle was over, and each one added a little more excitement to the fight. Eventually, Gwayne was felled, Khalid picked up what looked like a scale from the ground in front of the treasure cache, and the demo was over. Romancing Saga 3: Destinies United offers classic turn-based RPG gameplay from a long-gone era, but does so with enough modern tune-ups that it feels like it can fit right in with the current role-playing offerings. Then again, not many of those other games have a fighting snowman, so perhaps standing out from the rest of the RPG pack will be snow problem.  We'll find out when Romancing Saga 3: Destinies United launches in February for PlayStation 5, Switch, Switch 2, Xbox Series X|S, and PC.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) Preview - A Beautiful Step Back In TimeNEW

Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Nintendo Release: November 5, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ For nearly 20 years, The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time was my favorite game. While it was eventually supplanted by The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, Ocarina of Time remains a formative, treasured gaming experience that laid the foundation for the wider 3D action genre. However, I am the first to admit that while the original Nintendo 64 version – the only one available on Switch and Switch 2 – has aged better than most games of its era, much like its iconic hero, it doesn't take long to realize it's from a bygone era. For years, I wanted Nintendo to bring the 2011 3D remaster to Switch, but it's doing me one better: a full, from-the-ground-up remake with lush visuals, several quality-of-life improvements, and even voice-acted cutscenes.  We are just weeks away from the launch of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time on Nintendo Switch 2, and ahead of its November 5 release, I spent over an hour exploring Hyrule as both Young and Adult Link. My time begins the moment I take control of Link after Navi wakes him up. Following a brief conversation with Navi and Saria, who also ran up to Link's treehouse, it's time to retrieve the Deku Shield and Kokiri Sword so Mido will let me past to see the Great Deku Tree. As increasingly endearing as Saria is thanks to her improved facial and body animations, as well as her more fleshed-out dialogue, Mido is just as increasingly obnoxious; I was definitely happy to make him eat his words after he mocked Link for previously being the only one without a fairy.Running around Kokiri Forest is nearly enough to sell me on this remake. The visuals are absolutely stunning. The new jump button and ability to sprint out of a roll make it feel like an all-new game. The freely controlled camera adds to this feeling, even with some relatively basic level design. And while I never had a problem with how Young Link looks in this remake, I'm even more impressed and comforted by seeing him in action in the actual game. In fact, everything surrounding Link also looks great; Kokiri Forest is much more bustling than its previous forms, light fog dissipates as you run through it, and swinging your sword looks better than ever before. Though I love Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom's stylish anime-inspired art style, the sheer amount of detail present in each and every character, enemy, and environment accentuates the masterfully executed maximalist approach Nintendo took with this remake. After finding 40 rupees to buy a Deku Shield, I crawl through the tunnel to the area where you get the Kokiri Sword. This time, however, there's added verticality as you dodge the rolling boulders to reach a ladder, which you climb and then jump across a gap to reach the chest that contains the blade. This is indicative of small changes dedicated fans will find scattered throughout; I didn't uncover anything monumentally different from the original game, but eagle-eyed viewers of last month's Nintendo Direct will notice that Lon Lon Ranch is now on level with the rest of Hyrule Field rather than up on a plateau, and that Kakariko Village is now slightly rearranged. Outside of some small upgrades to the diversity and placement of foliage, I didn't notice many more differences, but it's also been several years since I last fired up either the N64 or 3DS version of Ocarina of Time, so it's entirely likely they were subtle enough that I didn't notice. After entering the Great Deku Tree and completing the first part where you jump off the ledge and break through the spiderweb, I must jump to the next part of the demo. Still as Young Link, I watch as Saria gifts Link the Fairy Ocarina through a touching, fully voiced (outside of Link, obviously) cutscene. From there, I'm dumped into Hyrule Field. It doesn't feel nearly as impressively endless as it felt in 1998, but it is definitely way more beautiful. After a brief conversation with the owl, Kaepora Gaebora, I sprint across Hyrule Field, catching passing yet impressive glimpses of landmarks like Lon Lon Ranch and Death Mountain. One new addition is apparent as I navigate the menus: The Threads of Time. Here, a tapestry tells the story of the Hero of Time in real time; I can look back at what has already happened in Link's adventure, as well as see how much is still on the horizon. Additionally, if you get lost, the tapestry can give hints for where you're supposed to go. After a brief exploration of Hyrule Castle Town, including a short tour of the Temple of Time, it's time to jump ahead to the final segment of my demo: the Forest Temple. This section lets me try out Adult Link, who is equipped with the hookshot, and later, the Fairy Bow. This was the one place where my memory felt fuzziest, as I struggled to speedrun the puzzles that I last played through more than a decade ago.Fighting Wolfos, Stalfos, and Skulltulas is more straightforward than most modern third-person fights, which may be the one area that fell slightly short of my admittedly lofty expectations. Using the lock-on targeting allows Link to dodge and counterattack, but I didn't find a simple way to swap targets when you're fighting multiple enemies at once. Instead, I had to release the left trigger, move the camera, and squeeze the trigger again. I get that this is how it worked in the original game, but I would have liked switching to the next target without going through that. Maybe there is an easy way to accomplish that, but I didn't find it in my nearly 90 minutes fighting through Hyrule. Regardless of that one reservation, there is no doubt in my mind that The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time will consume my life when it comes out. And with October already halfway in the rearview, we really don't have long before a whole new generation gets to experience what could very well be Link's greatest adventure. This remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time comes to Switch 2 on November 5.

Castlevania: Belmont's Curse Review - Worthy Of The WhipNEW
Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S ...
Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: Konami Developer: Konami, Evil Empire Release: October 15, 2026 Rating: Teen Castlevania has been a dormant franchise for years, but Belmont's Curse revives the series with fiery passion. There are hundreds of indies inspired by Konami's gothic vampire-hunting games, putting a lot of pressure on the publisher to live up to its far-reaching legacy. Konami's surprising, yet effective solution is to bring in a new developer: Evil Empire, best known for its work on the Castlevania-inspired Dead Cells. The resulting game has a fresh perspective, a brand-new protagonist, and whip-smart execution. Rose Belmont and her father Trevor have been summoned to Paris, a city under supernatural siege. Rose is eager to prove her worth, partially to impress her father, who killed Dracula 23 years ago, and partially because she wants revenge for her bewitched arm, a curse from Dracula, passed down from her late mother. It's a solid premise, and while the narrative is not particularly complex, Rose's character arc has standout moments that will stick with me.For newcomers, Belmont's Curse stands alone perfectly fine, though it is technically a sequel to Castlevania III, which the first two seasons of Netflix's Castlevania are based on. Arguably, it's a better sequel to the show than the game; the art style and character designs have clear anime inspirations, and some voice actors reprise their roles. I enjoyed seeing them in a new light, especially as they passed the torch to Rose. The best example of this torch-passing is a deck of tarot cards, gifted to Rose by her late mother, Sypha. It's my favorite mechanic in Belmont's Curse. Each is a specially crafted vessel to hold the power of Paris' most powerful figures, friend or foe. As you approach each target, Rose will gather clues from her surroundings to aid in hunting them down or besting them in battle. This investigation ranges from just-okay, linear moments in the early game to cryptic, map-spanning missions in the late game. Creatively designed quests to chase down the last handful of tarot cards were my favorite moments in the whole game.Once you find your targets, you absorb their souls into a tarot card, allowing Rose to speak with them. What starts as a lonely journey becomes almost like a road trip, as Rose has a packed backseat of antagonists and allies who can chime in whenever they see fit.Each card also unlocks a spell, ranging from fireballs and a magic cross to a blast turning foes to stone. They also come with a list of Works of Mercy: achievements that upgrade your spells upon completion. These range from "Use This Spell 20 Times" to "Kill 44 Skeletons" or "Find All The Rooms In The Clock Tower." It provides a fun reason to use abilities you might not otherwise, which is especially nice when you become overwhelmed by options in the game's closing hours. I was swayed on more than one spell after achievement-hunting for upgrades. Speaking of combat, the action in Belmont's Curse is magnificent. Rose is light on her feet and only becomes lighter as she gains new abilities, becoming a whirlwind of death by the game's conclusion. Between six weapon categories, a variety of perk-granting relics, and your deck of cards, players are flooded with choice in how to approach encounters. I constantly switched up my loadout, often mid-fight, to adapt to the enemies that appeared. Fights were tough enough to keep me on my toes but never insurmountable, and a forgiving checkpoint system keeps repeated deaths from becoming frustrating. Inventive and intense boss battles against Dracula's coterie of minions are also a major highlight.After 16 hours, I ended my playthrough with 92 percent completion. I just couldn't resist doubling back to earlier zones to see the new areas I could access with my newfound traversal abilities, and Belmont's Curse never failed to reward me. Between two dozen weapons to find, upgrades to health and mana, and even some huge, optional areas, I'm excited for the satisfaction that awaits me as I dive back in to reach 100 percent.Belmont's Curse is a strong reminder of why the Castlevania series is so influential, with a rich world, tense combat, and a map chock-full of secrets. While this latest entry certainly pays homage to the Castlevanias of yore, it also breathes fresh life into the franchise, making me excited for its future. Castlevania is back, and between Rose Belmont and Evil Empire, it's in great hands.Editor's Note 10/13/26 12:18 PM ET: The original version of this review incorrectly stated that Trevor Belmont is voiced by Richard Armitage in Belmont's Curse.
