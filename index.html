Queen’s Domain Has The Most Terrifying Mimic Design I’ve Ever SeenNEW
I thought I was holding my own in Queen’s Domain. It took a while to figure out the basics of movement and combat as I danced around carnivorous ladybugs and hid from fish that loved nothing more than...
I thought I was holding my own in Queen’s Domain. It took a while to figure out the basics of movement and combat as I danced around carnivorous ladybugs and hid from fish that loved nothing more than to dive out of the water in pursuit of my precious derriere, but otherwise my years of experience playing games like Dark Souls and King’s Field meant I always knew what to expect. That is, until I encountered my first mimic.  Considering how many sick surprises this indie adventure loves to throw at you, I should’ve seen it coming. But when every single other chest in the opening area rewarded me with an assortment of new weapons and healing items, surely this wooden vessel atop the cliffside held similar goodies? I was a fool. The second I opened the chest, a pair of bulbous human eyes were staring back at me in ferocious anger, like I’d woken a bear from hibernation. And now, its only goal was to tear me limb from limb.  Queen's Domain's mimic is the stuff of nightmares. Upon waking, a fleshy tentacle protrudes from the bottom of its chest-shaped body and two sharp claws burst from its sides, which the creature then uses to begin crawling towards you. It pursues you with an instinctual urge to destroy that nothing else in the game has had up until this point.  I can handle small insects and the occasional skeleton with ease, but this thing? It needs Jesus. I managed to outsmart it by running up a nearby staircase and jumping straight back down, creating enough distance between that horrific creature and myself that it eventually fell off a ledge and spent the next several minutes trying to find a way back up in order to keep chasing me. That’s the most terrifying thing about this game's mimic--it never stops coming.  How the mimic is presented set my imagination running wild. Perhaps the poor monstrosity stuck inside the chest used to be an adventurer just like me, only I was lucky enough to escape and fight another day while he must spend the rest of eternity in silent agony trying to satisfy an unending hunger.  The introduction of the mimic feels like a point of no return for Queen’s Domain, when its selection of traditional fantasy enemies are slowly but surely replaced with unexpected horrors that only get stranger and stranger the more you progress. Each new area hides a cavalcade of disgusting monsters to be conquered that hit hard, look ugly, and won’t stop chasing you, even after setting them on fire. Trust me, I’ve tried.  Like King’s Field, Dark Souls, and the myriad classics it takes inspiration from and subverts, Queen’s Domain drips with an unsettling melancholy that seeps into every part of its being. The lo-fi aesthetic only adds to the foreboding atmosphere and makes each living thing you encounter feel like a dreamlike figment of your imagination come to life. It’s goofy at times, a quality furthered by the occasionally tongue-in-cheek presentation, but the grim nature of it all always rings true--especially in terms of its creature design.  The crawler, for example, is a four-legged beast that used to be human, but was gradually transformed into a quadrupedal killing machine with an upside-down mouth bearing razor-sharp teeth and a set of blood-red, human-looking pupils that seem to stare through the screen deep into your own eyes. Much like the mimic, it feels like a monster begging for help, or to be put out of its centuries-long misery.  Chickets and Ratbugs are homunculi cobbled together by some unknown force, like the living things that used to call this realm home became the playthings of sorcerers who view the mortal world as little more than a means to an end. Pus Bags used to be human as well, before being struck by magic that turned them into piles of ooze with giant eyes and rotting teeth. You fight hundreds of these creatures in the opening area alone, and it’s fascinating to encounter new variants the deeper into this universe you dare to venture. According to the in-game bestiary, I’ve only bumped into 22.6% of the monsters in Queen’s Domain, but every single variant tells a compelling story. These monsters frighten me, but their intriguingly horrific designs and lore also ignite a fire of curiosity within me that makes it impossible for me to walk away. It is so rewarding in its combat and exploration because you are always discovering something beautifully macabre to sink your steel into.  Rather than using its retro aesthetic to present us with enemy designs we’ve seen countless times in the genre before, developer Freshly Baked Games decided to use Queen's Domain's lack of realistic detail to make hostile entities that blur the lines between dreams and reality. They fascinate and frighten in equal measure, as if something from your deepest, darkest consciousness has stepped out and taken form for the very first time. So far, the mimic has been the most memorable, but I eagerly look forward to being surprised with a new horror down the line.

Going forward, Haven staff will support other PlayStation projects, the report said. "Combined with challenging market conditions in the PVP shooter genre, we have made the difficult decision to cease development on the game.” Creative director Mathieu Leduc said in a blog post that Haven was founded with the goal of making games that its staffers wanted to play, and that’s why the team chose a competitive multiplayer game. “Fairgame$ came from a desire to create a new type of PvP game with emergent sandbox gameplay. We want to deliver a fresh multiplayer experience that rewards creativity and mastery and delivers surprising stories each and every time you play,” Leduc said. “In a nutshell, this is a thrilling competitive heist game where you join an underground movement to rob the ultra-rich and rebalance the scales,” Leduc said. After this announcement, Naughty Dog’s The Last of Us multiplayer game was canceled in December 2023. At the beginning of 2024, though, Sony found its biggest success to date in the live-service market with Helldivers 2–the game went on to smash sales records and become the No. 3 overall best-selling game of 2024 in the US. In July 2024, Sony’s Destiny developer Bungie announced mass layoffs and major structural changes. A month later, Sony launched the multiplayer game Concord. It had such a dismal performance out of the gate that Sony quickly took the game offline and refunded all players. Its developer, Firewalk, was later closed. At the end of 2024, Sony confirmed that it canceled multiple projects, including a live-service God of War and another live-service title from Bend. It was reported in 2023 that PlayStation boss Jim Ryan, who left Sony in 2024, had a mandate that Sony’s teams should make more games-as-a-service titles. A number of PlayStation developers were reportedly upset with this, and the blame was said to have fallen not on Ryan but Connie Booth, one of the most important and veteran PlayStation developers, leading to her eventual departure. Another former PlayStation boss, Shuhei Yoshida, said the chance of finding success in the competitive live-service market was “small,” but his replacement, Hermen Hulst, was given the resources to fund both single-player games and live-service titles at the same time. However, had Yoshida stayed in that role, he said he would have “tried to resist” the push into the live-service market. He joked, “That’s one of the reasons they removed me.”

Australia Did It: End-of-the-World Railway Defense Business Review - Delivering Where It MattersNEW
Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PC Publisher: Mystic Forge Developer: Rami Ismail, Aesthetician Labs Release: November 10, 2026 Australia Did It: End-of-the-world Railway Defense Business is a game I catch myself playing in my head long after I shut it off. Though it uses turn-based strategy as its base, it expands beyond the genre’s regular confines, blending elements from roguelikes, puzzle games, bullet-heaven titles, and the tower-defense genre. The result is an experience that demands wide-scale experimentation and rewards improvisation. In a post-apocalyptic world where the Atlantic Ocean has dried up, you risk your life to ensure your shipment reaches its destination. Riding from station to station by train, you must build your team amid your many missions as various mutated creatures, malicious machines, and coordinated soldiers attack your convoy. Lore is doled out piecemeal and serves mostly as background dressing, but each time I learned more about the world, I became more curious.To survive the harshness of the wasteland, you must use cindermint, an in-game resource gained for surviving turns and killing enemies, to spawn defenders, with three base classes to choose from. The revolver-wielding Gunslinger is your primary damage dealer, while the Radshot applies corrosive radiation damage, and the Nomad offers a close-range shotgun blast with knockback.  Those base-level characters offer solid variety upon which to build. And that’s where the main hook for Australia Did It comes in: experimentation through combination. In the midst of battle, you can merge two units of the same level to create new, more powerful defenders, influenced by the traits of those used in the merge. At the onset, you can merge two Nomads to create a level 2 Nomad with increased damage and knockback, but the real fun comes in combining disparate units to create completely new characters. Even now, more than 15 hours into my gameplay, I’m still concocting new combinations I want to try on my next run.By the end of my first successful run, I had multiple level 6 characters, including a railgun-toting heavy, mages who steal health from enemies and redistribute it to nearby allies, a drone that can move beyond the walls of the station to attack enemies directly, and, my personal favorite, a dual-shotgun-wielding one-man wrecking crew. However, many of these higher-level units also possess drawbacks to consider, like the railgun scorching all adjacent tiles or the drone running out of battery if it’s outside the walls for too long. Though I started keeping notes in my head about which combinations led to my favorite results, I was grateful for a comprehensive compendium that tracked the tens of unit types I unlocked through this rewarding experimentation. The meat of the experience places your squad on a 25-tile square board with enemies spawning from the four principal directions. Thoughtfulness and positioning are crucial, as each unit on your squad possesses different traits and movement efficiency, as do the encroaching enemies. Basic enemies often make a beeline toward the station, while others may take a weaving route or keep their distance and blast you at range. Improvising and adjusting your strategy on the fly is the most engaging part of Australia Did It, and when you perfectly adapt in the moment, or escape the scenario by the skin of your teeth, there are few better feelings I’ve experienced in a game this year. Your goal in this phase is to simply defend the station and gather resources to strengthen your squad before evacuating and moving on to the next area. Considering each unit's strengths, weaknesses, and capabilities while strategically positioning them around the station takes some getting used to, especially during the more chaotic stages. I fist-pumped as I watched the train pull out of a station that was about to crumble, and I lamented the loss of high-level units I had to leave behind to ensure the survival of the convoy. The process is ceaselessly thrilling, even as you begin to see how the gears work under the hood. I just wish I could say the same about the intervening train sections.Once you escape the turn-based strategy portion, your surviving squad members hop aboard the train and chart their route. Using a basic map, you choose your next stop, with factors like enemy types and modifiers shown to you ahead of time. The top-down train segments between station-defense stages are largely passive; the units you evacuated from the previous station automatically attack swarming enemies with the same traits and perks they have in the main gameplay areas. You can aim a focus cone to instruct your units, but you’re at the mercy of the units you’ve brought. You can trigger an ultimate ability to send your defenders into overdrive, but I rarely needed it.While I love the idea of intentionally crafting a team that’s good at both the turn-based strategy and bullet-heaven transport sections, I was never fully engaged getting from point A to point B. Instead, I often caught myself checking how much longer before my train arrived at the next station; these sections can be completed in a minute or two, but that doesn’t alleviate the feeling that it’s a step down from the other gameplay. These novel portions aren’t bad by any means; they’re merely an average part of an otherwise excellent experience.Looking back on the hours I poured into Australia Did It, the forgettable train segments will occupy only a small part of my memories, just as they do in each run. Instead, what will stick with me for months to come are the creative combination mechanics, the outstanding turn-based defense system, and the thrill of uncovering the mysteries of this bleak, yet engaging world.
