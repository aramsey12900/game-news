Why Is Link The Worst-Looking Character In The Ocarina Of Time Remake?NEW
The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake was always going to prove divisive. Whether it strove to be a faithful retelling or a bold reimagining like Final Fantasy 7 Remake, Zelda’s 3D debut is so b...
The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake was always going to prove divisive. Whether it strove to be a faithful retelling or a bold reimagining like Final Fantasy 7 Remake, Zelda’s 3D debut is so beloved, so seminal, and so ingrained in our collective nostalgia that aiming to please everyone was always going to be impossible. Personally? I think it looks just fine.  I was slightly underwhelmed by the fact that, aside from quality-of-life improvements and an essential modernization of certain gameplay mechanics, Nintendo is pretty much poised to deliver a 1:1 remake. The scale of characters, depth of locations, and overall scope of Link’s world is seemingly identical to the Nintendo 64 original. Monsters might be newly positioned and items could be gained in different ways, but it’s clear that the gameplay experience fans are intimately familiar with is being preserved. For better or worse.  Putting gameplay improvements aside though, we need to talk about the visuals. I can’t lie; I got a little emotional when the gameplay demo dropped us straight into a modern take on Kakariko Village, demonstrating the remake’s take on Link glancing around at familiar houses, shops, and friends of the forest he has spent years growing up with. It’s just a shame that our Hero of Time doesn’t look like he belongs in this world at all thanks to his cumbersome design.  There is no denying that Young Link is cute, yet he also feels strangely overdesigned as a way to flaunt both the best and worst parts of this new artistic direction. Link’s piercing blue eyes, rounded cheeks, and emotionless demeanor make him look weirdly out of place, like one of the creepy puppets from Jim Henson’s Labyrinth. This lack of expression worked in the Nintendo 64 original because of the technical limitations at the time, but future versions of Link from Wind Waker, Twilight Princess, and Breath of the Wild understood that even if our hero doesn’t speak, he still needs to act like a human being … or rather, a Hylian. Here, he’s like an aging toy being brought back to life for the first time in decades.  You only need to look at the characters around Link to see what I mean. His childhood friend Saria is introduced early on in the gameplay presentation and looks absolutely adorable, not to mention how she visually matches the remake’s lusciously lit world. Her body language is perfectly attuned to the updated dialogue, while a number of villagers who surround her also feel like they belong in Kakariko Village.  There is one moment where Link dives into a small body of water to collect a rupee, and when he emerges, the liquid gives his entire appearance an uncanny sheen, like he’s some sort of underground mole creature instead of a cherub of the forest.  Such criticisms are impossible to avoid in a remake that wants to remain structurally faithful to a game that came out almost three decades ago but doesn’t want to risk upsetting one of the most passionate audiences in gaming history. Nintendo wants controlling Link to feel like meeting up with an old friend for the first time in years and for the surrounding world to invite familiar curiosity rather than unknown challenge. But this is a foolish task when you’re trying to update the graphics to look better than ever.  Castle Town is where Link feels even more like an outsider, largely because the majority of NPCs are more-or-less identical in design to those of the original game. They are more detailed and expressive, but the consistent proportions and realistic facial expressions make Link seem like he’s been pulled from a completely different game.  They aren’t overdesigned or stuffed with fancy bells and whistles for the sake of it like our forest child, and thus they belong. He looks best when nobody else is around him, and he's standing alone amid the swaying grass of Hyrule Field. With instrument in hand and maximum fidelity in close-up shots, this version of Link doesn’t look bad at all, but removed from that context, it all falls apart.  Many people have already begun comparing the Ocarina of Time remake to Unreal Engine 5 demos made by fans or the cursed viral video of Mario if he were in a modern photorealistic title. I also can’t deny that I have a lot of nostalgia for Nintendo’s masterpiece, so perhaps I was always going to react this way. But older Link looks great, and so do major supporting cast members like Zelda, Ganondorf, and Sheik; so it stands to reason that Link simply possesses way too much visual fidelity for his own good. The presence of voice acting doesn’t help either, as I’m already picturing Link staring dead-eyed as emotional scenes unfold around him.  The remake is only a couple of months away, and I’m sure we’ll receive a more detailed look at gameplay before November, but I hate that my first impression is dominated by the tragic fact that a relatable child of the forest who once propelled my childhood fantasies now feels like an unfortunate intruder.

The story as we've seen it also looks very faithful, from the Great Deku Tree to the fearsome appearance of Ganondorf. But among the quick flashes of story sequences one character in particular caught my eye, and it made me wonder if this remake will be more overt with one of the series' most famous twists. The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time spoilers follow. Ocarina of Time has a significant time jump, from child Link to adult Link, which earned this version of Link the nickname The Hero of Time. The child Link last saw Princess Zelda being spirited away from the castle by her bodyguard, Impa, during an apparent power grab by the evil Ganondorf. When Link takes the Master Sword and awakens as an adult, the castle is in disrepair, and the royal family is nowhere to be found. What exactly happened to Zelda is a major driving mystery through this second major act of the game. As those who have played Ocarina before know, adult Zelda is hiding in plain sight as the mysterious Sheik. Sheik presents themselves as a survivor of the Sheikah people and helps Link along his journey, pointing him in the right direction of the Seven Sages. Eventually Zelda reveals herself. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A0WX-jsir94 It's a plot twist that seems obvious in hindsight. The golden-haired Sheik was always hidden behind scarves and had a vested interest in helping Link, but the character is male-presenting, and this was a game that frequently introduced new bespoke characters. Sheik was just another part of the network of people who helped Link along his journey, no different than his childhood friend Saria or the Zora princess Ruto. That's not necessarily the case anymore. Zelda's dual identity as Sheik has become a major part of the series lore and been immortalized in other games like Super Smash Bros and Hyrule Warriors. It's hard to imagine many Zelda fans could be playing this game without knowing about that aspect of the character, so Nintendo may not see as much point in hiding it. Which brings me back to the look of Sheik. Likely in part because the character models are so much more realistically rendered, she looks very identifiable as Zelda in disguise. Even trying to put my knowledge of the character aside, this hi-def Sheik with a long blonde braid seems like she'll be easily recognized, even by newcomers. Princess Ze--err, Sheik, yes, that's it The presentation of Sheik being so easily recognizable as Zelda makes me wonder if Nintendo even really intends it to be a twist this time around. The company may figure that most players already know and those who don't will probably realize it. For that reason it doesn't seem to be going to great lengths to hide Sheik's identity. Instead, the Ocarina of Time remake could recontextualize this twist. Rather than a big reveal for the audience, Sheik's identity should remain a big revelation to the characters. Link himself will still be shocked to find out that the childhood friend he's been aiming to save the entire time has been helping him all along, and has grown into a skillful warrior in her own right. Watching the story proceed with a piece of secret information that we as the audience know, but the characters don't, is a different kind of satisfaction. And I suspect that is what Nintendo will capitalize on for this remake.

Whether you think it skews too far into the direction of realism or you're bawling your eyes out over how good Hyrule looks all these decades later, it's strange to see such a distinct and recognizable game look like this. And I'm hoping that it's received well, because I would love to see the same treatment applied to Ocarina of Time's incredibly strange and dark sequel, The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask, if only too see just how messed up that moon is going to look. It's possible you've never played Majora's Mask but will immediately recognize one of its primary antagonists: a menacing and ever-present moon that is slowly hurtling towards Clock Town over the span of three days. As time progresses, its creepy grin edges closer and closer to the ground, a constant reminder of the impending doom that only you can stop. It's a striking design that, alongside the equally iconic design of the titular Majora's Mask, gives such a different look and feel to the majesty of Hyrule in Ocarina of Time. While the Nintendo 3DS remake offered a way to see this menancing moon in more detail, I really can't wait to see what Nintendo can do if it decides to go down the same route as its most recent remake. Based on the footage in the latest Nintendo Direct, faces seem to be a divisive topic when it comes to talking about the look of Ocarina of Time. Young Link has an incredible amount of detail to the point of uncanniness, and that extends to an arresting first look at young Zelda that stood out (to me at least) as the strangest interpretation of the lot. There are many others that look a lot better--adult Link and Sheik look pretty great--but these are all characters that were designed to be friendly and inviting. Majora's Mask, by contrast, has characters designed to look the exact opposite, meaning we could probably see some truly alarming recreations that would fit perfectly with the tone of the game. And it's not just the moon, either. There's a whole host of freaky Clock Town residents that I would love to see in this style. The deranged mask salesman that swaps between eerily happy to eyes-wide crazed. The all-legs-and-no-body giants that you summon to stop the moon's impedning collision. The ghostly form of Kamaro as he roams Termina Field. And every single body-contorting transformation Link goes through when equipping the handful of masks you find during your adventure. There are so many opportunities to really lean into the haunting nature of Majora's Mask in ways that this new visual design might complement more than attempting to recreate the splendor of Hyrule, but it could also be informed by how Nintendo attempts to tackle the areas (or, times) when it isn't so magical. For those who know, Ocarina of Time takes a turn around halfway through, and some of the horror elements that went on to define Majora's Mask are experimented with in some effective ways. Without spoiling anything beyond that, I'm most excited to see how these areas are adapted, especially since they were so effectively creepy with previous technical constraints. Regardless of what you think about how Ocarina of Time looks, this is fertile ground for Nintendo to continue experimenting with. And while it's less likely that we'll get a continuous stream of remakes akin to Capcom's treatment of Resident Evil, I really do hope we at least just get one more.
