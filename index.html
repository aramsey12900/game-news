Carjacking in Grand Theft Auto games has been, as the title denotes, a staple of the series. Up until now, it's largely been the same. While there have been more details and animations added over the years, the premise has remained that you'll walk up to a car, press Y or Triangle, throw the driver out, get in and drive away. If the car was parked, you'd execute the same button press to smash the window and steal it. With GTA 6, carjacking is getting a complete overhaul. Here, we've got all of the information we know so far on how to steal cars in GTA 6. There are two ways to steal a car: taking one that's stationary, or taking one that someone's driving around in. However, there are more details that make GTA 6 feel more like true simulation gameplay now. How to steal a parked car As we saw in the GTA 6 extended look gameplay, when Jason approached a car, he had multiple options to choose from. The first option is a slim jim--a classic tool used to break into older vehicles. It slides down the window frame to adjust the lock, and in GTA 6, it seems like this is done via a lockpicking minigame. However, based on what we've heard so far, that won't work for every car. Depending on the year and model of the vehicle, you might not be able to use a slim jim. However, newer and more expensive cars might not work with this method. On top of that, there are trackers, meaning that the police can track your location when you steal a car. This changes the whole carjacking aspect of Grand Theft Auto. Normally, if you'd steal a car, you might catch some heat; maybe a nearby police officer sees you and follows you, but it's pretty easy to get away cleanly. This time, that isn't the case. If you do want to get away cleanly, you need an item to disable the tracking device. It seems like that item, along with the slim jim, is not something you start out with, meaning you'll need to progress through the story or complete side objectives to obtain them. Another interesting thing about the tracker is that apparently if you drive fast enough, the cops won't be able to track you. That adds an extra element of tension, because if you slow down and try to play it safe, they'll know exactly where you are and you'll need to get moving right away. Stealing cars isn't as straightforward as it used to be. How to steal occupied vehicles In some cases, driven vehicles aren't locked, so you'll be able to just walk up and open the door. It seems like there's a quick time minigame to complete, which will wrestle the driver out of the car, and then you can drive away. If there is a passenger, they will also try to prevent you from stealing the car. However, just because you steal a car doesn't mean the cops can't track you--the tracker is still there and you will need to find a way to disable it. Vehicle security and impound lots How do you know what kind of security a given car on the street has? The app Waink allows you to scan vehicles, and it'll tell you the security systems on board as well as how much it'll cost to register it as your own vehicle. One other interesting detail about registered vehicles that was in one of TGG's videos is that if you ditch one of your own cars while you are wanted, the police will set up a perimeter around your car so that if you return to it, in theory, they will be waiting for you. If you wait too long, they will impound it. This leads to some interesting gameplay implications. Perhaps you'll have to sneak into the impound lot to retrieve it, or if you have the money, you could show up and pay the fee before driving off. There's certainly something deeper going on with the Grand Theft Auto mechanics we've known for years. Supposedly, when you start the game you won't have access to a lot of these tools. You won't be able to instantly steal cars; you'll have to progress to that point. It seems like better vehicles will slowly be introduced throughout the game. You'll have to pre-plan your robberies a little more. If you want better vehicles, you'll have to work for them, whether that's through stealing or more legitimate means. This also leads to interesting repercussions when it comes to robberies and other activities. You'll want a fast getaway car--previously, you could just acquire the nearest suitable one, but in GTA 6 you'll have to plan ahead. If a nearby car is parked, you might not have the equipment to stealthily steal it. Even if you do, maybe you don't have the gear to remove the tracker, which will bring more heat on you. This means that robberies need to be pre-planned. Perhaps Jason or Lucia will be on watch with a vehicle, so the other character can run out, hop in, and drive away. Maybe you'll choose a vehicle as your getaway car early on, steal it, park it nearby, and then complete the robbery knowing you have a method of escape. GTA 6 is bringing about many more changes for the series. While many thought Jason and Lucia would be in a romantic relationship, it turns out that's not actually necessary. The game has also removed the need to button mash to sprint--something I've been desperate for.

I’m Begging You To Stop Obsessing Over Player Numbers And Just Enjoy GamesNEW
Horror fans are confused. Every time a licensed multiplayer horror game gets brought up, two things tend to happen: commenters wonder if it can dethrone Dead by Daylight, and they speculate that the g...
Horror fans are confused. Every time a licensed multiplayer horror game gets brought up, two things tend to happen: commenters wonder if it can dethrone Dead by Daylight, and they speculate that the game will shut down quickly. It's happening again today, as I've recently published my Halloween review. Comments on YouTube and in Discord servers are already repeating the sentiments I've seen for many years now. "Probably be dead in six months to a year because no one can live aside DBD." "It will be dead within a month like the rest." "The real question is, will it go the way of Evil Dead, Friday the 13th, and Texas Chain Saw, and not touch Dead by Daylight?" I view this response as a kind of mass hallucination. This obsession over a game's health and future prospects is a widespread problem in a number of genres right now, but it's been especially irksome in the multiplayer horror space, where people rely on points that may feel true but don't prove accurate upon closer inspection. I've played nearly every licensed multiplayer horror game, like those named above and others, like Killer Klowns and Predator: Hunting Grounds. For the past decade, Hollywood licensors have been increasingly interested in adapting their intellectual properties to games, and with each new one revealed, there's an expectation among players and onlookers that they will die off, failing to topple the titan of the genre, Dead by Daylight. It's true that Dead by Daylight remains in a league of its own in terms of its community size. What I find absurd, though, is that these other games are seeking to usurp Behaviour Interactive's decade-long success story. Not one of these games seems to have aspirations or expectations of replacing DBD. Ultimately, they're very different things, and these other adaptations are only looking to earn their place alongside DBD. Halloween is the latest multiplayer horror game to be held up to the unfair DBD standard. In some important ways, Dead by Daylight is closer to Fortnite than it is Friday the 13th or Halloween. To date, DBD has brought more than 40 killers, many of them from licensed horror movies and shows, to its one-size-fits-all framework. Except in cases of limited-time modes, DBD is always about four players sneaking around dozens of different maps, repairing generators, avoiding being hooked, and escaping off the map before the killer ends them. It's a fun game I literally play every night, but it doesn't offer a bespoke experience. Whether you're playing as Ghostface, Leatherface, Pinhead, or Chucky, your job is to prevent survivors from fixing generators and opening the exit doors.  It makes for a great, highly competitive gameplay loop, but it doesn't make sense in any of these killers' original universes. Freddy Krueger is obsessed with invading the dreams of Springwood kids. Outside of DBD, he's never been seen guarding generators to prevent Steve from Stranger Things and Detective Tapp from Saw from powering the exit doors. Again, it's a wonderful game, but the thing it lacks most is authenticity for any one slasher or other horror franchise. It's never the point for games like Texas Chain Saw Massacre to try and take the crown from DBD. What these other games want to do is capture the spirit of their source material. That's a goal that can, and does, coexist with DBD quite peacefully. It's true that some players who enjoy games like this only have enough time in a day to play one, and maybe on many days for many players, that's DBD. But so far, hardly any of the other licensed asymmetrical multiplayer horror games have had grand designs as live-service games. They get cosmetics and updates, sure, but most don't include things like daily or weekly check-in incentives, and when they have, they're more lax than DBD's full suite of live-service mechanics. They don't want to be your forever-game the way DBD does. They're different tastes, and I love the variety they collectively offer. As prevalent and wrong-headed as that "DBD killer" narrative is, it's really secondary to the "dead game" narrative. Players like to predict that each one of these new licensed horror games will die off quickly, "like the rest," they say. But the truth is, history isn't on these doomsayers' side. Games like Texas Chain Saw Massacre don't try to beat DBD; they just want to live alongside it. Friday the 13th died because of a legal drama that saw its rights change hands, and any ongoing deals based on it, such as IllFonic and Gun's multiplayer game, were forced to end. Suffice it to say, though this could happen again in theory, it would seem to be a rare case, perhaps never to be repeated. If that legal battle had never happened, I expect Jason would still be alive today. In nearly every other case, games of this sort are doing fine. Not everything has to be the biggest game in the world. I worry players have become so obsessed with Steam player counts that they've stood in the way of their own fun. You can hop into a round of Killer Klowns, Texas Chain Saw Massacre, or Predator right now. While some are no longer adding new content, they're all in good shape, and I still enjoy rotating through them regularly.  Players especially like to call out Texas Chain Saw Massacre as some sort of cautionary tale, saying that the developers failed to deliver on content promises, even though all they ever promised was to support the game with new updates as long as it made financial sense for the studios involved. That lasted about two years, which seems fine to me. If I paid $30 for a game and had a blast for two years, I'd call that getting my money's worth.  The pushback against that seems born from the idea that every multiplayer game must now be both massive and ever-changing. Can't games just be fun for a while anymore? I don't want to see them go offline; don't get me wrong. But so long as they stay playable, why must they keep offering new things to do? It's like regular players now take their cues from the venture capitalists infecting every fandom with never-ending expectations to grow and prosper more than they did last week, last month, last year. It makes me want to scream: We have a bunch of fun video games based on some of the best horror movies ever made!  I do admit, not every example supports my argument. I know of two horror games that went offline and were delisted: Evil Dead and Puppet Master. In the case of the former, I'd only note that I found it to be the least enjoyable of all those I've played. That doesn't mean I wanted it to be delisted, and the teams behind that decision, Saber and Boss Team, deserve criticism for taking it away like that.  Evil Dead is a rare example of a multiplayer horror game closing down unceremoniously. As for Puppet Master, I almost have to excuse that one. I'm neck-deep in this genre, and I nearly never knew it existed, only spotting it on Steam a short while before it got taken down. It seems it wasn't even on the map of players like me. I certainly wish I got to play it, and that others could do so whenever they wanted to, but to me these are exceptions to the rule.  On the whole, horror multiplayer games are doing well. It feels like we've hardly scratched the surface of what other games can spring up from this trend--please, somebody make my Scream game with social deduction, whodunnit leanings. For now, Halloween is the latest of them, and yet another really fun one. I wish horror fans could experience these for what they are, rather than fold each of them into a bigger conversation about their financial prospects. I am literally Michael Myers, and I'm having a great time. That alone is worth celebrating.

Playing Halloween: The Game's Single-Player Campaign Is A Tutorialized Love Letter, But Not What You're Here ForNEW
Colorado-based developer IllFonic has been making asymmetrical multiplayer games based on iconic movie IPs for most of its existence. Arguably its biggest hit remains its first stab at the ge...
Colorado-based developer IllFonic has been making asymmetrical multiplayer games based on iconic movie IPs for most of its existence. Arguably its biggest hit remains its first stab at the genre, with 2017's Friday the 13th: The Game (a great game now infamous for its shutdown), but since then, the team has released Predator: Hunting Grounds, Ghostbusters: Spirits Unleashed, and most recently, Killer Klowns from Outer Space: The Game. Finally, though, IllFonic is making the biggest horror-movie-turned-asymmetrical-multiplayer layup game of all time with Halloween: The Game. If you preorder the more expensive fancy-smancy version, you can hop into the game early on September 4; otherwise, the game goes wide on September 8. I need more time to work through and digest IllFonic's multiplayer offering in Halloween, but I have completed the single-player campaign (the studio's first in one of these games) and wanted to bring you some initial thoughts. A Few Notches Above Glorified Tutorial If you aren't a multiplayer person and were curious about picking up Halloween for its single-player offering... I wouldn't. It lasts just a couple of hours – though you can extend it by replaying chapters to knock out optional objectives and unlock cosmetic rewards – and is just a few notches above a glorified tutorial for playing as Michael Myers. Each chapter introduces new mechanics in his kit that you will then hone and master in the multiplayer side of Halloween. There is a heaping dose of love from IllFonic, however, to the movies, primarily the 1978 Halloween from director/synth player/gamer John Carpenter that started it all, that elevates it just so.As you might expect, a single-player campaign where you play as Michael Myers based on the first Halloween film is, well, a video game version of that movie. If you've never played an IllFonice game before, this campaign will give you the rundown on how its games feel, including the at-this-point-must-be-a-feature-and-not-a-bug baked-in instability. I encountered nothing game-ruining, but like its predecessors, Halloween has a distinguishable jank to it that you will either come to love because it adds to the unpredictable nature of these multiplayer experiences or you will hate because... it's still jank. It runs fine, performance-wise, but I did encounter some noticeable bugs, like falling through floors or being unable to hide bodies in designated hiding areas; I would not categorize this as the acceptable jank I expect in an IllFonic game, and I hope the team is working on patching issues like that up.  Nonetheless, escaping Smith's Grove, killing a mechanic and donning Michael's trademark navy blue jumpsuit, and killing the clerk at a department store so you can steal and put on the William Shatner mask to complete the set is good fun. A filmic look overlaid on the game's true-to-movie visual style sells the aesthetics. Though if you aren't a fan of film grain, you won't like what you see. Performances in the campaign are fine, with Dr. Loomis being the only real standout/named character in the otherwise boilerplate cast who are there to be victims. Running around Haddonfield's suburbs as Michael is good, terrifying fun. In quiet moments, his pained breaths add a sinister air to every step, while screams from nearby houses or hushed breathing in closets sell the idea that you embody evil and everyone is afraid of you. So, too, does the wide array of kills at your disposal. You can stalk victims from afar, completing minigames to raise what is essentially their fear level, and then strike using your knife, your hands, other weapons you find out and about like pitchforks and fire pokers, or even the environment for special contextual kills (like piercing bodies onto telephone poles or throwing someone off a second-floor porch). It remains a good time throughout the short campaign, which ends with Michael escaping Haddonfield to presumably go kill a bunch of random players in online matchmaking lobbies, but it does little more than teach you the ropes within the context of some of Michael's most notorious kills.  I'm satisfied as this campaign is about what I expected, but I would have liked something bigger to dig my knife into for IllFonic's first go at a single-player experience like this. Admittedly, however, that's not what you're here for (or at least, not what you should be here for). IllFonic's games of this ilk live and die by the multiplayer, and though I can see how playing as Michael will be interesting, I still have no idea what it's like to play alongside real players or as a resident running from him. With the campaign behind me, I'll be starting my multiplayer Halloween journey properly.Look forward to my review for Game Informer at a later date.In the meantime, read Game Informer's reviews for Friday the 13th: The Game, Predator: Hunting Grounds, and Killer Klowns from Outer Space: The Game. After that, read our interview with IllFonic about what to expect in Halloween.

Final Fantasy Resonance Is A Little More Than Just NostalgiaNEW
...
Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: Square Enix Developer: Square Enix Release: October 22, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ Nostalgia for the olden days of Final Fantasy is on no shortage. Trading card games, merchandise, and even spin-off games have dipped into the well, including the 2016 mobile title, Final Fantasy: Brave Exvius, which is serving as the base for Final Fantasy Resonance.With Resonance, Square Enix and Lancarse are following in the footsteps of Octopath Traveler 0: taking a former mobile game and giving it a premium game treatment. And while it's certainly a little strange at times, the results have so far turned out well.The big draws for Final Fantasy Resonance are immediately apparent. For starters, it's a new HD-2D game, an aesthetic that's become a trademark of the retro side of Square Enix's portfolio. There are some new advancements, including some 3D mapping using pixels that makes for incredibly gorgeous story moments. And the Visions you can call upon unleash their biggest attacks in elaborate 3D CGI cutscenes, in ways that strangely reminded me of Square's movies like The Spirits Within and Advent Children, though not in a bad way. Those Visions are another draw. During my demo at Gamescom 2026, I had an established party of characters specific to the story of Resonance, but each of them could equip a spiritual Vision of famous Final Fantasy characters from throughout the series, augmenting their move sets. Final Fantasy XV's Noctis was invaluable in accessing more Blizzard attacks for my boss fight, for example, while the Warrior of Light from the original Final Fantasy served as a perfect companion for my party's vanguard, healing and casting protective magic.Combine that with a classic turn-based system, with a stagger bar setup that encourages you to press an enemy's weakness for extra turns, and you've got a pretty well-rounded RPG setup. It would have been easy to stop there: make an RPG, fill it with Final Fantasy references, slap a coat of HD-2D paint over the top, and you've got a decent entry for the fall release season. Oddly though, it wasn't the nostalgia that hooked me the most. I'm an avowed Final Fantasy enjoyer; I've collected the aforementioned trading cards, played numerous mainline and spin-off games, and spent likely hundreds of hours in Final Fantasy XIV. What I enjoyed the most in Resonance, though, was the promise of what will be fresh.Some additions are small but meaningful, like the conversations your party can have while wandering around a dungeon. I really like options like this – the Tales Of series' skits are a great example – and they naturally flesh out the lore and world of Resonance, as well as the relationships between my four party members. I didn't get a big intro to their backstories leading into the dungeon I played, but even just hearing them discuss the ethics of summoning a primal entity to play a bedtime lullaby for the party was a fun way to get to know this crew of adventurers.The battles, too, add a lot of interesting strategy to your conventional turn-based combat. Clever management of the stagger bar against tough enemies, especially the wind-wielding boss of the dungeon I faced, was the difference between a victory fanfare and a full party wipeout. Chipping away at the bar meant constantly balancing who was attacking, defending, healing, and using items to keep the magic flowing.Final Fantasy Resonance falls into an interesting spot, as Square Enix's trend of revitalizing former mobile games into standalone console releases continues. There are some upgrades and quality-of-life changes built up over the narrative framework and building blocks of Brave Exvius, with plenty of series iconography draped over the top; a towering sundae of RPG. The cherry on top, though, is a solid new RPG built on both new and old. I might have shown up because of the Final Fantasy moniker, but now I'm just interested in a good ol' fashioned RPG to dig into this October.
