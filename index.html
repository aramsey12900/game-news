Marvel's Wolverine Review - Relentless MomentumNEW

Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5 Publisher: Sony Interactive Entertainment Developer: Insomniac Games Release: September 15, 2026 Rating: Mature Wolverine memorably and repeatedly quips in the comics about being the best there is at what he does, and I imagine Insomniac may have tried to take that directive to heart in crafting what aims to be the definitive experience of the character. Following in the wake of the successful Spider-Man games, Insomniac’s Wolverine nails one very specific thing with near perfection: the brutal efficiency of the lead character’s destructive ferocity. The high speed, brutal slashing, and unstoppable force of Wolverine are captured better than any film or game to come before, and that alone could be reason enough to embark on this adventure with him. If the accompanying story sometimes stumbles or the driving linearity doesn’t always land, you can still have confidence that the fights along the way will not disappoint.Marvel's Wolverine Video Review: As one of many fictional variations on the X-Men mythology, this latest take is an especially bleak one, defined (at least so far) by the notable absence of Professor Xavier and Magneto. Without their influence, Insomniac can play more freely with the relationships and world dynamics that shape Logan’s life. Moment-to-moment interactions between characters are excellent, thanks to an impressive voice cast that doesn’t flinch from the high intensity and feral encounters that push the plot forward. At several points, the game slows down to allow for character development, but these more languid moments don’t always work, since virtually everything about them is on rails, with little to no choice in how to progress.The bigger storytelling problem emerges in the second half of the game, as we begin to get hints and sequences about where the story is going next. Even as a lifelong fan of these characters, some of these later moments left me scratching my head, trying to understand what was actually happening, or the implications around Wolverine’s mysterious past. For someone relatively new to Logan’s convoluted backstory, I fear it might be even more impenetrable. At times, the game feels more interested in teasing where Insomniac wants to go next than in delivering a complete, satisfying resolution to this game’s key relational arcs. In all honesty, you may simply not care much about all that, so eager are you to get back to the fight. From one encounter to the next, the extremely M-rated battles that ensue are deeply satisfying and smartly balanced. Logan’s varied attacks, dodges, and parries feed into a momentum-based loop to power his healing and rage, encouraging a relentless and fast-paced approach to every exchange. Additional attacks and abilities unlock gradually and add to the arsenal, consistently supplying more ways to steamroll basic enemies and help you survive the bigger and more nuanced fights. And, without spoiling it for the longtime fans, it’s enough to say that if you’ve been crossing your fingers to see a boss fight between Wolverine and a particular foe, there’s a good chance this game has you covered with an intense and memorable showdown.Marvel’s Wolverine doesn’t wear out its welcome. The linear adventure hits its conclusion before you might tire of the ferocious melees. Several secondary challenge stages (including some difficult wave-based gauntlets) give players some targets to prove their mastery. A  New Game + lets you ramp up the difficulty, replay favorite battles, and lose yourself in the flurry of claws, but that’s mostly gravy on top rather than a motivation for extensive replayability. Spider-Man and Wolverine are very different characters who deserve different interactive treatments, but any illusions that you might have of the open-world exploration Insomniac delivered in that first hero’s adventures are best left at the door.Insomniac’s latest action opus is beautiful to look at and listen to, and it plays like a dream. Whether you’re a fan of the wily mutant lead or you simply gravitate to taut, tightly animated combat like what you’d find in God of War or Star Wars Jedi: Fallen Order, Wolverine is an intense and unrelenting ride. It may not hit a lot of notes, but like Logan himself, it nails the vitals where it counts.   Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system

Wolverine Only Aims As High As Its Stout HeroNEW

A few hours into Wolverine I found myself asking, “Why did Insomniac Games make this?” There are obvious answers, of course: It has three excellent Spider-Man games under its belt; it's an opportunity to expand its Marvel Universe; and Wolverine presents ample opportunities for a brutal and bloody action game. But none of that explains why Insomniac Games, specifically, made Wolverine. Spider-Man is an ideal fit for the studio: an energetic and dynamic hero in the hands of a team known for open-worlds that are sprawling jungle gyms, where it can let players loose with thrilling traversal mechanics and varied combat systems for crime fighting fun. Insomniac also excels at crafting narratives that are as earnest as they are dramatic--perfect for the tumultuous lives of Peter Parker and Miles Morales. Wolverine is much less ideal of a pairing, as was evident throughout my time with the game. Insomniac doesn't make bad games and Wolverine certainly isn't one. It's a solid, cinematic, and occasionally thrilling experience that ticks all the necessary boxes for a decent action game. But it does so in a workman-like fashion and never aspires to much more than the essentials. Wolverine is an action game that serves the character well enough, but it doesn't lean into Insomniac's strengths or leverage its creativity, and the game feels unremarkable because of it. Straying too far from who Wolverine is and how he operates would no doubt create questions around the authenticity of the adaptation, but only coloring inside the well-defined lines means never offering anything new or exciting. It turns out that the most authentic version of Wolverine, in this case, is also the embodiment of the character action genre at its plainest. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k7Nz-WyUq7o In a world where pretty much every superhero game subscribes to the Batman: Arkham template of combat design, describing how you fight would belabor how familiar it all is. Suffice it to say: light attack, heavy attack, dodge, parry, a few special abilities, and some attribute modifiers.  There are very few distinguishing elements of Wolverine's combat system that make it stand out. The closest it comes is a leaping attack, which allows players to launch him across distances to close the gap and begin an assault on unsuspecting enemies. It's an undeniably satisfying tool to keep up the intensity of each skirmish and ensure you're always applying pressure, but loses its luster after a couple of hours when it becomes clear that it can be employed pretty mindlessly. The other unique element, a mechanic that represents an escalation of power and brutality as Wolverine's rage boils over, has more longevity in how drastically it shifts the dynamic of combat and amps up the satisfaction of it. As players dispatch enemies, an on-screen rage meter builds until the point where Wolverine goes berserk, putting him in a state where you can chain together executions to tear enemies apart in quick succession. In just a few seconds, Wolverine can create piles of brutally mauled bad guys, and there's a feral savagery to the whole thing that is very effective.  It's a consistently exhilarating moment because, as well as shifting to desaturated visuals to emphasize how unstable and blinded by rage Logan is, these sequences are strong instances of synchronicity between the character and the player. As Wolverine's rage builds, the player needs to be more efficient in dispatching enemies to keep the meter climbing, and when Wolverine finally hits breaking point, the input demands on the player simplify to just pressing two buttons together to eviscerate anything in your path. It is catharsis that comes from Wolverine giving into the monster within and the player embracing that monster in tandem.  If the appeal of Wolverine for you lies in how visceral its violence is, your virtual bloodlust will be well and truly satiated. Insomniac has spared no expense in lavishing Wolverine's finishes with copious amounts of blood and guts--his executions are exactly that in every sense of the word. The violence never strives for shock value by being grotesque in the way something like Mortal Kombat does, so it never feels gratuitous. Even when he erupts into a berserker rage, the coldness of his violence reinforces the idea that he is an efficient killing machine. Wolverine never revels in it; he just gets it done and moves on. The main issues with Wolverine's combat are that it often feels like it lacks weight, is missing lethality up until he goes berserk, and never finds rhythm. As a result, I often felt frustrated that the game was undermining the sense of power and ferocity that I wanted to feel as Wolverine. And at the same time, the individual components of the combat doesn't have the connective tissue to create a natural flow that made me feel like I developed a mastery of Wolverine's skills. The adamantium claws that serve as Wolverine's dealers of death and destruction often felt more like needles than blades. The feedback on each attack always felt just a little lacking, and enemies never responded to being struck in a way that made me feel empowered. Quite the opposite, in fact. I can't count the number of times that I would be mid-combo, clawing away at an enemy, and they'd suddenly ignore incoming damage to clobber me over the head with a rifle or slice me with their own blade. It came off as the game deciding it was the enemy's turn to attack because it needed to put an end to my barrage. It made me feel like an angry cat being bonked on the head to calm down. Fortunately, the various abilities Wolverine has at his disposal feel like they pack more punch and can swing momentum in your favor. These range from a spinning claw attack that can control space and a leaping knee that interrupts enemies, to a grapple that slams enemies down so that Wolverine can mount and pummel them. There's also a spinning Psycho Crusher-like maneuver that can be chained multiple times, an all-out barrage of powerful strikes, and a recovery ability that focuses Wolverine's rage and uses it to heal, among others. All of these introduce new considerations into the combat, making it feel a little more involved and dynamic. However, since there's no real depth or connectivity to the combo system, their execution feels very rigid and disjointed.  Everything is on a cooldown, which suggests that timing and strategy is important in how they're used, but in practice, they can be employed thoughtlessly without much consequence. Many of Spider-Man's abilities served as ways to bridge one attack sequence into another; after smacking an enemy around, he could launch them into the air, chase up, smash them back down, zip to another enemy, swing them around to hit others in the vicinity, launch them into the distance, use a web to close that distance, and continue to string physical abilities together or use gadgets to manipulate enemies to his advantage. The point being, there was an if-this-then-that flow to it that, admittedly, is made possible by the character's agility and dynamism. Wolverine, meanwhile, feels awkward and brutish by comparison--and not in a good, character-appropriate way. Instead, using his various skills is akin to how you'd approach battles in an MMO, where you just let basic attacks fill the time in between cooldowns and then sequentially work through everything on the ability bar. Sure, you can use those abilities in a way that complements each other, but there's no real requirement or incentive to do so beyond your own initiative.  Defensively, Wolverine suffers similar issues. Tapping the L1 button at the right moment will parry an attack, but the window--even before you use an adaptation to make it more forgiving--is pretty lenient, so it doesn't really take much skill to parry. In fact, I had a few occasions where I was surrounded by enemies all attacking me, sometimes overlapping, and I was able to survive their onslaught and take no damage by spamming the parry button with my eyes closed. That's not hyperbolic--I tested it. Parrying is another part of Wolverine's combat that is functional but unrewarding. It may be hard to believe but all of that doesn't mean the combat is bad. It is perfectly functional, and I suspect that many people won't think too hard about how it all fits together. The cinematic brutality of it provides enough of a spectacle that everything else may not even register or matter. But as someone who wants to feel engaged and challenged, the vast majority of my time playing Wolverine felt rote. In a couple of stretches, I opted to reduce enemy health to make them easier to kill. Not because they provided any sort of challenge, but because I wasn't engaged enough to care and wanted to hit the next story beat. Being a fan of the character, experienced with intricate combat systems, and knowing how good Insomniac can do combat, this was disappointing. However, through it all, the care and attention given to visual presentation remained one of the game's strengths. Unlike its open-world Spider-Man games, Wolverine is linear, corralling players from one combat arena to the next by way of streets, up and down elevators, through buildings, onto rooftops, and into the thick of a remote forest or two. Any deviations off the main path are slight and usually to pick up resources used for unlocking new outfits or bottles of whiskey that expand how many adaptations you can equip.  The most evident benefit of this narrowing in scope is in the fidelity of the visuals. Wolverine is a beautiful game, both artistically and technically. I really appreciate how Insomniac is able to bring together the vibrancy of comic books and a grittier aesthetic that allows the game to feature superheroes wearing bright costumes, while also giving the game a grounded, more realistic vibe befitting of Wolverine. There's an impressive amount of detail in the character models, with Wolverine's stout and muscular physique giving him an imposing presence. When the camera gets up close, you can really see just how hairy he is (can relate), but also the gradation in his skin color, his pores, and the grime that builds up during brawls.  Environments also have a high degree of destructability, so while I may not have always felt powerful as Wolverine when attacking enemies, watching the world around me crack, crumble, and collapse picked up some of that slack. This high level of visual fidelity pays dividends most in the larger-scale set-pieces, of which there are only a handful, but they're a lot of fun. There are battles against towering enemies (surprise, there are Sentinels) or knockdown-dragout battles against other superpowered characters. Wolverine serves the character well enough, but doesn’t lean into Insomniac’s strengths or leverage its creativity There are other parts of the gameplay experience that never outright excel. Everything in between the combat fits into the Uncharted-esque guided platforming mold, so you'll spend a lot of time jumping from handhold to handhold, swinging from branches and poles, or hopping across rooftops. But because Wolverine has very limited mobility and no interesting traversal mechanic to speak of beyond a big jump, the running, jumping, and climbing portions of the game often feel equally uneventful. Similarly, stealth feels like more of an afterthought than a key pillar of gameplay. Again, I know that's not Wolverine's thing. He doesn't swing from buildings like Spidey or barrel across skylines like Hulk, or sneak like Mystique, but the truth of the matter is I didn't find much to get excited about when moving around the world. The only real exceptions come in the rare high-octane escape or desperate climb for survival, in which the circumstances bring the thrills instead of anything I'm actually doing. Ultimately, it is what it is because it's all Wolverine really has. And that point is driven home in the numerous times that Jean Grey, the ultra-powerful mutant with telepathic and telekinetic abilities, offers to simply fly herself and Logan to the top of a building or across a city, and Wolverine declines repeatedly. Even when the fate of his friends or of the world is on the line, he opts to take the long and arduous way because, I don't know, it makes his tummy hurt, or he doesn't like how it feels or some other contrived excuse. But I know that taking the quick and direct route would trivialize massive portions of the game, so instead we go through the uninteresting but inoffensive motions. The strongest aspect of Wolverine is its narrative and character development, but even that isn't without its fair share of oddities and pacing issues. Insomniac has done a commendable job of taking touchstones from the history of Marvel and building something a little more original around them, while also trying to explore Logan from a more emotional, human perspective. Logan's story is intertwined with the increasingly threatened existence of mutants, which have fairly recently emerged.  Although Bolivar Trask is presented as the main threat to mutantkind, the game doesn't waste any time in hinting that a bigger threat may be a little closer to home. The game takes place at a time when the X-Men haven't yet formed and, instead, Wolverine is a member of Team X, a crew also featuring Sabertooth and Mystique. Crucially, Team X was assembled by Nathaniel Essex, better known as the villain Mister Sinister in other Marvel stories.  What begins as a mission to rescue Essex eventually leads to a battle for mutant survival against Trask and his Sentinels, and then a hunt for a mysterious artifact that could change the future of mutant and humankind forever. Along the way, relationships become strained, hidden motivations emerge, and the state of Insomniac's fledgling X-Men universe rapidly develops in some unexpected directions. There are stellar performances all around, with Liam McIntyre's take on Logan bringing more heart and charm to the character than expected thanks to writing that allows the character to be vulnerable and, on occasion, goofy.  Krizia Bajos's Jean Gray also has an emotional depth to her that makes the time she spends with Logan meaningful for both characters. She has been finding and rescuing mutants, and has a worn-down, sometimes defeated vibe to her, but is uplifted by the community of mutants she has built around her. The game is very comfortable letting Logan and Jean have their moments of flirtation and bonding, and although it can occasionally feel rushed, the performances make the deepening connection between them believable nevertheless.  The standout performance, however, is undoubtedly Troy Baker as Nathaniel Essex. Anyone with a bit of X-Men knowledge knows exactly where Essex's story will take him, but the journey is still compelling thanks to the performance. Baker is able to present Essex with the same kind of warmth and conviction that makes him a believable surrogate for Charles Xavier in Insomniac's version of the X-Men universe. But every scene he's in has a line delivered just manipulatively enough to keep the player on edge, waiting for the inevitable turn. The impact Essex has on those around him, especially Mystique and Sabertooth, is also part of the brilliance of his character and Baker's performance. He's able to prey on everyone's vulnerabilities and exploit their nature to his advantage. And Logan is the victim of some of the worst of it.  The story takes its time getting the pieces in the right place, and there are certainly some pacing issues during this process. The early parts of the game are perhaps a bit too introspective and focused on Wolverine and Jean's relationship, while also including some lengthy sections chasing a macguffin around for a bit too long. But the final third of the game essentially switches from being a Wolverine story to a straight-up X-Men story. That's not a spoiler--you won't suddenly see Cyclops and Gambit appear--but the stakes will become crystal clear and the groundwork is laid for something momentous. As a longtime X-Men fan, I'm a sucker for that kind of stuff and it worked on me. Wolverine is a difficult game to come to terms with. As I've emphasized repeatedly, there's nothing outright wrong or bad about it. It delivers an authentic, highly playable Wolverine experience. I don't regret my time with it and, in fact, I left hoping there's a sequel that makes good on what the game sets up. But at the same time, that inoffensiveness comes from the fact that it's a barebones third-person action game executed in a no-frills way, with no real surprises. At best, it's a fun but forgettable experience that'll entertain you for a few hours. At worst, it shows that just because you can make really good superhero games with one character, that doesn't mean you can make really good super hero games with any character. Insomniac is an incredibly talented developer, but it shines with characters that allow it to flex its well-trained design muscles. Wolverine might be the best at what he does, but what he does hasn't let Insomniac's developers be the best at what they do.

Final Fantasy 7 Revelation Preview – Excitement For The Finale, Despite A Mediocre DemoNEW

Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Square Enix Developer: Square Enix Release: April 8, 2027 I adore Final Fantasy VII Remake – it was my 2020 game of the year – and I thoroughly enjoy its 2024 follow-up, Final Fantasy VII Rebirth (you can read my 8.5 out of 10 review here). I say this because I don’t think either game received great demos, and the finale of this remake trilogy, Final Fantasy VII Revelation, followed suit during Gamescom. Despite that, I’m not worried this threequel will fail to capture me the way its predecessors have. Though my story-focused demo didn’t do much for me, my Game Informer colleague Eric Van Allen had a much better time at Revelation’s Golden Saucer, and my interview with trilogy director Naoki Hamaguchi inspired the confidence I was looking for during the preview. Admittedly, my demo disappointment might be my fault. We were given the choice to do whatever we wanted with Chapter 4 of Revelation, with most of the world open to us by way of the Highwind airship. Eric smartly headed straight to the Golden Saucer for minigames and more. "This is the demo for the new Queen's Blood expansion, right?" I heard him proclaim as he entered those shimmering halls. He tells me the Golden Saucer remains the same as you remember it: laden with attractions and mascots, though the latter have now staged a workers' uprising at the Saucer, and either Vincent or Cait Sith has to take charge and get things back in order. For the rest of the demo, Eric jumped between different minigames to see both the new and the old. Queen's Blood has an interesting new trump card mechanic with a guaranteed starter card for your deck, and opponents are much more likely to have game-breaking advantages you'll need to prepare for. Snowboarding is here, and is pretty fun to get into, though it -- and the chocobo racing track -- felt shallower in comparison to the changes made to Queen's Blood. For lovers of minigames, though, there's hardly a shortage of things to do in the Saucer, or even outside, as Queen's Blood opponents still roam the world for Nanaki (our new card game collector) to challenge. The side game sickos (Eric) won't be hurting for options in April.Knowing he was heading to tackle some side-game content, I followed the main quest, but not before a quick tour of the Highwind’s facilities. Wize is your onboard go-to for playing Queen’s Blood on the ship, editing your deck, and more – he tells me Regina from Rebirth is back in Gongaga, asking me to visit her. Chocobo farmer Billy and his trusted chocobo Piko are now onboard as well, as is Chadley. Fortunately, Chadley is busy tracking down Weapons, so you’ll hear less from him in the world, but unfortunately, MAI is back and quite chatty. It might have been the result of this being a demo, but my word, I was inundated with notifications from MAI that not only paused the action, but took up the entire screen. One pop-up from her would close and another would arrive, over and over again – it was terribly intrusive and hopefully not representative of the final game, which Hamaguchi says can be golden-pathed in about 35 hours but stretched up to 100 or more for completionists; that’s a lot of MAI time. Final Fantasy VII Revelation Preview Screenshot Gallery: After this Highwind tour, I took the airship out for a spin. It controls about how you’d expect, similar to its nautical version at the end of Rebirth, but it’s far more exhilarating to view this world from the sky than the sea. The detail visible from the clouds is impressive, and seeing returning locations like Nibel, Junon, and more brings back memories I forgot I had of each place. The regions you explore down below look exactly as you think they would from above, and it’s easy to map in your head where you’ve been as a result. That said, a lot of Revelation’s playable world is filled with the aforementioned returning locations, and while I enjoyed them in my Rebirth playthroughs, I’m not dying to get back to, say, the opening Grasslands outside of Midgar. Hamaguchi says visual differences to the environments, added verticality, and new things to do have refreshed these locations. “Obviously, where the story is at this point, you have the meteor in the sky, you have Weapons coming from the middle of the planet now rampaging around, so those have caused a lot of environmental changes,” Hamaguchi says in a group interview. “So, for example, there are some areas now which have been flooded and filled with water where they weren’t before. The Weapons have been attacking places and the land is now scarred and destroyed, so there are definitely some obvious visual changes that are the first things you’ll notice.” But the team went further, changing how you explore each returning area. Hamaguchi says the concept of Rebirth was traveling and exploring this wide and vast land area, and that in Revelation, that concept has been expanded to include verticality. Not only is there exploration from the skies, with the ability to parachute down from the Highwind from virtually anywhere in the world, but revamped Chocobo mechanics, too. Chocobos in Revelation can now fly much higher and have an aerial dash that lets you accelerate quickly while flying. Hamaguchi says viewing these old places from new heights changes how you interact with them and explore. “I remember this feeling of great satisfaction when we were doing experiments with this and playing around with it,” he says. “When I first saw the view of the Grassland from the airship coming down and then flying through them on a Chocobo’s back, [it] felt completely new [even though] this is a game I’ve played more than anyone.” While my demo felt like every door in the game was suddenly open with no guidance on what to do, Hamaguchi says the final game isn’t like that. He recognizes that there are many types of open world designs in games, and that Revelation isn’t the type to throw you into the world completely free, with no hints or instructions. “We didn’t go for that style,” he says. “We wanted to get a balance where it was you getting a certain level of hints and explanations and ideas about where you might want to go and what you might want to do, and if you start following those [...] you start to see the options open up and it becomes freer and freer as you go.”He adds that Revelation is built to be more approachable for those wary of open world games. I ask the director about balancing the open world exploration with the very real stakes present narratively in Revelation – not only is it set days before the trilogy’s finale, but the incoming meteor sits ominously in the skybox of this world, threatening to destroy it all. Couple that with the massive Weapons trying to protect the planet and indiscriminately destroying anyone on it as a result, and it’s hard to wrestle with the idea of a Queen’s Blood match and the stakes Revelation sets. “This is days before the final battle,” Hamaguchi says. “The idea is all of the party members having to find their motivation, their reason to fight. [It’s] a good time for the characters to really look inside themselves and examine the reason why they’re still fighting. What’s their motivation? Can they continue? Why should they continue fighting here? And so the idea is that all of the subquests, the side stories – they’re not just random things. [The] apocalypse is coming and [these characters] are trying to face off against their destinies before the end, and Revelation is really tied into that.” He continues, “All of the side content is within the atmosphere of the world and the situation we’re in. We have very much thought about that in [Revelation’s] design. It’s not just some random side task – it’s all tied into why you’re there.” That said, Hamaguchi says it’s part of Final Fantasy VII’s DNA to mix serious dramatic moments with light-hearted humor and funny side stories as well, so to expect those kinds of hybrid stories in Revelation. That answer quelled my concerns I felt while playing Revelation, with its expanded planet to explore and even more activities to complete. Hearing that all of that optional content is aware that a giant meteor will smash into the planet in just a few days means the team is thinking not just about this narrative finale, but the mechanics that we engage with in the lead-up, and that has me excited.After some airborne exploration, I dove Fortnite-style down to Wutai below, parachuting into the Japanese Garden-esque home country of party member, shuriken-throwing ninja, Yuffie. She recommends the team, short of Cloud and Aerith for reasons, go to Wutai because everyone there listens to her and will help their cause. Some significant story discussions happen here involving Glenn Lodbrock, a certain Viceroy, Godo Kisaragi, and others, but I’ll leave that for you to discover.  I quite enjoy Wutai’s visual design, and Yuffie’s remarks about her home are a delight as I explore the lush area. Engaging in combat here is fun, too, as it lets me try out party newcomer Cid’s “dashing dragoon” playstyle. Not quite as ranged as Barret, and not quite close-ranged like Cloud, Cid sits somewhere in between. He uses a spear to target and hit multiple enemies at once with large-swath swings, and it’s a role I didn’t know I needed filled on my Final Fantasy VII Remake trilogy team. What I experienced is barely a taste, though, but it whetted my appetite enough to indicate he’ll be a crucial member of my own party in the final game.  I’m sure the kind of emotional moments, exciting setpieces, and quieter character interactions are present in Revelation, even if none of that was featured in my preview demo. In fact, Hamaguchi says there’s one moment in Revelation that ensures those who didn’t cry in Remake or in Rebirth will have shed a tear by the end of the game. I have a few guesses about what that moment might be; the game launches in April, so I don’t have to wait long to learn if I’m right.

Halloween: The Game Balances Tense Horror With Hilarious Antics, Perfect For An Evening With FriendsNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Gun Interactive, IllFonic Developer: IllFonic Release: September 8, 2026 Rating: Mature I start up Tiny Tim’s 1968 song, “Tiptoe Through the Tulips,” as I creep closer to an unsuspecting Civilian, breathing heavily behind the stark white mask of Michael Myers, moving close enough for proximity chat to turn on. The player controlling the Civilian before me shrieks, shouting, “Aw hell no, it’s Pennywise!” Pennywise is not in Halloween: The Game, nor has he ever been connected to the long-running horror series (or Tiny Tim, for that matter). But alas, my random song selection and the unexpected reaction it inspired from an online stranger is now canon to my time playing IllFonic’s new asymmetrical horror game. The interaction is exactly the one I needed to confidently tell my friends – the ones who would join me in hysterics over this Tiptoed Tulips-infused jumpscare – that this is a game they need to pick up. The single-player campaign is a glorified but impassioned tutorial; charming jank is present throughout the experience; and its multiplayer confidently strikes a balance between tense, terrifying, and downright hilarious stupidity.In other words, it is a game I look forward to playing for countless future hours.  If you aren’t a multiplayer person and were curious about picking up Halloween for its single-player offering, don’t. It lasts just a couple of hours (though you can extend its runtime by replaying chapters to find Dr. Loomis’ audio logs and complete challenges that unlock cosmetic rewards) and is just a few notches above a standard tutorial for playing as Michael. That’s not to say it’s not worth playing; it is, and that’s because while it ultimately serves as a tutorial for the game’s killer, it is also an incredibly detailed love letter to the 1978 film from director/synth player/gamer John Carpenter. The campaign is just clearly not the main attraction here, nor should it be, and I’d be leading you astray by telling you to pick it up if you were expecting a gamified adaptation of a horror movie you might love.  However, if you’re open to it, or already enjoy the asymmetrical horror genre, which pits multiple players against a single killer (Civilians vs. Michael, in this game), Halloween offers a refined and confident approach that is likely to get even better over time. Matches begin quickly, but offer upwards of 20 minutes of fun each time, though exactly how much fun depends on your ability to survive. As a Civilian, your goal is to escape, but more importantly, it is to help others escape. Primarily, should search Haddonfield’s suburbs to locate the three special target residents, which are the NPCs that call each map home. They’re not all that different from the other residents you can save in each match, but they are marked as special for you and Michael, and prioritizing them secures the most XP. It’s a fun mechanic that forces Civilians and Michael into the same places, which is where the most exciting face-offs happen.In doing so, you encounter other non-special residents, whom you can order to call the police, hide, or follow you, and ideally build the ranks of people who want to stop Michael and escape. Different playable Civilians have unique traits that affect how they play, such as Athleticism, which dictates stamina and how long you can sprint, and passive abilities that make you better at calling the police or knocking Michael down. While skilled players will take these traits into account for marginal gameplay gains, having played as each, the experience feels largely the same no matter who you play as; choosing a Civilian because they look cool, for example, is just fine. The fun isn’t dictated by your Civilian’s traits, but rather, what you get up to as them, and I appreciate that this won’t force a meta-game of who you should play as in order to win.  You can play it straight, looting cabinets, drawers, tool shops, and more for weapons to defend yourself with, repair kits to fix escape cars, or keys to open escape hatch padlocks… or you can stock up on firecrackers, lawn darts, and a baseball bat and be an absolute menace to the real player controlling Michael. The amount of fun I’ve had bullying Michael alongside other stranger Civilians in the game matches the enjoyment I get out of a successful escape; whether I’m whooping on Michael, watching him go down only to pop up for another whopping, or following the rules and picking up residents in my sedan for a four-wheel escape, I’m smiling. That is the joy of Halloween. It is as fun as you make it, and IllFonic has filled its horror toolbox with more tools than ever. Playing as Michael naturally leads to more straightforward gameplay because you’re all alone, but you possess brutal, movie-inspired killing prowess, an empowering set of abilities, and proximity chat begging for Tiny Tim to be played through. While the movies explain Michael’s ability to be everywhere and anywhere with the unknown – he’s simply the embodiment of evil, void of “whys” or “hows” – IllFonic has created smart gameplay mechanics to bring him to life. Shape Jumping speeds you around the map like a specter, though light slows him and prevents him from advancing through doors (just find the power box and disable it). Evil Presence effectively sells just how terrifying The Shape is, boosting your fear factor and terrifying anyone nearby when you use it. Even the Stalk feature, which lets you look at a resident or Civilian and increase their level of fear by holding your gaze on them, feels good, and it’s literally just a mechanic for looking at someone. Turns out, putting you behind the mask, with first-person eyeholes on screen and Michael’s inhuman breaths escaping his mangled throat, further sells that you are pure evil.  Stalking those you wish to kill makes doing so easier, and it opens you up to even more kill types, like context-specific fridge smashes, window bashes, or phone cord strangulations. Halloween can get quite brutal, and I am still eagerly chasing new ways to kill players, earn XP, and level up, which unlocks new cosmetics. IllFonic has also made commendable efforts for Halloween to be enjoyable even after you die. On-screen minigames, like mashing buttons or pressing a button within a small timed UI window, can benefit those still alive (or even bring you back into the game as a police officer or Dr. Loomis), but they get old quickly, and it sucks that you have to sit through the entire 20-ish minute match to get your XP, alive or not. I understand you don’t want players to quit the match since they might not come back in, but I’ve spent more time than I’d like playing the same boring minigames just for them to amount to little more than some additional XP. Halloween needs some massaging. There are bugs galore, its Civilian onboarding isn’t great, and leveling up to unlock new color tints on sunglasses, or a new shirt or hat, isn’t the best motivation. But when I’m actually in a match, all of that fades away. The bugs aren’t game-breaking and blend in with IllFonic’s feature-not-a-bug jank, like one where another player and I hilariously moonwalked when we ran instead of sprinted. Learning what to do as a Civilian doesn’t take too much time, even without any helpful context built in-game, and cosmetics don’t affect how I play. What matters in Halloween is the scares I’m delivering and receiving, and the joy I’m feeling through it all. Death has arrived in IllFonic’s latest, and while the fear is abundant, so too is the fun waiting in the sleepy little town of Haddonfield.  Score: 7.75 About Game Informer's review system
