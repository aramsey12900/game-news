In theory, the updated barrier around the processor die should keep the liquid metal better contained over time, ensuring stable thermal transfer performance even under intensive loads. https://twitter.com/Modyfikator89/status/2105382987755814984 Sony has also moved to a newer, lower-cost HDMI retimer chip, which likely cuts down bill-of-materials costs and improves display signal stability. Overall, this will likely lower production costs while maintaining higher assembly yields, at a time when sourcing components has become more challenging. Companies routinely make stealth revisions to cut manufacturing costs, but unlike previous console generations that shrank and became cheaper over time, the PS5 has only gotten more expensive since it launched in November 2020. When it first launched, the entry-level PS5 Digital Edition had an MSRP of $400, but in 2026, you can expect to pay $600 for one. Only two of its exclusive games support it currently--Marvel's Wolverine and Ghost of Yotei--and the technology will be released to more developers soon. Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Secret Mode Developer: Fuse Games Release: October 6, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ Star Wars: Galactic Racer is absolutely a Star Wars game, and familiar locations, musical motifs, and characters will delight fans. Developer Fuse Games crafted an excellent racing roguelite that’s enthralling, challenging, and hard to put down, even when minor frustrations choke its engines. This fun spills over into the game’s various other modes, too, making for a robust package with no shortage of tournaments to speed through. Star Wars: Galactic Racer – Video Review: Galactic Racer’s campaign is the foundation of Fuse’s revival of the Star Wars racer. You first compete to join the Galactic League to thwart champion Kestar Bool, an alien responsible for your prior imprisonment. Once in, you compete in a multi-planetary tournament and hopefully defeat him, bringing shame and embarrassment to Kestar and the treacherous Bool family. Fun twists and turns make for a story that goes above and beyond what’s typical of the genre. Great voice performances sell the stakes, and getting to know this game’s cast of allies and villains remained interesting throughout; it helps that they have no problem ribbing me via mid-race comms or outright mocking my mistakes on the track. Across multiple planets, including beautifully rendered movie highlights like Jakku and Crait, with various tracks on each (with reverse alternatives for added range), I raced so fast that familiar sights blurred into uncharted territory. This was a turn-off at first; it’s downright hard to get a grip on where you are and where you’re going, and the lack of a minimap accentuates the feeling. I respect Fuse’s commitment to tracks feeling like part of this galaxy, using materials and environmental terrain to create each one, but it’s sometimes hard to tell whether a crashed Star Destroyer’s docking bay is something I should enter or avoid on Jakku, doubly so when at top speed. But this campaign is a roguelite: Accrue too many losses and you’re dropped from the tournament. Losing is part of the gig, however, and in time, confusion on the track gave way to confidence as I blitzed through razor-thin hallways and drifted around tight corners to first place. I wouldn’t blame anyone for dropping early, though, because the initial hump is tall, and Galactic Racer does little to guide you toward success.  Each planet brings a new pitstop to talk with characters, engage with rivalries, fix damaged parts, purchase new abilities and parts, upgrade your vehicle, and renew objective-based contracts. Currency comes generously, as do upgrades and new parts earned after winning races, and crafting a new engine type each run keeps roguelite repetition from getting boring. Abilities like auto-pilot gave me moments to breathe in tougher stretches, while lock-on ion blasts, corrosion vents, and shockwaves gave me an edge against some of the more dangerous racers on the circuit. Because these racers stay the same in each tournament run, I learned how each raced, whether they were offensive or defensive, and the abilities they typically use, which adds a real sense of athleticism to the campaign. Upgrading stats like your afterburner, drifting capability, or battery – which lets you equip more abilities – enabled me to craft an engine that works best for my playstyle. As someone all about speed, my upgrades went into the ramjet, which is a powerful but dangerous redlining turbo; the afterburner to increase my overall speed; and the battery, so I could use additional speed-granting abilities like Surge more often. But there’s room to build a vehicle focused on offensive maneuvers, great for elimination events, or countering environmental hazards like acid or ice, and I’m still finding weird but surprisingly effective builds. Galactic Racer focuses its racing on drifting landspeeders, speeder bikes that can stop on a dime and burst forward with extra speed, and skim speeders, which can cut through corners using a “knife’s edge” turn style. Though landspeeders brought me the most success, speeder bikes and skim speeders bring their own amusement to the course. The Arcade mode, with time trials, advanced races, and more, and the Scenario mode, with quick, curated six-race campaigns where you play as other pre-crafted characters/vehicles, forced me to leave the comfort of my campaign-crafted cockpit and try something new. In turn, I discovered even more avenues of racing fun. The same goes for the multiplayer, which smartly brings over most of the roguelite upgrade offerings and places them within quick six-race tournaments, complete with pre-race bets against other racers to raise the personal stakes. Single quick races, an XP leveling system, and cosmetic unlocks round out the multiplayer suite with enough to chase after for dozens more hours. There’s a fourth vehicle type too, though: the podracer. Fuse clearly delights in infusing this legendary racing engine into the game’s narrative, and there’s a lot of pomp and circumstance around just how dangerous and fast podracers are compared to the other three. The thing is, however, is that they simply are too fast and dangerous, and being forced to use them in specific campaign events at the risk of ruining an entire run remained a frustration throughout. Going from the comfort of my own vehicle for five to six races, only to be forced to use a podracer with the risk of my run ending should I lose, was the wrong kind of left turn. Podracing is exciting, as the associated speed and unruliness make races unpredictable. But it’s nice that it’s available in other modes, including multiplayer where you can race exclusively against other podracers. I get that a Star Wars racing game without the canon’s most popular vehicle might be a miss, but I would have preferred them to stay relegated to a bonus style of racing in other modes, not an irritating, unavoidable core of the campaign. Twenty hours later, with the campaign behind me and plenty more time sunk into the other offerings, there’s still so much Galactic Racer left. I spent 45 minutes on one track yesterday not for the gold medal, which I already had, but for the chance to beat a fellow reviewer’s time on the leaderboard, and I don’t regret a minute. Galactic Racer is a Star Wars game, and that might be enough for some, but not for me. Fortunately, it’s an even better racing game, one filled to the brim with courses, vehicle builds, and characters that keep me coming back to the track for one more race.  Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system

Dune: Awakening Review - Only The Strong SurviveNEW
Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S ...
Reviewed on: Xbox Series X/S Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Funcom Developer: Funcom Release: June 10, 2025 (PC), September 22, 2026 (PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S) Rating: Mature As much as Dune: Awakening guides you around Arrakis, it can be a hot and unforgiving place. Almost everything around you is trying to kill you, and if not, then you're being used as a pawn to further someone's agenda. Awakening succeeds in making players feel like everyone is out to get them, all while encouraging you to rise to the occasion and come out the other end stronger.Dune: Awakening is set in its own timeline. Here, things play out very differently than in the films and books. Paul Atreides' mother gives birth to a daughter, as she was supposed to. Because of this, House Atreides never falls, and a War of Assassins between House Harkonnen and House Atreides begins. As a result, the Fremen are all but dead. Awakening takes time to explain the ins and outs of the game. With a planet known for being as hot as it is, it only makes sense that the sun's rays are one of the constant threats. Learning how to survive never felt like a chore; it was a necessary sequence to learn. By the end, I truly felt like I was setting myself up to succeed. Exploring the map is a lot of fun. Whenever I saw a crashed ship or a small camp in the distance, I knew there would either be minerals worth mining or a dungeon to run. I never felt I wasted my time whenever I went off the beaten path. You're always encouraged to explore new areas and see what mysteries await you as you get into the desert.If it's by accident or on purpose, one of the best things you can stumble across is the feud between House Harkonnen and House Atreides. Here is where Awakening really starts to home in on the idea of never trusting anyone. As you align yourself with one house or the other, you're always encouraged and given the option to betray the one you've sided with to help the other. Even though it's such a prominent part of the overall story, it never really affects your character's personal journey. Having no real consequences is a missed opportunity when you consider that both Houses have areas you can visit throughout the world. There was never any danger when I walked into Harkonnen territory well after I sided with Atreides. The only hostilities I faced were a few rude remarks and higher prices on certain items.Combat, on the other hand, never falls short, thanks in large part to classes that grant you ample agency in how you approach the game. Throughout most of my playthrough, I prioritized the Mentat, which gave me the ability to drop a turret anywhere, and the Trooper, which allowed me to use a grappling hook and grenades. Combat played out in several different ways for me. If multiple people rushed me, I'd throw a grenade and drop my turret. If there was a sniper off in the distance, I'd use my hook to close the gap. The freedom I had with whatever threat was in front of me made going into combat a joy. If I got bored of one playstyle, I could invest in another skill and try out something new altogether. Each class has its own set of perks and benefits that keep combat fresh and fun.Being able to handle whatever was thrown at me was vital when exploring Awakening's main questline. The first half of the main story does a fantastic job of showcasing the importance of the Fremen while also serving as an extended tutorial. The way the game romanticizes the Fremen is a breath of fresh air compared with the harsh realities of living on Arrakis. By the end of the opening act, you'll feel you learned a lot about how the world used to be before the war began.This contrasts sharply with the game's second half. It serves as a reminder of the daily horrors people endure on the planet, all while making you question your humanity and whether you even have a say in what's going on around you. The end of the base game left me in awe of everything thrown at me and of how far they took an original idea. Another aspect of the story I appreciate is how approachable it was for solo players. Even though there's a single-player option, I chose a multiplayer server and experienced most of the game alone. I never felt like I hit a wall because I didn't have the manpower to deal with whatever was in front of me. If there was, it was simply because I wasn't playing smart. The few times I did play with someone else only made the game more enjoyable. Even though I haven't fully explored the post-game, the time I've spent in the Deep Desert was what I'd hoped it would be. This area can only be reached by Orentogether, which you unlock toward the endgame, and can be experienced in PvE or PvP. Regardless of how you tackle it, the Deep Desert seems to go on forever and resets once a week, so fully exploring it is a task in itself. However, Deep Desert also boasts some of the best loot and dungeons the game has to offer.If you want to take it a step further, you can also try the Landsraad. This feature pits both Houses against one another and tasks them with completing a specific goal. Whichever side comes out on top is rewarded with loot on par with what you'd get in the Deep Desert. While it doesn’t add much to the story, it aptly supports the fantasy of truly being part of the ongoing conflict.Even if you know nothing about Dune, the game does a great job of explaining the significance of each house and why you should care about everything around you. Dune: Awakening has everything it needs to remain true to the franchise: endless deserts, war and espionage on all fronts, and of course giant sand worms. It takes a lot of creative freedom and hits many of the important marks, all while reminding you of the brutal reality of living on Arrakis.  Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
