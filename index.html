Not-so-Ordinary beginnings https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qO7ebIRggv0 Set in 2019, the first game opens with Jesse Faden arriving at the Federal Bureau of Control, a government agency that oversees paranormal phenomena. Jesse is originally from the fictional Maine town of Ordinary, which was subject to a supernatural occurrence in 2002 that the FBC refers to as an AWE, or Altered World Event.  As children, Jesse, her brother Dylan, and their friends found an old slide projector in the town’s landfill. It turns out this slide projector is something the FBC calls an Object of Power: an item from our world that has become preternaturally infused and can even imbue those who interact with it with powers of their own. In this case, the projector acted as a doorway to other dimensions when different slides were inserted into it. One of the slides housed a benevolent entity that made contact with Jesse and Dylan. Jesse described the otherworldly being as “like a star”, dubbing it Polaris, and it would be telepathically connected to her for the rest of her life. The FBC showed up at Ordinary to claim the slide projector after it caused an Altered World Event (AWE) that resulted in the disappearance of the town’s adult population and physically altered some of the kids who had discovered the Object of Power. In addition, the Faden siblings’ interaction with Polaris had turned them into parautilitarians, humans with a supernatural disposition. Parautilitarians are of great interest to the Bureau, as being one is a requirement for becoming Director of the FBC, and so the Bureau attempted to detain the Faden siblings, although it only managed to snag Dylan, with Jesse escaping. Before doing so, however, Jesse had managed to end the AWE with the help of Polaris, which told her how to switch off the slide projector and burn most of the slides to prevent further catastrophe, except for the one slide that connected her to Polaris. Welcome to the Federal Bureau of Control Jesse would spend the rest of her life trying to find her brother, but it was only when Polaris called out to her and revealed the FBC’s headquarters in Manhattan that she found out where Dylan was being kept. The Bureau’s headquarters, known as the Oldest House, is itself an Object of Power, but as it’s a location, it’s referred to as a Place of Power; the walls and halls within it constantly shift and change. When Jesse arrives, the Oldest House has gone into lockdown, as it has become infested with a paranormal resonance known as the Hiss. The Hiss is a hivemind that infects and warps most of the FBC staff, save for the few who manage to get their hands on a Hedron Resonance Amplifier (HRA), a technological vest that prevents its wearer from Hiss contamination. While Jesse does not have an HRA of her own, her connection to Polaris holds the Hiss resonance at bay. In the early moments of her time at the Oldest House, Jesse discovers the Bureau’s director, Zachariah Trench, dead in his office, having committed suicide with a weird-looking gun designated the Service Weapon. When Jesse picks it up, she is transported to the Astral Plane, the reality between dimensions.  This is where the Board resides, yet another supernatural being that oversees the management of the FBC. Since Jesse is a parautilitarian, she can wield the Service Weapon, connecting her to the Board, which deems her worthy of succeeding Trench as the FBC’s new Director.  Within the Oldest House, Jesse meets several surviving FBC employees, including researcher Emily Pope and the Bureau's head of security, Simon Arish. In return for her help in combating the Hiss, the remaining FBC agents promise to provide Jesse with information on her brother. She ultimately discovers that, while undergoing training to potentially become the next Bureau Director, Dylan lost control of his powers, killing a number of FBC agents and resulting in his being locked away at the Panopticon, a location within the Oldest House where the most dangerous Objects of Power are stored. Reunited at last Once Jesse finally makes contact with her brother, she discovers that he has been infected by the Hiss, although he remains relatively lucid, almost as if he has merged with the hivemind’s resonance rather than having been completely taken over by it. This is primarily due to his innate power as a parautilitarian, but potentially in part because he also welcomed the Hiss, as he had grown incredibly resentful of the Bureau for his abduction and mistreatment. Dylan reveals to his sister that the Hiss made its way into the Oldest House thanks to the slide projector they discovered as kids. Jesse also uncovers a wealth of information around the FBC’s experiments involving the slide projector while exploring the Oldest House. It turns out that Trench, along with the Bureau’s head of research, Casper Darling, had been leading expeditions into the single slide that survived the Ordinary AWE; the one connecting her to Polaris. The FBC discovered the entity and brought it into our world, with Darling naming it Hedron after its shape. However, while exploring Hedron’s plane of reality, the Hiss made contact with Trench and slowly infected his mind. Give us a Hiss The Hiss was originally from one of the slides Jesse had burned, but a piece of it had burrowed its way into Hedron’s world, and it wanted to use Trench to unseal its native reality. It seems the Hiss is a being that simply wants to spread as far across the multiverse as possible, with a potential ultimate goal of accessing the Astral Plane and infecting the Board itself. While Trench succumbed to the Hiss, Darling began to commune with Hedron. Through it he learned of Trench’s infection, as well as how to build the HRAs that could tap into Hedron’s protective resonance. Once he fully submitted to the Hiss, Trench inserted the burnt slide that connected to its homeworld into the projector and turned it on, allowing it to overrun the Oldest House. Beforehand, Darling disappeared, potentially into Hedron’s slide, leaving behind as many HRAs as he could build before the infectious entity arrived. Simon Ordell’s journey through the Scottish town of St. Amelia in 1996 is not without its shortcomings, but the experience is an affecting one buoyed by the first-person perspective. Silent Hill has dabbled in first-person before with Silent Hill 4: The Room, 2024's experimental The Short Message, and the whole P.T. debacle. We also, of course, all definitely played and remember the on-rails shooter, Silent Hill: The Arcade. Townfall, however, is the first time the franchise has fully embraced the perspective. The view makes the game unique within the Silent Hill oeuvre. Through Simon’s eyes, St. Amelia is overbearing and imposing in all the intended ways. That the game looks great also helps. The claustrophobic interiors are full of details that reward inspection, and you can practically taste the fog while exploring outdoors. The peeking mechanic is also expertly integrated and makes it feel like you truly are cowering and exposing as little of yourself as possible while staying quiet and keeping an eye on monsters.The broad story, at least structurally, will be familiar to Silent Hill players, which I offer as a compliment. Townfall may take place in St. Amelia, but there is no question that this is a Silent Hill game. Simon and others are confronting their inner demons in a foggy town, and what exactly is happening or why the streets are littered with monsters is never truly clear. You do know, however, that something is off and the only way out is through. Uncharacteristically, compared to past Silent Hills, the individual plots that have led to the emotional distress of Townfall’s cast are more transparent. There are fewer questions about why characters feel what they’re feeling. It eliminates some of the dreamlike qualities that make these games ethereal and mysterious, but I am not opposed to more clarity, as sometimes information is withheld unnecessarily. I like the narrative balance Townfall strikes.Simon has an era-appropriate portable television that lets him track monsters through the screen’s static, and it does a good job of letting you monitor creatures’ whereabouts while maintaining intensity. Combat is heavily discouraged, with perhaps the fewest number of bullets in any Silent Hill game (that feature guns, anyway) and melee weapons that expire quickly. As a result, Townfall is a stealth game, but I struggled with understanding what alerted enemies to my presence. Sometimes crouching and moving slowly behind cover was enough. Sometimes enemies saw me anyway. The inconsistency made me reload saves often, which diminished the mood of the encounters. I got particularly frustrated with one invincible, instantly-kill-you-if-caught monster who never seemed to walk away no matter how long I tracked him on my TV while hiding in a closet. In these moments, I was bored and annoyed instead of scared.What I have enjoyed so much about Konami’s renewed efforts with Silent Hill is how it is finding developers like Screen Burn Interactive that understand what makes Silent Hill so intriguing and are managing to produce unique experiences within an admittedly undefined structure. The franchise is about loneliness and trauma and how that affects your emotional state of mind. Townfall presents these emotions well, and my only frustrations arose when I was forced to grapple with some of its unbalanced video game mechanics. Score: 8.25 About Game Informer's review system
