7 Cheaper Alternatives For Popular GamesNEW

Gaming has never been more expensive. We’re here to investigate why, and help you play more while spending less. Welcome to GameSpot Cheap Week. Massive, popular games go on sale all the time, but there are a plethora of titles that emulate those experiences with a lower starting price than the typical $60-$70 price tag. With that in mind, here are some fantastic games that are often cheaper than other titles in their wheelhouse, with many also bringing fresh ideas to the table. We've assembled this list based on these games' lower-than-average retail prices, but you'll often find them on sale for even less, making them even more of a steal. Another Crab's Treasure (souls-likes) Another Crab’s Treasure, which has a starting price of $30, is not only a great souls-like, but also features a bright and colorful world--a stark contrast to the doom and gloom often associated with the genre. Its main character, a hermit crab named Krill, has more in common aesthetically with the likes of Mario or Spyro than he does the Tarnished. Yet be that as it may, Another Crab's Treasure is every bit as enticing (and challenging) as many other beloved souls-like games. Another Crab’s Treasure also expands upon the genre, introducing 3D platformer elements that provide some nice gameplay variety when mixed in with the game’s combat. Combined with other novel ideas, such as Krill’s ability to interchange his shell for different effects in encounters, and an unexpectedly thoughtful story, Another Crab’s Treasure is a great experience for anyone who considers themselves a fan of souls games. See at Fanatical Cassette Beasts (Pokemon) Pokemon games are notorious for a lot of things, including rarely being discounted significantly. If you’ve been looking to get a modern pocket monster fix at a reduced price, check out Cassette Beasts, which retails for $20 (or the same amount that Nintendo charges for Pokemon Fire Red and Leaf Green, 20+ year-old games, on the Switch eShop). Cassette Beasts borrows heavily from the series that inspired it while also introducing several fantastic mechanics, such as fusions. This is a feature many fans of Pokemon have posited could be added to that franchise, and consists of taking two creatures and fusing them into a single entity for the duration of a match, greatly expanding upon gameplay variety and adding new layers of strategy. Featuring an open-world that you can explore with another player locally or up to eight players online, as well as the ability to play it on devices other than the ones Nintendo makes, Cassette Beasts is a delightful throwback with modern sensibilities at a great price. See at Fanatical The Crew 2 (Forza Horizon) Forza Horizon opened up the world of simulation racing to a whole new audience by injecting a healthy dose of arcade flavor into the mix. In the years since, it has become one of Xbox’s most popular franchises both on and off its consoles. If you’ve been wanting to jump into the series with Forza Horizon 6, but don’t want to subscribe to Xbox Game Pass Ultimate Edition or shell out $70, you should take a look at The Crew 2, which now retails for only $30. While cars are front and center in The Crew 2, the game features a ton of other vehicles to explore and compete with in its open world with, such as boats, bikes, and planes. This offers something entirely new even for those who have already had a taste of Forza Horizon. Additionally, when in free roam or specific match types, the game also allows you to switch to a different vehicle type in an instant, making it unlike any other racing game you can play. These quality-of-life changes set The Crew 2 apart, but its core gameplay still adheres to a familiar, popular formula that is sure to appeal to Forza fans. See at Steam RoboCop: Rogue City (FPS Campaigns) Sadly, not a lot of first-person shooters nowadays launch with a strong emphasis on their single-player experience. Instead, many opt to center these games around multiplayer experiences to various degrees of success. However, those looking for a meaty story campaign with great FPS gameplay are not entirely out of luck thanks to Robocop: Rogue City, which retails for $40. The game authentically captures the dystopian world featured in the movies and fills it with interesting side stories that you can undertake in a contained, open-world Detroit. It also sets itself apart from most other modern FPS games, which tend to err on the side of speed and agility, by taking a more methodical approach to the genre. RoboCop is a meticulously moving, nigh-unstoppable machine, and the game perfectly adapts that trait into satisfying gameplay. And did I mention actor Peter Weller returns to voice the titular character? If you’re hungry for more mechanical mayhem after completing Rogue City, there’s the standalone expansion Unfinished Business, which costs even less. See at Fanatical Neon White (Doom) One of the few FPS series that does release games with a focus on single player is Doom, which delights with its frenetic, aggressive gameplay. Anyone who’s enjoyed a Doom game, or been interested in the series, would love Neon White, which features a comfortable starting price of $25. As with Doom, Neon White sees you face off against the hordes of hell, but this time, you’re doing so in the sunlit, angelic spires of heaven, which have become infested with demons. The game features similarly energetic gameplay to what you experience in a Doom title, but with a delightful twist: Soul Cards. Each Soul Card you collect gives you a different weapon to use in your demonic slaughter, but sacrificing those cards also provides specific movement mechanics depending on the card being discarded, resulting in a bespoke tactical approach for every run. It’s a unique FPS experience, while costing much less than many other titles in the genre. See at Fanatical Kena: Bridge of Spirits (Action Adventure) In a sea of modern action adventure games, Kena: Bridge of Spirits stands out for its aesthetic and its $40 price tag. Studio Ember Labs brought its years of film animation know-how to the world of game development, and with it made Bridge of Spirits, an impressive debut that is able to stand toe-to-toe with other, higher-priced games in the genre. Its familiar combat can be deceptively difficult while not being overly punishing, and when mixed in with the use of the Rot, little Ghibli-esque creatures Kena can command to help solve puzzles, Bridge of Spirits has a satisfying mechanical depth to it that is matched in quality by its engaging, emotional story. Plus, Ember Labs has announced an upcoming sequel, Scars of Kosmora, so it's a great time to explore this studio’s inaugural title. See at Steam The Ascent (Cyberpunk 2077) While cyberpunk is a popular aesthetic, there are only a handful of titles in the genre that deliver a satisfying gameplay fantasy in that world. Fortunately, The Ascent is one of them, and retails at $30. It offers everything players have come to expect from a cyberpunk game: a neon-soaked megacity, augmentations and implants for their character, and multiple elements within the world that are open to hacking. Featuring twin-stick shooter gameplay, The Ascent includes a critical feature that is missing from the marquee title in the genre, Cyberpunk 2077: multiplayer, both local and online. For anyone that is looking to jump into a mechanical dystopia with deep gameplay at a discounted price--and potentially bring along their friends for the ride--The Ascent is the game for you. To stagger them, you must nail a parry-like howl before an enemy lands a hit, opening a brief window to go for the neck. I like turning several enemies to my side early, letting me roll up on rivals with a small posse backing me. At best, it lets me overwhelm an opponent; at worst, I’m surrounded by distractions and living shields. This parry is also useful against other players, as it can interrupt their attacks. My favorite Kin is Baritone, a singing behemoth whose vocals grant stat buffs as he slams threats using wrestling moves. Other Kin include a health-boosting gargoyle and a nimble, lightning-quick dual-sword-wielding warrior. They’re all as strange as they are useful, and although you can recall them until they’re needed, I rarely saw an opponent possessing a Kin that wasn’t exploring without it constantly by their side. You can switch to a different Kin if you find another portal; however, they’ll be at Level 1. So even if you find a Kin you like better, it may be strategically unwise to swap out a higher-level Kin to use it. At the event, 16 journalists and influencers were divided into two tables – eight each – letting us play against each other in randomized configurations on a closed server. Although the game features random matchmaking, Miyazaki confirmed The Duskbloods features private lobbies to play only with friends when I asked him during the group interview. Each match, called a Dusk Battle, pits eight players against each other and consists of four timed Phases, where players are gradually eliminated as the match progresses. The last player standing wins. Winning a Dusk Battle requires accumulating Virtue, a resource earned in several ways: in battle by defeating other players, smaller threats, and challenging bosses, and by exploring and finding Virtue-infused corpses among other secrets. Repulsion Virtue, for example, is earned by defeating bosses ranging from mid-tier foes to greater threats known as Trespassers. To acquire Commendation Virtue, you must perform smaller feats, like killing a large number of common foes or collecting more dropped items than other players. Flame Virtue is obtained from purple throne-seated corpses hidden throughout the map. The first person to touch Flame Virtue will be the only one to do so, as it’s a one-time, first-come-first-served reward. Being the first person to discover a Flame Virtue is always exhilarating; conversely, the disappointment of approaching a Flame Virtue only to find that someone else already activated it is immeasurable.  In a battle-royale-esque format, players with the most Virtue at the end of a phase advance to subsequent rounds, while those who don’t hit a Virtue quota are eliminated. I didn’t clock it, but each Phase feels like it takes between 8-10 minutes. Phase I features no eliminations, so it’s best spent gathering strength and unlocking new skills by defeating enemies and searching for Virtue. I spent most of this time killing everything that moves to quickly level up, as doing so also unlocks level-gated special abilities for Bloodsworn, while trying to find Flame Virtue before other players got to it. There’s no penalty for dying during a match; you’ll just respawn to a chosen area on the map. Each player has two health flasks, a limit that can be increased by finding a special item. Character-specific missions called Sigils offer another optional avenue to earn Virtue. Each Bloodsworn has an accompanying Sigil that can be enabled before each match; Albert, for example, has the Sigil of a Sworn Enemy, which tasks the player with hunting and killing a marked player. Sigil of Old Friend requires seeking out and befriending someone by offering an alliance, a broader mechanic that lets two players team up (more on this later). Sigil of the Eye of Moralis is similar to that of the Sworn Enemy, in that players must hunt another player branded as a Heretic; however, the Heretic can also defeat their hunter for rewards. The last Sigil available in this build is the Sigil of Love, the most bizarre of the bunch since it requires the player to propose marriage to a designated rival (yes, really). If the proposal is accepted, those players become lovers, though it’s unclear what the other player gains from this union.  Eliminations go live in Phases II and III. Phase II “culls”, to use the game’s term, three bottom-ranked players, and Phase III cuts two more. These Phases also introduce Ritual Sites, special zones where players can earn heaps of Virtue by winning PvP battles or capturing and defending special checkpoints. If you’re confident in your battle prowess, besting other players at Ritual Sites rewards Sword Virtue. Those who prefer to avoid conflict can chase Pledge Virtue by activating golden symbols scattered across the Ritual Site. However, Pledge Virtue is not inherently conflict-free; someone else can activate your Pledge to steal it, so it may be wise to stay near Pledge zones to defend them if necessary. Ritual Sites unlock towards the end of Phases II and III and always devolved into utter, entertaining chaos as players converged on these points to frantically earn enough Virtue to stay in the game or reinforce their leads by preventing opponents from gaining their rewards. Dusk Battles can also be spiced up by Events, special stipulations that affect the entire map. These randomly selected modifiers flip the entire game on its head for all players. One Event spawns a zeppelin over the stage that rains fire upon the map, incinerating anyone caught in its blasts. One particularly annoying/amusing Event periodically zaps every player with unavoidable lightning, but instead of electrocuting them, they’re teleported to a random spot on the map. In one instance, I was warped right in front of a Trespasser, and it promptly clapped me. Another Event tasks players with reaching the top of Lowarno City’s bell tower and ringing said bell to earn extra Virtue. One neat Event sees a shooting star crash somewhere on the map; the player who can grab its shard and hold on to it at the end of the Phase earns bonus Virtue.  Once Phase III culls the remaining losers, the final top three players advance to Phase IV. This unfolds as an often chaotic arena battle where the last player standing wins the match. Arenas are much smaller than the main map and vary in type; one lacks walls, for example, allowing players to fall or be tossed over the edge Super Smash Bros.-style (which some players took full advantage of). Players have two lives to spend here, so dying a third time marks an elimination. I only reached Phase IV a handful of times, coming in 3rd place each attempt. Between facing off against leveled-up players, not to mention whichever powered-up Kin they’re rolling with, winning these bouts can be extremely challenging. Sometimes I found success hanging back and letting my two opponents duke it out while taking potshots from afar; of course, they inevitably realized what I was doing and would then (understandably) double-team me. Victory is hard-earned, but Phase IV also has the excitement of a hype fighting game match. Eliminated players routinely gathered around the monitors of whoever made it to the last round to watch them duke it out, eliciting plenty of cheers, groans, and laughter from the room. Although you can reach Phase IV using the non-combative methods mentioned above, it is the round where the familiar phrase “git gud” is most applicable. The single map (or "Lands of Dusk" as they're called) available in the build I played, Lowarno City, is a multi-layered locale emphasizing verticality. It features multiple distinct areas, including a cathedral, a cave network, underground ruins, a poisonous marsh (of course), and a massive bell tower, among other areas I know I missed. You can also find special chests that improve your melee weapons with enhancements, such as elemental boons like fire and lightning or poison damage. The scale of Lowarno City is impressive; it's big enough to make me feel isolated in a way that I love in FromSoftware titles, but not too large that I didn’t see another player semi-regularly, even briefly, at some point. Lowarno City is one of three maps planned for the final game. I’m curious to see the others, but I really enjoyed exploring this first battlefield.  The Value of Teamwork The Value of TeamworkAlthough The Duskbloods is a competitive experience first, you can work with other players either loosely or under a formal agreement called Alliances. Initiating an Alliance with another player requires them to accept; if they do, the two of you become partners for the remainder of the Dusk Battle.Alliances can be offered any time during a Dusk Battle before Phase IV, and partnered players can’t hurt each other. Both players share certain rewards. Should they make it to Phase IV, the Alliance ends since the players must become opponents. Ending an Alliance prematurely results in a penalty for the player who calls it off, forcing them to surrender a portion of Virtue (and potentially other resources, though this is unclear) to their former partner. Alliances can be a great way for bottom-ranked players to increase their odds of survival by working together. Duos Mode is a separate form of Dusk Battle that divides players into teams of two (making four teams total). Like Alliances, partners can’t damage each other but share Virtue and weapon enhancements. A single team is eliminated each round. Phase IV pits the two top-ranked teams against each other, and teams share a pool of two lives.  A Story Told Through Battle A Story Told Through Battle FromSoftware enthusiasts love dissecting the lore and story of the studio's single-player games. Although The Duskbloods is a multiplayer experience, it's not devoid of narrative. When I ask Miyazaki how the game delivers a story, he reveals that the House of Night, the explorable mansion hub where you initiate Dusk Battles, features a special NPC who won't be accessible until The Duskbloods' full launch. This NPC is located on the second floor of the House of Night, and as players regularly speak to them, "you'll sort of feel more about the story and will progress the story aspect to a certain extent," says Miyazaki.Miyazaki also reveals Bloodlines, a customization element tied to Bloodsworn, offers another storytelling device. "...Basically, as you progress, as you play with certain characters in the multiplayer, it will sort of unlock aspects of that customization that then reveal things like their history, their relationships with other characters, things like that," Miyazaki explains. "So the story will kind of be revealed in that way as well." Furthermore, Miyazaki adds that the emergent moments from playing Dusk Battles tell their own stories unique to each player while fleshing out Bloodlines. "And so, through multiplayer, with things like the Sigil system and the Events system, basically as you're playing and this sort of unexpected drama unfolds, that can also unlock new Bloodlines and sort of it can branch off from there as well, depending on who you're interacting with, what kind of events occur with what characters, who has what Sigil, things like that. So there's always this sort of unexpected way that the story might reveal itself to you as you're playing the game, outside of just the House of Night.” A Promising First Impression A Promising First ImpressionI like to enjoy FromSoftware games as solitary experiences. I’ve never summoned a human player to help me in other Souls games, and though I respect Elden Ring Nightreign and gave it a fair shake, I just didn’t like having to rely on other players to get the most out of the experience. Call me antisocial, but one reason I much prefer The Duskbloods is that I can jump into matches without having to align schedules with friends or coordinate with a randomly assigned partner (because I’m not unmuting my mic for strangers). The Duskbloods is a solo experience first; cooperation is merely circumstantial and intentionally strategic. Overall, I’m somewhat surprised how much I enjoy The Duskbloods. Much of that has to do with not needing to engage in PvP to get Virtue – I gathered plenty of it by exploring the map and fighting AI enemies while rarely encountering other players. By playing it like a single-player game, I’m still competing with everyone else and even winning. There were matches where The Duskbloods felt like a small Elden Ring sequel due to the surprising scale and diversity of Lowarno City, letting me soak in the surreal sights, albeit to a lesser degree. But the urgency introduced by the timer and Virtue score adds an intense wrinkle I enjoy, and the overall structure reminds me why I generally enjoy battle royales; I can play alone and get ahead in whatever manner I deem most strategically viable based on my skill level and loadout. The Duskbloods adds a potent and entertaining FromSoftware twist to what already works for me in that genre. The best compliment I can give is that I ended each match eager to begin another. The game’s mix of skill, strategy, and luck makes each match dynamic, exciting, hilarious, and unpredictable. Getting narrowly eliminated made me punch the air, while competing in the final round is genuinely thrilling. The characters are fun and varied, catering to multiple playstyles; if you prefer to deal damage from afar, go with Sniper Layla, while tank-loving players should roll with Zork. And if you’re concerned about the platform choice, don’t be: The Duskbloods performed perfectly fine on Switch 2 (which really shouldn’t be surprising; the console’s not a potato by any stretch) to the point I forgot it was the platform running it. There are still plenty of mysteries surrounding the game. What does the main narrative entail? Does the game utilize any Switch 2-specific features (Miyazaki immediately said he couldn’t speak on this when a journalist posed this question during our group interview)? What does post-launch support look like? We still have plenty to discover after the closed network test concludes, and that’s exciting. FromSoftware is on to something here, and like the game’s vampiric competitors, I’m thirsty to spill – and drink – more blood in many matches to come.
