Rainbow 6 Tactics Looks Like If Siege And XCOM: Chimera Squad Had A BabyNEW
During Gamescom Opening Night Live, Ubisoft announced the next game in the long-running Tom Clancy series: Rainbow 6 Tactics. The announcement trailer showcased how the turn-based tactics game will pl...
During Gamescom Opening Night Live, Ubisoft announced the next game in the long-running Tom Clancy series: Rainbow 6 Tactics. The announcement trailer showcased how the turn-based tactics game will play--it looks pretty much like Rainbow Six Siege and XCOM: Chimera Squad had a baby. And it's entirely single-player, which is great! I love playing Siege, but after years away, I simply cannot enjoy it anymore. The game has too many operators to keep track of (any hero shooter with 50+ characters is too much) and the skill ceiling to enjoy the game is so high--I've simply fallen behind and every month away makes it harder to try and jump back in. Rainbow 6 Tactics looks appealing because it lets me get back into Siege (kinda) without having to worry about other online players. And the game looks very cool, likely because it's being helmed by Ubisoft Paris, a studio that helped create Mario + Rabbids Sparks of Hope, which was super excellent for taking the formula for turn-based tactics games and evolving it. In Sparks of Hope's case, it was rewarding the strategy of purposeful movement. Rainbow 6 Tactics seems to also be building on the turn-based-tactics game formula, but instead focusing hard on how a battle starts. Like Siege, Rainbow 6 Tactics' missions are split into phases. In the initial phase, you use drones to uncover enemy positions, hostage locations, defensive armaments, and more. When that's done, you use whatever information you have to strategize during the breach planning phase, positioning your chosen operators at specific entry points and dictating how they'll get inside--burst through a window, knock down a wall, shoot a target from distance, etc. When you execute, all of your operators then act simultaneously. From there, the rest of the mission proceeds as most turn-based-tactics games do, with each side taking their turn in combat, trying to land shots depending on a percentage chance to hit, and using cover and special abilities to turn the tide of battle. And you direct one operator at a time, like other games. The one big difference to it seems to take inspiration from Siege: breakable environments.  And, of course, that first part of every mission looks very different from most other turn-based tactics games. That system of set-up and breach phases really intrigues me. The game seems to be putting a huge onus on trying to set yourself up for success as much as possible on turn one with a perfectly executed and synchronized assault, potentially putting yourself into a tricky scenario if you don't do your due diligence. So many tactics games see you reacting to an enemy already aware of you, so I'm interested to see how a game in the genre works where you essentially have a turn before a mission even begins. In the game, you play as Paul Bishop, aka Six (a familiar face for those who played Rainbow Six: Vegas 2). You have 15 of Siege's operators under your command and you're trying to rebuild Team Rainbow while still responding to terrorist threats. In a pre-brief for the trailer announcement, we saw Ash, Zofia, Nøkk, Glaz, Doc, Montagne, Sledge, Finka, and Twitch--community developer Emilie Cerkiewicz says that the chosen operators are among the most "iconic" of Siege and that players will see fan-favorites, so I assume the likes of Smoke, Hibana, and Dokkaebi will be there too. Rainbow 6 Tactics' story will have dozens of handcrafted maps and take place between the events of Siege Year 10 and Year 11 (the current season). This probably means that Year 11 operators Solid Snake and Noor aren't in the game.  Rainbow 6 Tactics is set to launch in 2027 for PC, PlayStation 5, and Xbox Series X|S. The game will have no in-game store (like, at all) or in-game monetization systems, and won't be a live-service title--all of which feels impossible for a new Ubisoft game, but that's what Cerkiewicz said during the pre-brief, so who am I to doubt?

Acornia: Mirror Worlds Hands-On Preview – A Fun New Nut To CrackNEW
...
Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Dotemu Developer: Splashteam Release: 2027 Rating: Everyone The Pikmin-inspired platformer Tinykin was my favorite surprise of 2022, and I’ve eagerly awaited the next project from its developer, Splashteam. My wait came to an end in April when I attended an event hosted by publisher Dotemu in Paris, France, where I not only learned what Splashteam is cooking up next, but also played what is decidedly not Tinykin 2.Acornia: Mirror Worlds shares the same visual aesthetic as Tinykin; it places beautiful, personality-filled 2D characters in a fully 3D world. The game also retains the “little person, big world” design, as it is set inside a snowglobe sitting in a bedroom; the implications of this are anyone’s guess. The comparisons end here, however.  The adventure stars a spunky squirrel named Alva who must defend her treetop home from a malevolent corruption overtaking it. The action game shares much in common with Kirby and twin-stick shooters, as Alva’s primary ability is sucking up anything not bolted to the ground into her massive cheeks (she’s a squirrel, after all) and firing it back at targets. She can store multiple objects in several quantities at once to unleash on targets in rapid-fire fashion, with players using the analog sticks to aim. Every item, whether it's various foods like fruits, building materials like stones and wood, or larger objects, deals varying degrees of damage. Even enemies can be sucked up and spat back at their allies for some forced friendly fire. You can cycle through each object much like selecting different types of ammo in a shooter, and it’s fun to pepper incoming threats using whatever is lying nearby. However, Acornia’s core lies in a mechanic that lets Alva explore an alternate, darker dimension. Thanks to a magical mirror orb, Alva can freely enter a dark mirror world called the Duramen, which resembles her normal world but with key differences in layout. Jumping to the Duramen can be used to bypass obstacles in the “light” world, such as large roots blocking a road I encounter early in my demo. Entering the Duramen lets me access the literal roots of this problem and destroy them, removing them from the main world. The Duramen also hosts multiple dungeons that players will explore to defeat bosses and grab valuables used for the game’s other big mechanics: shopkeeping and town-building.  The multi-story tree can be improved to attract citizens and allow new shops to open. Alva herself runs a store, and fans of games like Moonlighter will find similarities to Acornia’s gameplay split of dungeon-crawling action and cozy shopkeeping. Alva’s store can be decorated by crafting furniture using materials gathered while exploring, and running the shop involves selling merchandise that appeals to certain customers. Moles love cherries, for example, and sentient happy logs will eagerly buy pistachios. Acornia: Mirror Worlds isn’t what I expected from Splashteam, but it oozes much of the same charm and humor of Tinykin, and the gameplay I sampled was fun and varied. Even during my limited time playing, I could tell I only scratched the surface of what appears to be a deep gameplay loop. Splashteam already cracked one nut by doing Pikmin arguably better than Nintendo, so here’s hoping it can pull off the same in this multi-genre follow-up.

It's LLMs or machine learning in various forms, which aren't necessarily the same as artificial intelligence in the sense of conscious being. And Talos Principle…well, it's not really about AI, in the sense that we use artificial intelligence as a way to talk about materialism. So there is, obviously, a relevance to AI in the sense that if we manage to create an actually thinking being out of technology, that obviously has vast philosophical implications. And how can we use it to liberate ourselves or destroy ourselves according to various perspectives? So, there is a relevance there in general, of course, to you know any kind of labor-saving technology or technology that expands our horizons, but not specifically to what we are calling AI now in our world. Kind of a complicated answer.It's a complicated topic. There have been AI –  LLMs genAI – CEOs who claim we've hit the singularity, which to me, that seems absurd.
