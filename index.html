The Frustrating Mystery Of Who Makes Nintendo GamesNEW
Whenever a Nintendo game gets a splashy reveal in a Direct, my immediate hype takes on a few forms. I’ll replay the trailer at least once, then I’ll start going frame by frame. I scour Nintendo’s webs...
Whenever a Nintendo game gets a splashy reveal in a Direct, my immediate hype takes on a few forms. I’ll replay the trailer at least once, then I’ll start going frame by frame. I scour Nintendo’s websites for concrete information, then I read up on Reddit comments and theories to see what other people noticed that I might have missed. And then, I watch the trailer yet again. But almost every single time, my brain lands on the same question: Who the hell made this thing? The most recent catalyst for this vicious hype cycle was Metroid Ravenous, a game that looks remarkably like 2021’s Metroid Dread. That game was made in collaboration with Spanish studio MercurySteam, but I saw no mention of that company anywhere in Nintendo’s promotional materials immediately after the reveal. Being the inquisitive and nosy neurodivergent person that I am, my mind subsequently went down a rabbit hole, contemplating why Nintendo is so averse to talking about this. Nintendo’s devoted fanbase is many things, but one constant is that fans will never stop being curious. They are always seeking out specifics, no matter how trivial the subject, from the nuances of the Zelda timeline to whatever Mario’s current occupation is. With Nintendo often being a secretive company, the lack of information it provides only intensifies fans’ thirst for knowledge. And for whatever reason, Nintendo is particularly tight-lipped--arguably now more than ever--about which specific studios are behind its titles. After a cryptic post from MercurySteam, we probably know who's making the new Metroid. This has long been the case for Nintendo, but discussions over who is making what in Nintendo’s portfolio have increased over time. In the lead-up to the release of Princess Peach: Showtime in 2024, talk amongst enthusiasts about the game was dominated by speculation on who was developing it, rather than the content of the game itself. Nintendo was oddly evasive about the subject, saying that players needed to look at the game’s credits to find that out. (It turns out, as datamines indicated, it was frequent Nintendo partner Good-Feel.) The same happened months prior, when it took some sleuthing to figure out who made the Super Mario RPG remake (ArtePiazza, in that case). While this is purely speculative, I can take a guess as to why Nintendo takes this approach. When Nintendo reveals a new game, the company wants you to see it as a Nintendo game–not an Intelligent Systems title, or a Grezzo production, or a HAL Laboratory original. By framing it as such, Nintendo is presumably hoping that you do away with preconceptions relating to the developers’ previous games and make a judgment on the game once you actually play the final product. That practice has the potential to save some consumers from certain pitfalls; for instance, it could avoid pre-release discourse centering on the studio’s track record instead of the game itself. That type of dialogue could lead to people unfairly evaluating a game before it’s even out. I know I would be mortified if someone prematurely judged Next Level Games’ titles solely based on its work on the ill-fated Metroid Prime: Federation Force. It’s also a way for Nintendo to strengthen its brand. Being a Nintendo-published game comes with some level of shine and prestige, no matter who made it. When a Nintendo game succeeds, no matter if it was a partner studio or one of the company’s in-house and generically named EPD studios, you can owe it to Nintendo’s special touch. And when one fails, then Nintendo can own the mistake instead of the studio. But as the years have passed, Nintendo appears to be deemphasizing its subsidiary studios and its development partners, and there’s a magic that’s lost as a result. When you know Next Level Games is behind a new title, you can expect excellence in character animation. There’s a sense of joy I got from some of Nintendo’s GameCube-era titles, which featured the developers behind those games loudly and at the very start. Whether it was Waluigi yelling “CAMELOT!” in Mario Power Tennis or Daisy screeching “NEXT LEVEL GAMES!” before Super Mario Strikers, the pride and passion from these studios was palpable. I always enjoyed how the original three Metroid Prime games opened with “Nintendo and Retro Studios present” before the title screen, highlighting how essential the talent from Retro was to these games. Fast forward to Metroid Prime 4 in 2025, which shortens that vanity card to “Nintendo Presents,” as if it’s ashamed to acknowledge its Austin-based affiliate studio. I can’t even find the Retro Studios logo on the back of the box. At most, you’ll see studio names like Intelligent Systems or Monolith Soft in the copyright notice at the bottom of the title screen. And while you don’t want potential customers to be stuck with preconceptions based on who’s making a game, more information is always better for making informed decisions. I want to know if Good-Feel is making a new Yoshi game, because that studio excels in crafting unique art styles. I want to know if Next Level is making a new Luigi’s Mansion, because the folks there are particularly great at making expressive character animations. I want to know if MercurySteam is behind a new Metroid (which appears to be the case for Ravenous), because while that studio makes a damn good Metroid, it also caught flak for not crediting certain developers, received accusations of crunch and intimidation, and recently underwent layoffs. Nintendo is always going to do things its way–it wouldn’t be Nintendo if it weren’t. But the least it can do is give its fans a better idea of what they’re getting.

Someone playing Refractive Entertainment's recently released action-RPG Luminary for less than an hour accused the team of using AI for the game's only voiced character. The player said the voice "killed it" for them. There was just one problem: It wasn't AI. It was actually the developer's wife doing her first-ever voice acting gig. Refractive Entertainment launched Luminary into Steam Early Access on August 11. It's a resource-gathering action-adventure sandbox with an art style that's giving Fable meets Immortals Fenyx Rising. It's cute-looking and well-received, with a "Mostly Positive" rating out of almost 350 Steam reviews. But for an indie developer who says his game's success lives and dies by its Steam review score, one particular negative review caught the creative director's attention. On September 10, user NyteKnyte posted a short Steam review after only 0.4 hours with the game to say that Luminary looked good but "the awful AI-generated voice killed it" for them. "Feels off, like an AI game," NyteKnyte said in their review. "Hopefully its temporary and gets better, but [the AI voice] killed it for me." Not long after this review hit the game's Steam page, Refractive Entertainment responded, saying that the “awful AI-generated voice” that NyteKnyte decried was actually the creative director's wife. "While my wife might not be the greatest voice actress out there (that was her first time), I can assure you she is a real person and not AI haha," creative director Keith Kolod said. "We don't use AI at all in our game, we're just some indie game devs doing our thing with the resources we have." https://twitter.com/RefractiveENT/status/2098435648218910791 Kolod even shared the negative review on X on September 11 to wonder publicly why people just "automatically assume" that every voice in an indie game is AI just because it doesn't sound like AAA quality. He also thanked those who played Luminary and complimented his wife's portrayal of the Temple Guardian. In Discord DMs with GameSpot, Kolod said he doesn't have any issues with people using AI for any reason, especially in game development and for voiceovers. However, he doesn’t use the controversial technology because voiceovers are a way for him to "leave parts of me, my family, and friends" in the games he makes. "That said, Mikell did NOT jump to the opportunity," Kolod explained. "She is shy, felt like it would be bad, and didn't want to hurt the game. I told her this part doesn't need a lot of 'acting.' It's an ethereal goddess who should feel calming to the player as though she has delivered this speech to hundreds of Luminaries over centuries, and that I believe she could do it. The first few takes of recording you could tell she was extremely nervous, even though it's literally just me and her at my computer and a mic. [She] stumbled on words, worried about all of her inflections and how certain words sounded, but with enough takes and only reading a couple sentences at a time, I thought we got a pretty decent run. She was happy it was over." Kolod said the whole reason he built Luminary was so "me, my wife, and son [could] play together." As a Souls and Elden Ring fan, he wanted something that could introduce newer players to the action-RPG genre without the punishing difficulty of those games while creating something his whole family could enjoy together. Luminary is that for Refractive Entertainment. With no funding, no publisher, and no other job bringing in income, Luminary has a lot of expectations on its shoulders. For Mikell, lending her voice to the game wasn't about becoming a professional voice actor or saving money. She did it because she wanted to help her husband succeed. "This was my wife's first time voice acting, she wasn't a huge fan," Kolod told GameSpot. "You know how it is--everyone hates hearing their own voice in a recording who isn't a professional. As [much] fun as we had together doing it, it may be a hard sell for me to convince her to do it again. She really just helped me out of love and wanting to see me succeed with Luminary, and helping in any way she could." Despite the AI accusation, Luminary appears to be off to a promising start for the two-person team at Refractive Entertainment. Two weeks after its Steam Early Access launch, Kolod took to X on August 25  to share that the action-RPG had generated over $100,000 in sales in just one week, with the team pulling in approximately $70,000 in revenue after Steam’s cut. Not bad for a two-person studio with no funding and no publisher. The attention surrounding Mikell's voice has apparently brought even more eyes to the game, too. Kolod told GameSpot that posts about the story on Reddit and elsewhere have generated views, likes, upvotes, and more wishlists, though he said the team has yet to see that attention translate into a comparable bump in sales. And perhaps nobody was more surprised by the whole thing than Mikell herself. Before Kolod pushed the update to the game containing her voice, she had one request for her husband (who will celebrate 15 years together in November): “Ok, but I don't wanna hear if people hate my voice in reviews or on X. I did what I could.” “Little did she know it would turn into our biggest story,” Kolod said.

More Than Anything, Zach Cregger’s Resident Evil Movie Understands How It Feels To Play A Resident Evil GameNEW
In 2006, I eagerly attended the first showing of Christophe Gans’ Silent Hill. I loved Silent Hill 2, but was ready to mock the film, as video game adaptations to that point had been und...
In 2006, I eagerly attended the first showing of Christophe Gans’ Silent Hill. I loved Silent Hill 2, but was ready to mock the film, as video game adaptations to that point had been underwhelming, to say the least. In one scene, protagonist Rose checked her map, and I leaned over to my friend and said, “Now she just needs to check the map every few seconds to make it really feel like Silent Hill.” To my surprise, Rose proceeded to check the map on every block as she made it to her destination. The movie leading up to that scene and the rest following it is generally forgettable, but that moment made me pause and realize that Gans had in fact played Silent Hill, and was probably even a fan.Zach Cregger’s Resident Evil made me recall that moment from Silent Hill. A moment that expressed the feeling of playing Silent Hill more than just being a direct adaptation of the property. As promised by Cregger in multiple interviews promoting the film, Resident Evil contains very few overt references to the larger Resident Evil franchise. It primarily takes place in Raccoon City, the Umbrella Corporation is acknowledged, there is a typewriter, and protagonist Bryan uses Heavy Field brand shotgun shells. That’s about it. But the whole film expertly expresses the feeling of playing a Resident Evil game. The intense, terrifying highs, the cathartic moments of catching your breath, the frantic pace, the moments of failure as a player, the reward of simply figuring things out, and even building your skill over the course of the game finally paying off in the end.The movie follows Bryan on the worst night of his life. He is a normal guy (who doesn’t know how to use a gun) trying to make some money on a delivery job while managing his feelings during an emotionally tense event currently affecting his girlfriend and himself. And then he gets stuck in a Resident Evil game – and there is no Leon to whisk him away on a jet ski.From that point forward, Bryan reacts to everything not as a Resident Evil protagonist, but as a player of the games. He stares wide-eyed at unbelievable biological horrors; cannot stop comically swearing as he sprints full speed away from danger; screams at embarrassing pitches when monsters leap from the shadows; makes myriad mistakes; searches rooms for anything that could help him; knows he shouldn’t open that scary door even though he has to; is constantly wasting ammo; and during one of my favorite moments, accidentally gets rid of an item he absolutely needed. The whole debacle (which lasts a sublime 90 minutes) is intense, scary, fun, and most surprisingly, often hilarious.Director Zach Creggor and Austin Abrams, who plays Bryan, nail a very important element of Resident Evil that I believe no other Resident Evil movie has been able to figure out: you can’t take this too seriously. Resident Evil is absurd. The Resident Evil characters are the Leslie Nielsons of this Naked Gun horror universe, and we as the players are the ones that get to scream and laugh and have a good time. Bryan is our stand-in in Creggor’s adaptation, and I love it for that. I don’t need all video game adaptations to be like this. There is undeniable joy in seeing specific elements of a property you love getting the Hollywood investment and being projected on a big screen. I have no idea if it will be good or bad, but I would be lying if I said I wasn’t excited for the Zelda movie next year. But I love that the emotional experience of playing a Resident Evil game has been so expertly translated into a film here. It officially unseats my previous favorite video game film adaptation, which was, of course, Josh Ruben’s 2021 take on everyone’s favorite Ubisoft game, Werewolves Within.

Control Resonant – Review In ProgressNEW
Control Resonant’s review embargoes have lifted, but I’m still working through Dylan Faden’s sizable paranatural adventure. We’ll publish our scored review sometime next week, before the game...
Control Resonant’s review embargoes have lifted, but I’m still working through Dylan Faden’s sizable paranatural adventure. We’ll publish our scored review sometime next week, before the game’s launch on September 24, but in the meantime, here’s a quick summary of my thoughts so far.As a fan of Control, I’m having a lot of fun with Resonant. Despite its many differences in combat and world design, it feels the same in several ways. The game’s world and lore are as weird and fascinating as ever; if anything, moving the setting beyond the Oldest House has allowed Remedy to concoct even stranger scenarios now that the general populace is exposed to the full force of the paranatural. While I’m enjoying the main quest, some of my favorite moments in Control Resonant have occurred in the side stories. Many of these optional excursions tell fascinating tales that vary in humor, surrealism, and disturbance, and they sometimes flip the gameplay in unexpected ways, making me excited to experience each one I stumble upon. The game’s myriad recordings and other heavily redacted documents remain as much of a highlight to absorb as in the first Control. The melee action, which resembles games like Devil May Cry, feels good. Not quite to the level of a DMC or Bayonetta, as points of comparison, but good, even if it feels repetitive during the early hours due to your initially limited options. This improves dramatically as you unlock new forms for your transforming weapon, the Aberrant, and new supernatural powers. Mixing and matching main and side weapons, combo finishers, and superpowers becomes a deeper, more strategic treat that caters to multiple playstyles. The game is more difficult than I expected; enemies’ levels often keep pace with you and swarm in large, sometimes wave-based mobs. But I appreciate how the difficulty pushes me to rely on my ever-growing arsenal of powers, whether it’s igniting foes by unleashing explosion-inducing punches or force-pushing them off ledges and into hazards. I suspect, however, that the difficulty will catch many off guard, as some main missions will seem like wars of attrition if you don’t invest some time into completing other primary objectives and the different tiers of sidequests. Thankfully, those are worth checking out anyway. The distorted Manhattan is a surreal visual treat. Beyond the predominantly kaleidoscopic sights that make the city resemble the Mirror Dimension from Doctor Strange, other, even wilder locations keep the game aesthetically interesting. Combined with the fun side quests, this makes Manhattan fun to explore over a dozen hours into the adventure. If you miss the Metroid-esque process of unlocking areas of the Oldest House, take some solace in the fact that Manhattan, despite being a large hub, also feels like a bigger version of Control’s main setting (which you do visit in Resonant). Numerous areas are gated by abilities or keys, and other, typically optional locations require careful searching to find their entrances. Though it can be frustrating when the path forward isn’t as obvious as it seems, entertaining traversal powers gradually make getting around easier and more quickly.At the time of writing, I seemingly have a substantial chunk left before rolling credits on Control Resonant, but I’m largely positive on the sequel at this juncture. I always look forward to jumping back into it; the combat and world continue to become weirder and more interesting to engage with; I have to regularly curb the desire to uncover every hidden mission, area, or secret to get the review done in a timely manner (so much for that, obviously). I’m also enjoying the fun banter and dynamic of the genuinely likable Dylan and his sassy FBC agent partner/handler Zoe (not to mention hanging with returning faces like Pope and Langston). If the remainder of the adventure continues this positive momentum, Dylan’s quest to save the world will rank right up there with his sister Jesse’s.
