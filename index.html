There's no denying that Afterworld is a traditional grand strategy game in many ways--big foggy maps, territorial clashes, macro and micro decision-making--but its end-of-the-world scenario presents an opportunity for Paradox Studios to elevate the genre further still. Even in the brief glimpse we had during a hands-on play session at Gamescom, a number of differences are already showing.  For a start, Afterworld has a very clear focus on lore-building, which is something the more historical games often take for granted. If you're playing as a Roman General or a post-industrial capitalist, the player tends to know what happened before that point, as a rough approximation of historical events can be summed up in an info screen before moving on. Afterworld is very clearly the outlier: Taking place some 100 years after a mysterious fictional apocalyptic event known only as The Fall, there's a lot to unpack.  When you start the game, you're given a vague choice of how you'd like to conduct yourself during the early hours of the campaign. For the demo, we were the de facto leader of a band of newly emerged vault citizens (where have we heard that before?) and our reactor was about to explode. A ranger was quickly sent into the fog beyond, and our people moved a safe distance away from what was about to be a very smoky hole in the ground. There are other beginnings to choose from, but the game offers seemingly endless chances to change who you are as you live through its various terrors and triumphs. The beginning is not as important as what you choose to do next.  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9PU1e7kfkfs Instead of traditional nations, you'll have bands, tribes of people with established belief systems and leaders--some friends, some foes, some undecided and purely mercenary. You'll come head-to-head with them fairly quickly, and if they don't get the jump on you, you might be able to integrate them into your society by spreading your values, finding shared beliefs, offering home comforts and political representation, or forcing them to kneel to your might. As well as managing resources and territory, you'll be managing your band's morality and ethical code. The depth to which this is done dawns on you the more you play. Those decisions you made during the starting slides can quickly be overridden as new ideas emerge.  Instead of a traditional technology tree, you'll play with concepts in the Idea Tree, which builds knowledge through specific clusters that are unlocked through exploration. Your tribe begins to build its story almost immediately, and if you lean into a particular side of the Idea Tree, you might become a pacifistic band of animal herders, a brutal gang of feral cannibals, or a cult looking to follow the path of enlightenment at any cost. You can embrace or reject old world ideas, as well as making tough choices to survive that could compromise your ability to lead. A cult of personality only works when everyone buys into it, and if people start starving, dying off, or being ignored, you'll have a revolt on your hands.  Your people will also probably, by necessity, be more nomadic than in most grand strategy games. There is a mid-to-late game goal of conquest if you want it, but the first objective of the game is simply to survive. You'll not always emerge somewhere hospitable--procedurally generated disasters will dictate what you find. Your next area could be irradiated, picked dry by scavengers, or filled with warring tribes. You'll need to gather food and resources, but setting down a permanent home might take some time.  Some of the most powerful beings in the game are the Ancients: warlords and religious icons who wield immense power and can be appeased and worshipped or angered and made hostile. You can also curry their favor and organize a coup if you're confident you have the support. Unlike other grand strategy games where you can choose powerful established nation states to put your weight behind, with Afterworld you will always start as the underdog. You're not the protagonist of any story, just someone who also has to survive and do what's best for your people. The developers have been very clear that the world moves on with or without you.  Afterworld When you join the world in this state, you discover the mechanics of the game at the same pace you discover the in-game world. A tentative exploration could lead to resources, ideas, or tech. It could also land you in trouble. The consequences of The Fall are far reaching, leaving fragments of story scattered around the map for you to find. Ruined cities can be repatriated and old weapons remade, the technology of the ancient world in-game is still the stuff of science fiction in real life.  Victory conditions are also more survival-adjacent than anything we've seen in most grand strategy games. Afterworld is perhaps more comparable to Frostpunk than anything Paradox has done before. You could, technically, spend all your time and resources repairing the bunker so you can slide back in, shut the door, and live out your short lives in blissful ignorance. You could build your troops and religious fervor and take on one of the Ancients, ruling in their stead, you could build advanced tech and escape to the stars--whatever your idea of survival is, Afterworld says you can make it a reality. It's been a frustrating few days as someone who saw Nvidia's DLSS 5 demonstration at Gamescom last week. Since a recent mod leaked that showed DLSS 5 adjusting some characters in a pretty confusing manner, I've been under embargo and unable to discuss the true functionality, or intent, of the AI-powered filter. First teased back in March 2026, the feature received a largely negative reception due to the stark differences in character models, with some claiming they were "unrecognizable." To address the elephant in the room--yes, some of the DLSS 5-infused creations are horrific. Final Fantasy's Cloud looks like something straight out of a horror game, while adding multiple layers of DLSS 5 has made characters from The Legend of Heroes series look utterly harrowing. But from my time with Nvidia at Gamescom, I'm trying to be realistic about the use and power of the feature. Different models will produce different outputs. "DLSS 5 is intended to be used for photorealistic situations and characters," Edward Liu, Nvidia’s director of applied deep learning research said at the demo. That means no Final Fantasy, no Kingdom Hearts, and nothing that would stylistically suffer as a result of implementing it. What's more, developers can opt in or out of using the AI-powered tool, and there's more customization than just "on" or "off." Developers can create masks and adjust the filter's intensity. For example, developers can create masks in their games to ensure DLSS 5 is only applied to those areas. If you just want to enhance the appearance of food in a game, that's possible. If you're just looking to make the foliage and plants look better, that's okay too. The intensity of the filter is completely customizable by the developer. Perhaps more importantly, it can be disabled entirely as a gamer--there'll be a simple toggle in the menu to turn it on or off. DLSS 5 uses several layers to preserve artistic intent. It's trained to recognize the surface albedo, lighting semantics, and surface normal. In simpler terms, it looks for the original measure of how much sunlight hits the surface, the way the lighting is designed, and the direction the surfaces are facing to determine the way DLSS 5 is applied and ensures it's done in a way that does not change those three original layers. DLSS 5 will not affect the original artistic intent. It's important to remember that DLSS 5 is something the developer opts into. As such, it's highly unlikely any of them will be deliberately implementing a tool that creates an eldritch horror instead of making their characters, objects, or environments look better. The first game to officially launch with DLSS 5 capability will be NBA 2K27, where photorealism is obviously the intent. As such, character models appear more detailed, and even vast numbers of NPCs in the crowd can easily have DLSS 5 applied to improve their appearance. There have also been reported concerns around performance. Nvidia states that, when using its 50-series GPUs, DLSS 5 has a minimal impact on performance. When running NBA 2K27 on the Ultra settings at 1080p using 6x Multi Frame Gen, a 9800X3D, and 64GB of RAM, the 5090 runs at an average of 797 frames per second. The 5080 follows up at 602, with the 5070 Ti at 530 and 5070 at 386. The 5060 Ti is at 325 and the 5060 is 258. Nvidia claims performance is not a concern. Of course, with the average person's setup not quite rivaling that of Nvidia's, there will be variations to that. However, we were shown various builds running NBA 2K27 on different settings, from high-end to mid-range, with next to no performance issues, though we haven't yet been able to independently verify those figures. For me personally, whether I choose to toggle DLSS 5 on or off will depend on the developer's use of the function. If egregious filtering is used to the point where I no longer recognize the characters, or I find myself distracted by the filtering, I'll turn it off. But if Nvidia's demonstration is accurate to the level of customization it provides developers and preserves artistic intent, I don't see any reason I won't be using it.

Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Frogwares Developer: Frogwares Release: August 18, 2026 (PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC), 2027 (Switch 2) The Sinking City 2 excels at delivering a captivating and eerie atmosphere, artistically alluring visuals, and jump-inducing sound design. However, much like the shambling reanimated corpses players confront, it tends to stumble as it walks the same well-paved road laid by Resident Evil 4. This long-awaited sequel aligns with its predecessor’s reception; a respectable scare fest with entertaining puzzle-solving, but middling combat, flawed progression, and an underwhelming narrative hold it back from reaching the horrific heights of its more celebrated contemporaries.Despite the number 2 in its title, this follow-up to 2019’s The Sinking City is a standalone story starring a new protagonist (though he is aesthetically identical to former lead, Charles Reed), making it a newcomer-friendly adventure. Calvin Rafferty is an adventurer trying to revive Faye, his lover and a university professor, who has fallen into a supernatural sleep. Early events separate Faye's soul from her physical form, allowing her to accompany Calvin literally in spirit as the duo tag-teams the horrors lurking in the flooded city of Arkham to retrieve her stolen body from the clutches of a Lovecraftian cosmic horror. Despite solid performances for both leads, their characters never develop much beyond the fact that they really love each other (as Calvin constantly reminds the player), making Calvin himself an uninteresting lead beyond his love life. The intriguing supporting cast is similarly underdeveloped; I hoped to spend more time getting to know an unsettling doctor with worms for eyes or a sassy fish-woman who has shackled herself to her office for everyone else’s sake (her ominous words). This lack of substantial character development makes the overall plot, while a decent mystery, tough to fully invest in. The story also feels rushed, with its final act abruptly arriving sooner than the story’s methodical pacing led me to expect.The Sinking City 2’s flooded Arkham boasts a killer atmosphere and art direction that makes it tough to look away from even its most grotesque sights. When I wasn’t admiring the sights while cruising Arkham’s flooded streets aboard a motorboat or trudging through my two favorite locations, a hospital and a fish market, I blasted enemies in over-the-shoulder gunplay that reads from Resident Evil’s familiar playbook. Although combat is competent, firing weapons doesn’t pack as much punch as I’d like, even with powerful guns like the shotgun and rifle. The same is true of exploding gas canisters, which don’t feel satisfying to ignite due to the disappointing explosion effect and blast radius. Despite bolstering weapon effectiveness by equipping collectible attachments, the damage upgrades feel negligible as enemies are annoyingly bullet-spongy, even when you target their bulbous, glowing weak points. Tetris-esque inventory management, a regularly updating map, and resource crafting are all present and well-replicated, but one element I wish The Sinking City 2 borrowed from Resident Evil is the ability to perform a 180-degree quick-turn. Calvin moves comparatively slower and more deliberately than his sometimes quicker, at times nimble adversaries who have a habit of striking from behind, occasionally to a cheap extent. The Sinking City 2 often surrounds players with multiple enemies, at times in confined spaces, and between dodging foes who spit deadly goo with the accuracy of a Tom Brady pass and annoying leaping, spider-like critters, encounters are often a fight for breathing room since you spend so much time manually turning around to keep targets in your sights since you’re getting flanked so often. Arkham may be flooded, but the enemy pool is disappointingly shallow; the small handful of creature types means combat encounters become overly familiar by the halfway point of this roughly 13-hour journey.Thankfully, the locations and level design do a better job of switching things up. You’ll go from piloting a speedboat to derelict locations in the submerged open city hub to sinking your teeth into unraveling the labyrinthine hospital and fish market. The latter locations best scratched my survival-horror itch as I engaged in the well-worn but still entertaining exercise of finding keys to unlock doors, trinkets to solve puzzles, and clues to stitch together solutions on an investigation board. Puzzle-solving as a whole is more consistently enjoyable thanks to its balanced-yet-stimulating challenge and variety, and I was always excited to encounter a new riddle to unravel. I also enjoyed reading the fascinating lore notes and listening to a radio DJ share bizarre Twilight Zone-esque tales of this world. Solving puzzles, among other feats, rewards Dream Essence, but the progression system is bafflingly steep. You can either spend a couple of these points to earn a single perk such as damage reduction or faster reloading, or spend 10 points to unlock one of three slots to hold said upgrades. I opted to invest in the latter, assuming the large number of locked perks signaled enough Dream Essence is available to unlock a reasonably diverse pool of perks to fit into my three slots. Despite solving most of the optional mysteries and finding many special chests rewarding Dream Essence (among other upgrades like increased inventory slots or weapon attachments), I was barely able to unlock the six perks in the first tier (out of four). This strange scarcity of Dream Essence means you have a choice between unlocking many perks that can only be equipped one at a time or equipping three perks from a minuscule pool. This imbalanced progression severely limits crafting varied builds, something The Sinking City 2 seemingly encourages. Ultimately, however, the perks don’t make much of a difference. I was still able to finish The Sinking City 2 on the standard difficulty without feeling underpowered.The Sinking City 2 offers a decent take on a familiar formula if you’re itching for survival horror. But with so many better options, it’s hard to recommend it over similar titles that nail the style more consistently and evenly. Though I did find some fun within the madness, its shortcomings keep its potential from fully breaking the surface.  Score: 6.25 About Game Informer's review system

If you don't opt in to that mode, however, a standard video game waypoint will help you get where you need to go. "You can never play the game wrong – that's our core tenet," Berg says. You get XP for reaching the informant's room, regardless of how you do it. "We always reward the 'what,' but never the 'how,'" Berg says. "If I gave you XP for lockpicking [which we do to enter the room with the password], you'll start lockpicking everywhere. The Blood of Dawnwalker Review - Time Doesn't Heal All WoundsNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Bandai Namco Developer: Rebel Wolves Release: September 3, 2026 Rating: Mature In an age of overconsumption, The Blood of Dawnwalker shows the potential of limitation. You're given 30 days to rescue your family from the fangs of a vampiric court. Growing stronger for the final fight, either by improving your skills or helping others to forge alliances, ticks the clock onwards. As uneven as this mechanic (and others) can be, it brings a breath of fresh air to an often excessive genre.After an arresting prologue, the open-world RPG by Rebel Wolves gives you free rein to tame it however you see fit. The studio was formed by CD Projekt Red veterans, which shows; almost every element carries the fingerprint of The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt. If you've played Geralt of Rivia's epic, the adventure of Coen of Laslea will feel overly familiar. The dialogue structure, the tone of the quests, conceptually novel combat that falls short in practice, and how you plunge into the myriad question mark icons on the map all make it impossible to avoid comparisons.Thankfully, as much as the studio draws from the acclaimed formula previously established by its founders, it does make attempts to subvert it. The time mechanic is a big example. Rather than having an always-ticking clock, time is dictated by your actions. With Coen being a Dawnwalker – a human by day and vampire by night – you have access to both day and night slots. Digging a treasure buried underground can take one slot. Playing tag with your siblings or looking for missing scouts can take three slots. The promise of this mechanic at first feels limitless, as you must carefully consider how you approach each cycle and decide what is actually worth your time. At first, it's quite effective in conditioning your choices. During an early optional mission, I tracked down a grave robber who had desecrated a young man's tomb in order to steal his silver necklace to make arrows, an effective weapon against vampires in this world. Sadly, I ran out of time before I could return it to the grieving mother. Later finding I still had the necklace in my inventory is surprisingly heartbreaking for what was technically a simple fetch quest.Confronting the main vampire in The Blood of Dawnwalker is a tantalizing endeavor, as the quest is available from the jump. You can literally exit the prologue and attempt to fight him. This would prove absurdly difficult, which is why you have 30 in-game days to grow your character and find people who are willing to lend you a hand in the final showdown. You can also hunt down the other three members of the vampire court to further turn the tide in your favor. However, the time pressure progressively eroded the closer I got to the end of the 30-day period. For reference, I was warned about the point of no return if I accepted a character's help with 13 days to spare.Up until then, I was being overly cautious about how I spent my time. Obtaining skills and their respective upgrades requires points, but the action itself also uses time slots. Doing so made Coen stronger, but it was time that I could have spent aiding more people. The moment I saw the point of no return sign, the pressure wore off entirely. It doesn't help that I unlocked three additional routes to tackle it shortly after the first, as if the story was urging me to put an end to it.Coen's own vampirism is similarly frictionless. Its concept has strong potential, as magic can only be used during the day, with the entire skill set replaced by vampiric abilities at night. Some activities and quests are specific to each cycle, and you get to mix-and-match two armor builds, as equipment often has bonuses for each Coen persona.Of course, once the sun sets, you also need to satiate your blood craving by feeding from animals or humans. Fail to do so, and you run the risk of giving into hunger during conversations, potentially killing important characters. The prospect of killing outside of your control is terrifying, yet one that I never saw in action, as blood is tied to your health. I never ran low on healing items, and while you can feed during combat, there was never any shortage of targets to feed from outside of it either. The standout features of The Blood of Dawnwalker never quite come together. The combat backbone also falters. It starts off with a similar promise, with blocking requiring you to follow directional cues to effectively riposte attacks. Yet, enemies tend to repeat movesets. Aside from the main boss fights, confrontations are repetitive Simon Says sequences. Nonetheless, I was enthralled by the gritty world on display, especially when learning more about the corrosive nature of the vampire court on the townsfolk. They are not only killing hundreds, but also corrupting aspects like faith and turning themselves into religious iconography in churches and running baptisms with blood instead of holy water.Despite my mechanical gripes, I remained invested in Coen’s story to the end. The final confrontation encapsulated the many decisions and paths I took and had me face their ramifications one after the other. I may have been unfazed by the time mechanic’s pressure, but the limitation did shape my adventure. The Blood of Dawnwalker shows that curating a massive RPG rather than pursuing every single quest holds immense potential. The ending made me realize that having all the time in the world would have prevented inner ambitions to finally come to broad daylight.  Score: 7 About Game Informer's review system
