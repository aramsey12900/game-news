You Have Some Homework To Do To Make The Most Of Star Wars Zero CompanyNEW
Star Wars Zero Company takes place right at the end of The Clone Wars, my favorite period in Star Wars' huge timeline. That conflict begins with the Battle of Geonosis in Attack of the Clones and conc...
Star Wars Zero Company takes place right at the end of The Clone Wars, my favorite period in Star Wars' huge timeline. That conflict begins with the Battle of Geonosis in Attack of the Clones and concludes with the deaths of the Separatist leaders in Revenge of the Sith, but a vast majority of the galaxy-spanning war is covered in the seven seasons of Star Wars: The Clone Wars. As such, Zero Company makes several references to the earlier seasons of the TV show that debuted in 2008. The biggest one, of what I've seen of the game so far anyway, comes right at the start of the story: Protagonist Hawks was made the scapegoat for a battle that went very wrong while working under General Pong Krell. And, man, that name does a tremendous amount of narrative heavy lifting in characterizing Hawks. With one name, Bit Reactor basically gave me all the proof I needed that Hawks had not only seen some truly horrendous war crimes, but that whatever they were accused of was almost certainly not true.  "The intended effect of the line worked, then," Bit Reactor narrative director Aaron Contreras told me. "Like, [Republic Intelligence] is talking about whether they can trust this guy, because he's a washout and a loser--he worked for Pong Krell. And so every [player] who knows this guy is like, 'It's not [Hawks'] fault then. That's the worst boss ever."  So even if you don't need to know Pong Krell to play Zero Company, I highly recommend you familiarize yourself with him beforehand. And the best way to do that is by watching The Clone Wars. Or, at the very least, one specific arc: Season 4's four-episode Umbara Arc. The Clone Wars is always a tough show for me to recommend because it has some absolutely stellar episodes of television hidden in between mind-numbing episodes that are too silly or simply excruciatingly dull. I can only see Jar Jar Binks on my screen for so many minutes before I contemplate punching my TV. But the show's gems are really, really good, largely for how they toe the line for what's typically acceptable for a children's show and include storylines that respect that kids can handle dark material in what is a show all about war, child soldiers, and the fall of a democracy. I'm still floored by some of the stuff that the showrunners were able to include, especially as the show departed from its sillier tone more often post-Season 2. For example, Season 5, Episodes 17-20 is the Temple Bombing Arc, in which the Jedi Temple is bombed in a terrorist attack, and in a storyline that feels super 9/11-coded, both the government and the Jedi Council leap to assumptions, suspect and hastily imprison innocent people, and betray the trust of allies all in the name of catching the bomber. And then there's Season 7, Episodes 9-12: the Siege of Mandalore Arc. That sees Ahsoka lead an army of Clones to free Mandalore from the control of Maul in a storyline that occurs simultaneously with Revenge of the Sith, with scenes from the movie slotting into events of the show for a tense war story where you're just waiting for the bombshell of Order 66 to drop. There are a ton of other fantastic arcs besides these ones: the Second Invasion of Geonosis, Mortis, Asajj Ventress trying to assassinate Count Dooku, Maul taking over the criminal underworld, Fives trying to unravel the Clones Conspiracy, Obi-Wan Kenobi pulling a Face/Off to go undercover as a criminal bounty hunter, Anakin dealing with his PTSD regarding slavery while on an extremely pro-slavery planet. But none come close to the Umbara Arc, which I consider to be the best four episodes in the entire show. The arc sees Anakin Skywalker needing to help Chancellor Palpatine on a special assignment and leaving his legion of Clone Troopers under the command of Pong Krell, a Jedi with a reputation for success at the cost of the troops under his command. We learn that no other Jedi has lost as many Clone Troopers as he has, and as soon as Anakin leaves, Krell immediately starts treating the clones as subhuman. What follows is an arc in which the clones argue amongst themselves over how to deal with Pong Krell. Do they continue acting loyally to him like good soldiers do? His disregard for their lives and treatment of them as dispensable chess pieces makes it hard to follow him, but he does get positive results and achieves victories through his reckless tactics, like a crueler and less passionate Anakin Skywalker. Would it be better to act in the spirit of his orders and achieve his goals but not do so exactly as he says? That could work but also carries some risk because he threatened to kill a clone just for daring to express an opinion, so any clone would need to bend the rules secretly to follow that plan. Do they rebel against Krell entirely? That's an act of mutiny that might lead to all of them being court martialed upon Anakin's return.  The arc goes into how, under the laws of the republic that they serve, the clones aren't people. They're property. The only reason some of them have opinions at all is because most Jedi encouraged them to embrace their unique personalities and express themselves. Some of the clones argue that Krell isn't evil; he's just treating the clones as they're supposed to be treated. The other Jedi are the weird ones, they say. Others argue it doesn't matter--they know that they're people deserving of respect now thanks to the Jedi's influence, and you can't just turn that line of thinking off so easily. Every clone has a different opinion on the matter, fracturing the legion in the middle of an invasion that sees them fighting on a planet blanketed in eternal darkness and endless forests, where the locals use guerrilla tactics to resist these republic invaders, chipping away at the clones' numbers and their sanity.  Then Krell does something really bad. It's one of those moments in the show that makes me go, "This cannot be a show for kids anymore." It's not overly violent. It's just disturbing to realize what's happening, and then the show lingers for a few extra seconds on the horror of the aftermath so you can really come to terms with Krell's cruelty. So when Zero Company starts off by telling me that Hawks is a disgraced ex-Republic soldier who once served under Pong Krell, all I can think about is The Clones Wars' Umbara Arc and what Krell did during it. And it completely reframes my understanding of Hawks' past as a supposed failure. I don't think Hawks is using Zero Company to try to make up for a past mistake because I don't think there's a mistake for them to make up for--they were most likely framed. Which is why, if you've got the time and a Disney+ subscription, you should check out The Clone Wars (specifically Season 4, Episodes 7-10) before picking up Star Wars Zero Company. That arc does a lot to inform what might have happened to Hawks prior to the events of the game and makes them a far more interesting protagonist than just another generic disgraced war hero. And you might as well watch the rest of the show too if you have the time, as well as its spin-offs (The Bad Batch, Tales of the Jedi, and Maul - Shadow Lord) because it sounds like Zero Company will be making plenty of references to the Star Wars universe at large. "My writers are real [into] the Clone Wars era," Contreras said. "I'm an old guy, so I'm sort of like a Luke Skywalker, Han Solo, Princess Leia fan. I love the Clone Wars as well, but they're really into it in an unhealthy way, so if you like a deep dive, [the lore] is there. I think as far as the spread, I believe almost all of our locations existed already in lore. Some of them you may be seeing for the first time [fully] realized, but like we'll grab a name." Zero Company's first mission occurs on Serolonis, for example, a planet first introduced into Star Wars in The Bad Batch. A later mission takes Hawks and their squad to Courtsilius, a planet in the Hosnian system. That's a place we've never seen before, save for at a great distance as it was being destroyed in The Force Awakens. And, as the gameplay reveal trailer for Zero Company already pointed out, Anakin Skywalker (who is likely mere months, if not weeks, away from turning into Darth Vader) will also be in the game. Star Wars Zero Company is scheduled to launch for PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X|S, and PC on August 27.

Exoprimal was built using Capcom’s proprietary RE Engine, which is surprising considering the other Capcom games that have used the engine are quite different. The first game to publicly use it was Resident Evil 7: Biohazard, which caused many to dub the engine the Resident Evil engine, which is not what the ‘RE’ stands for. Instead, the acronym is confusingly shorthand for Reach for the Moon. In Japan, the Resident Evil series is called Biohazard, which is why Exoprimal technical director Kazuki Abe is surprised that anyone ever thought the engine was named after the survivor horror series . “It’s really just a matter of coincidence that Resident Evil games are also developed using this engine.” Exoprimal Since Resident Evil 7, RE Engine has been used to develop Resident Evil Village, the Resident Evil 2, 3, and 4 remakes, Devil May Cry 5, Ghosts’ n Goblins Resurrection, Monster Hunter Rise, Capcom Arcade 2nd Stadium, Street Fighter 6, and the multiplayer Resident Evil Re:Verse. It is also the engine used to develop Pragmata, Dragon’s Dogma II, and Onimusha: Way of the Sword. Ghosts 'n Goblins Resurrection Its versatility and flexibility are impressive, thanks to the RE Engine team being in-house at Capcom. It means that if a Capcom game needs the engine to do something specific for the game being made, the team can go directly to the source and have it added. For example, the Onimusha team needed a Dynamic Dismemberment System, so they put in a request to the internal engineers at Capcom. Rather than putting a game in an engine, the RE Engine can pivot and mold around the game based on needs. “If certain components for the game don’t exist in our engine already, the RE Engine team can work to implement those features to allow those things to become possible,” Abe says. Super Mario Sunshine Charles Harte It goes without saying that Mario games are some of the most beloved in the entire hobby, so it's always been surprising to learn just how difficult some of them have been to acquire legitimately. Super Mario Sunshine, which got added to the Nintendo Switch Online library this week, is probably the best example. While it was first released on GameCube in 2002, it wasn't ported to any other console for nearly two decades, making copies difficult to find. In 2020, Nintendo finally released the Super Mario 3D All-Stars collection for the Switch, including Super Mario 64, Super Mario Sunshine, and Super Mario Galaxy, but for reasons none of us can quite understand, the game was only available to buy for roughly six months, from September 2020 to March 2021. Madden NFL 27 Review - Third And LongNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Electronic Arts Developer: EA Orlando Release: August 13, 2026 Rating: Everyone This year, the Madden franchise is hunting down the field for a quick strike to jumpstart itself and excite fans. In recent iterations, progress has come in fits and starts, so this year's game is taking some big swings in its Franchise and Ultimate Team modes. Unfortunately, despite having all the tools on paper, not all of the game’s attempts pay off. Gameplay remains a strong component of the series, delivering a satisfying feeling on the sticks in the running game and animations that often look realistic. This is bolstered by the addition of the optional catch meter from College Football. It – along with more interplay between receivers and defensive backs – creates some satisfying moments when you get the timing correct and haul in or break up a pass. CPU defenses can be nice and aggressive, but hopefully play-action passes are soon tweaked, because right now your QB gets blasted too often.The big addition to Franchise mode is the Persona Engine, which tries to add some personality to how CPU players, and thus their teams, act. This is paired with increased options that produce a more involved back-and-forth between teams and players for contracts and free agency. However, players’ DNA tags change as you progress through the negotiation rounds. One second the total salary amount is “critical” to them, the next it’s not; it feels like you’re getting the runaround. Each round, you have five different pitches you can deliver, but they are deployed in a similar pattern, so it can be frustrating when you want to bump up their bonus, for example, but it’s arbitrarily not an option in this particular round.The worst thing is that the mode’s underlying logic is still confusing. CPU teams can’t manage their salary cap or draft properly, and simmed stats get wonky. The new Free Agent Frenzy fizzles out quickly as the process feels like a black box where players inexplicably sign with other teams no matter what contract you offer.Even with more player-centric events to react to and news flashes to keep you up on what’s happening in the wider league, the mode still doesn’t capture the drama of the NFL. That the most exciting thing that happened during my multiple Franchise runthroughs was I talked a player out of retirement is a testament to how uneventful the mode often is. You can see that the mode can be fun and is taking steps in the right direction with this year's changes, but it's also clear it isn't delivering on its potential, which is a shame. The career-focused Superstar mode does not have as many new features. You can now play as a tight end, safety, and edge rusher, but the experience is still best as a QB or running back. There’s also a battle pass, which is optional, but adds frustrating monetization and multiple in-game currencies to a completely offline mode in a full-priced title.As your standing with your team balloons, so too does your influence; I enjoyed being able to eventually influence who my team signed in free agency. But what’s really needed is the feeling that you’re living the life of an NFL player. Even the ups and downs of playing your position isn’t fully captured because objectives don’t evolve as your career progresses. This means any sense of storytelling stops as soon as you complete that game’s objectives.Ultimate Team arguably contains the largest change for this year’s Madden. Similar to EA FC, you can improve players by purchasing Evolutions. You can also choose your own captains to upgrade, and certain players get better when you complete their objectives. The rub is that which attributes are actually upgraded when you spend a skill point is randomized within specific buckets. You can re-roll upgrades at the cost of the free, in-game UT Coin currency, but this will act as a Coin sink as players inevitably chase upgrades for the most desired attributes like speed. While this year’s additions undoubtedly increase your ability to customize your roster and should make the cards you choose more useful compared to the progression curve for longer, it will also further the grind and meta chase.Adding features to a game is about more than simply making it different from last year; it should be about tweaking and improving upon previous years’ entries with the aim toward creating an unmissable experience. In Madden NFL 27, that can happen on the field itself via gameplay, but when it comes to being the coach/GM of a team or its superstar, it doesn’t fully deliver.  Score: 6.75 About Game Informer's review system

Capcom’s samurai action series, Onimusha, has been around for more than two decades… kinda. The first game, Onimusha: Warlords, arrived in 2001, and within the next five years, three more mainline games were released. The last one launched in 2006, and it’s been 20 years since without a sequel. Onimusha: Way of the Sword is set to change that when it launches next month, and ahead of that launch, it is the latest game to grace the cover of Game Informer (and subscribers can read the cover story right here). I traveled to Osaka, Japan, to visit Capcom’s HQ, play never-before-seen parts of the game for hours, and interview the team making it. As a fan of the series, it’s clear to me that the “dark-fantasy” through line in the first four Onimusha games continues in Way of the Sword. That was particularly showcased in one section of the game I played through, which took protagonist Miyamoto Musashi into the underground lab of an evil Genma named Dokyo. There, the game’s dark-fantasy feel crossed over into full-on horror. Despite a couple of hours of gameplay that resembles a horror game, Capcom says Way of the Sword is not a horror game.  This Dokyo, highlighted in the Game Informer video above, is a particularly twisted Genma. She kidnaps people – including children – and experiments on them to create new Genma by fusing them with animals. In one scene, she hoists a man fused with a large pig into the air before disposing of him in bloody fashion. She can’t do this in the broad daylight of Way of the Sword’s early Edo-period Kyoto, so in typical Capcom fashion, the team created an underground lab befitting the demon. “There was not really an intention to make a horror game [...] but with trying to build the character of Dokyo, in particular, we wanted to really showcase her twisted nature and that unsettling side of her and combine it with the strange and eerie occult side of Kyoto myths,” lead writer Hiroshi Yamashita tells me, explaining why Dokyo’s lab feels like a horror level within an otherwise non-horror game. He adds that the scares of this lab – which, mind you, does feature jump scares that got me once or twice – are not indicative of the full game but rather just this specific stage built around Dokyo’s more gruesome nature. Way of the Sword director Satoru Nihei describes this stage as one of the more horrific in the game's dark-fantasy action experience, likening it to a “classic Japanese ghost house.” Somewhat familiar with this subgenre of Japanese horror, it’s clear in what ways: shadows on the lab’s shoji highlight horrific body mutations and animal experiments gone wrong, and the halls are dripping with blood that shimmers in the soft glow of nearby torches. Once-beautiful kimonos hang from the rafters above, and it’s evident they used to belong to the subjects screaming in agony throughout this stage. Even Dokyo herself is designed in a horror-infused way, with elongated appendages, a shrill and petulant personality, and the way she speaks as a mastermind of the maze that is her underground lab (which, during Way of the Sword, you are clearly trapped in).  Nihei says this specific stage heightens the anxiety and tension Musashi and Shizuka, the mysterious and ethereal woman trapped in his Oni Guantlet, are feeling, which is why the team “thought this [horror-inspired stage] was a good way to explore those feelings and contrary states in the setting.” There’s also contrast in Dokyo herself, who is quite demonic and inhuman in appearance, but speaks in English/Japanese and dons a beautiful kimono as if she’s a woman. I came away from my time playing Way of the Sword most enamored with Dokyo – she’s unsettling and twisted, but very compelling and absolutely a scene-stealer in the game. I look forward to seeing more of the horror her stage promises, as well as the other parts of the game where dark fantasy morphs into something closer to Capcom’s Resident Evil series.
