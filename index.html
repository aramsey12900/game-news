How 40 Years of Metroid Have Impacted The Biggest Metroidvania DevelopersNEW
Metroid’s impact on the industry is widespread enough to have a whole genre named after it. It’s a privilege it splits with Castlevania, but it’s an honor all the same: a rarity shared by the likes of...
Metroid’s impact on the industry is widespread enough to have a whole genre named after it. It’s a privilege it splits with Castlevania, but it’s an honor all the same: a rarity shared by the likes of Rogue and Demon’s Souls. This seminal 1986 NES title helped spawn an entire style of game and influenced many creators across the industry. GameSpot spoke to various developers behind a number of modern metroidvanias to get their input on the Metroid series and how it has impacted the games they have made. These interviews have been edited for length and clarity. Rémi Boutin, senior game designer on Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown I discovered Metroid through Super Metroid, and apart from the alien atmosphere, the amazing Samus Aran, and the unforgettable intro, what immediately struck me was its interconnected world. At the time, I was playing a lot of level-based games, and having this huge, hostile maze to explore, full of mysteries and secrets to uncover, was incredible. It conveyed a thrill of adventure that I had rarely experienced before, except perhaps with Zelda. For me, this is the heart of the Metroid experience: exploring a world, then making it your own. Samus becomes stronger through upgrades, but players also become better through their knowledge of the world and their mastery of its rules. Shattering the glass tube in Brinstar with a Power Bomb after passing through it many times (and seeing another smashed tube) remains one of my most memorable gaming moments. This process of mastering the world was a major inspiration for Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown. We knew some players struggled to remember places they needed to revisit and we wanted to preserve their autonomy without letting them feel lost. Our solution is to let players “annotate” the map with screenshots to mark places to revisit later. I hope it gives players the same pleasure of exploration I felt when I first explored Zebes. Marc-A. Jean, artist on Infernax Infernax You know a game is special when, years later, you can still remember all the little details surrounding the first time you played it. It was August 1994, I was 11, and my brother and I had just spent all our hard-earned money on Super Metroid. It had been a beautiful summer day, and as the sun was setting, we turned on the SNES and holy hell, this game went hard right from the start! The eerie opening, the music, Ceres Station, the return to a sleeping Zebes under the rain… We played until bedtime, and I couldn't sleep that night. I could still hear Brinstar's haunting music in my head while thinking about the game. I was completely hooked. From discovering Wall Jumps and Shinesparks to the Baby Metroid's sacrifice and the final battle with Mother Brain, the journey was unforgettable. To this day, Super Metroid remains one of the greatest games I've ever played. The biggest influence definitely comes from an emotional standpoint: How can I make the player feel what I intend them to feel? The same way Super Metroid got me to obsess over its atmosphere, the original Metroid got me hyped up for a juicy power up every time I heard its distinctive item room music, and Fusion captivated me with its story and the fear of the SA-X. I try my best to pay special attention to the little details that give a game an emotional impact and leave a lasting impression on the player. Thierry Boulanger, game director, designer, and writer on The Messenger The Messenger My love for Metroid happened very slowly at first, and then extremely fast. I had the original on NES, but I was only 6 years old, so it mostly landed as a confusing and unnerving experience. I'd play it for a few minutes from time to time, just until the eeriness ended up getting the better of me. I think I just needed to be a bit older, because about a year later it all clicked. Before me was a very unique kind of experience, a genuine sense of being all alone, and impossibly far away, in a world filled with countless mysteries and intricacies. While I've enjoyed new installments throughout the years, the original Metroid on NES is the one I keep going back to. But then, isn't the best installment in a series always whichever one you happened to play in your formative years? During these formative years in elementary school, I was so obsessed with Metroid on NES that it led to an early ‘90s kid hustle. Stumbling upon my first Red Door, the mystery overwhelmed me and I spent almost two hours shooting at it. Then I remembered a friend had an NES Advantage arcade stick, but his price for borrowing it was $2, which I did not have to my name. At my grandparents' house over the weekend, I opened up about this insurmountable situation and was able to get myself a whole $2 bill by picking gooseberries for them. I could barely sleep that night, overwhelmed by visions of the Red Door opening, and the anticipation of what lay beyond. A few days later, I plugged in the NES Advantage and tucked it under the ottoman so the turbo button would stay firmly pressed all night. You can imagine my disappointment the next morning when I turned on the CRT TV and heard the telling "ding" sound effect in the speakers before the image even came on. It took me several months to find the missiles, during which time I still believed the door just might open with enough shots. In fact, part of me still believes… Maxine Martinez, texture artist and level designer on Pseudoregalia and animator, environmental artist, level designer, and concept designer on Vernal Edge Vernal Edge Metroid is such a great inspiration partly because it's a series with a lot of diverse entries. Super Metroid is a staple of diverse routing and replay value, the Prime series is made up of some of the most beautiful and atmospheric 3D games ever made, and Game Boy Advance entries like Metroid Fusion and Zero Mission have a pristine 2D game feel. It's hard not to feel inspired in some way when you're going through the catalogue! I think going through games I've released and future projects you'll most feel the influence of Super Metroid's Wall Jump and Shinespark. Both are initially obscure tools the game teaches you about much later than when you first obtain them (thanks, animals) and change your relationship with the environment. The Wall Jump shatters the game's illusion of a fixed progression, making replays exciting and encouraging tightly timed tricky jumps to push you into new areas. The Shinespark takes previously innocuous terrain like slopes and long runways and turns them into new opportunities to create intricate Shinespark chains leading to collectables or making re-traversal faster and more fun. The way these basic tools interact with common collision and evolve your relationship with the game is something I'm always looking to mimic in my games and is part of why I'm so addicted to level design and platforming. I suspect everyone has something unique that inspires them about the Metroid series because it excels in so many different fields and is always trailblazing! Thomas Happ, sole developer of Axiom Verge and Axiom Verge 2 Axiom Verge Metroid has had a special place in my heart ever since I first played it on NES in elementary school. I didn't actually own it; the friend I borrowed it from thought it was boring. But I loved it, especially after learning how to bomb jump and use the wall walking trick. So I borrowed it from that same friend over and over, since, again, he didn't like it. It's hard for me to explain this, but I think whatever was going on in my mind wasn't quite what was going on in other kid's minds. I felt like there might be a whole hidden world inside there, and I spent way too long trying to find it. It was such a unique feeling that I don't think I've experienced anything like that since then, and I've always been trying to chase that down. Of course I liked the game on its own, as well as Super Metroid and most of the sequels (especially Prime), but that unintentional meta aspect is what started the spiral, I think. Chris Harvey, technical director on Guacamelee and Guacamelee 2 Guacamelee! Exploring an interconnected world, acquiring an ability that recontextualizes how you interact with that world, and returning to familiar places to push deeper into hidden locations--that loop creates a “Eureka!” feeling unique to video games. Other NES-era titles had pieces of the formula, but Metroid brought it all together in a way that made you desperately want to explore and discover. As a kid, though, the original NES game was brutally hard and simply impossible to finish. That’s why everyone at school somehow knew the "JUSTIN BAILEY" password, using it as our lifeline to see deeper into the game. Years later, I was at a friend's house playing the original game, and after a grueling fight we finally defeated Mother Brain. We erupted into cheers. My friend’s older sister mocked us mercilessly for how ridiculous we were being, but we didn't care. She had no idea how incredible that victory felt to us. By the late 2000s, Nintendo had put Metroid on hold, and Konami had moved away from its Metroid-like Castlevania games. When Shadow Complex hit Xbox Live Arcade in 2009, it reminded me how much I loved that game structure and left me wondering, "Why isn't this being used more? I would love to make a game like this.” That question helped inspire us to make Guacamelee, which is our most explicit love letter to Metroid's design. But the series' influence didn't stop there. The core concept of gear-gating is a fundamental part of Drinkbox's DNA. You can feel that influence running through other games of ours, like Severed and Nobody Saves the World. That mechanical magic is one of the most rewarding feelings. James Montagna, assistant director on Shantae and the Pirate's Curse Shantae and the Pirate's Curse Long before creating games at WayForward, I grew up playing titles like Super Metroid, Metroid Fusion, and, really, the whole series! First, it was purely as a player for the joy of it, and then later as a designer I studied how those games teach and guide so naturally. To me, the Metroid games do both at the same time. What's fun is how present Metroid is in our day-to-day at WayForward. When we're building in this genre, the team constantly references those Nintendo classics with things like "Super Metroid does it this way." It's become a shared language, and a shorthand that instantly puts everyone on the same page. That's a testament to the work of the original teams: 40 years later, these games are just as much an inspiration as they are vocabulary. James Petruzzi, designer, director and writer on Chasm and designer, director, and programmer on Wolfhound Chasm I was about six or seven years old the first time I heard of Metroid. My neighbor bought the game, and I thought it was very strange from the start. The name was bizarre, the atmosphere was impenetrable, and the gameplay was tough and unforgiving. Coming from bright and cheerful platformers like Mario where you could only go one direction, it was like whiplash diving into Metroid's dark maze of a world. It was too tough for me at that time, especially without a map or guide, but later on in life I came back to it and my appreciation grew greatly for what they were able to achieve in an 8-bit game. The brooding atmosphere, close-quarters gun combat, and power-up system were all masterfully executed and have inspired countless games since. For my next game, Wolfhound, I wanted to go back to the roots of the genre and figure out what really made it tick. While most Metroidvania games have gone down the RPG path with tons of items, characters, sidequests, mechanics, and skill trees, I found myself drawn back to the elegant simplicity of Metroid and Super Metroid. The key ingredients I found were a large, dark world that feels indifferent to your presence, a tricky maze to navigate and conquer by finding key items that also increase your range of movements or attacks, and tight combat and platforming to tie it all together. I'm doing my best to design Wolfhound around those core pillars, and not give in to overstuffing the world and ultimately detracting from the atmosphere. Kyle Thompson, artist, programmer, and designer on Sheepo, Islets, and Crypt Custodian Islets I grew up playing freeware PC games that were inspired by the Metroid series (An Untitled Story, Knytt Stories, Infection by Shawn64), but I hadn't actually played any games in the franchise until I was an adult and making games of my own. I started with Super Metroid and loved it. I think the aspect of it that I found most inspiring was the way it was able to capture this feeling of dread and isolation. There is very little text or dialogue or direct storytelling, but the world feels like it has so much depth to it. Yarden Weissbrot, game designer, narrative designer, animator, and artist on Grime and Grime II Grime Metroid was a pioneer of ability gating and may just be the game that ultimately popularized it. This one aspect alone is integral to the Metroidvania genre, making exploration feel so magical and rewarding. But I bet that’s going to be something plenty of developers are going to bring up, so I’ll get more specific with a Metroid game that helped influence my most recent development work. For me, Metroid Dread specifically was the first game that showed me what a high production value Metroidvania could look like. The animations and how they complemented the quick movements, along with the camera shifts during combat and parries, all served as a sort of blueprint for me as I was working on Grime II. I went frame by frame, trying to figure out what made the movement so appealing. I started by trying to incorporate more cutscenes that zoomed into the character mid-gameplay, like in Dread, but that proved to be a tad too ambitious for our small team of six! I obviously didn’t match their quality level, but learning from them ultimately made our game’s presentation far better than it otherwise would have been. James Deighan, CEO of Mega Cat Studios, developer of God of War Sons of Sparta God of War: Sons of Sparta My way into Metroid was backwards. My favorite metroidvania of all time is Castlevania: Symphony of the Night. I've replayed it on every platform I could get my hands on, and it's one of those games that actually gets better with distance. SotN is what sent me hunting for everything else that scratched the same itch, and that's how I found my way back to Metroid and the rest of the Castlevania catalog. My appreciation for Metroid isn't nostalgia in the traditional sense where it was my introduction to the genre. It's tracing a genre back to its source and realizing the DNA was there the whole time in the Metroid and Castlevania franchises. Across our core team, like many of our friends and colleagues, the games we grew up on inspired us more than anything, and games like this are why Mega Cat exists. We started as a group of hobbyists making new cartridge-based games for the love of it. Much of our core team came out of the retro community because we genuinely loved it, not because it was a career move. We followed our dreams to make a game for the fans that has such a lasting impact. Earlier indie days, even games like Axiom Verge and Cave Story paved the way by creating their own interpretation and genre innovations that were inspired by the metroidvania roots from the NES era. When the opportunity came up to build Sons of Sparta, it wasn't just another project for us; it was the same instinct that made us start this company, pointed at a franchise we adore with God of War. Play 8-bit and 16-bit Metroidvanias and then play Sons of Sparta back to back, and you'll see the stacks of design inspiration we pulled straight from it. Working with Santa Monica Studio on this project, we leaned hard into that classic Metroidvania backbone with the sense that the map is never really "done.” New abilities just recontextualize ground you've already covered. That's the lineage we're honored to be part of. Brjánn Sigurgeirsson, founder and CEO of Image & Form, developer of SteamWorld Dig and SteamWorld Dig 2 SteamWorld Dig 2 Metroid was an absolute gold standard of player agency and subtle environmental storytelling. I think we came to appreciate it not by studying game design in a regular sense, but by getting lost in it as players. When we were making the first SteamWorld Dig, we didn't even set out to make a Metroidvania. We just wanted to make a game about digging down into the earth. But as soon as you give the player a tool to unlock a new area, and then force them to navigate back through a world they thought they already knew, you realize you're standing on the shoulders of Super Metroid. To us, Metroid means respecting the player's intelligence: no glowing arrows or hand-holding, just pure curiosity driving you forward. I think Super Metroid is a masterclass. The moment that sticks out isn't any of the boss fights or shiny new upgrades, but the opening on Zebes. You land, it's raining, there are no enemies, just complete silence. You walk down to where you fought Mother Brain in the original game, and it’s completely deserted. The atmosphere in those opening 10 minutes conveys isolation just as well as any modern 3D game with a movie-level budget. Silence and empty space in game design aren't wasted space; that’s where the atmosphere lives. It made us realize just how important it is to pace empowerment. In Metroid, Samus starts out relatively weak, but every upgrade isn't just a key to a door--it fundamentally changes how it feels to move through the world. When we designed Rusty in SteamWorld Dig and Dorothy in SteamWorld Dig 2, we stole that exact philosophy. Getting the High Jump Boots or the Hookshot shouldn't just open "Gate B"; it should make you feel like a superhero in an area that bullied you 20 minutes ago. Metroid taught us that the best rewards in game design are new verbs for the player to use. You feel it most in the rhythm of exploration, risk, and return. In SteamWorld Dig, the loop is “dig down, fill your bag, get terrified that you're going to die and lose your precious ores, and sprint back up to the surface to upgrade.” That tension between vulnerability and mastery comes directly from Metroid. Even though we had procedural generation in the first game and went for a handcrafted world in Dig 2, that classic Metroid DNA surely is there: the satisfaction of looking at a map screen, seeing a tiny gap you couldn't reach earlier, and realizing, "Ah, I can finally get back in there now." Keisuke Okabe, director, level designer, and scenario writer on Ender Lilies: Quietus of the Knights and Ender Magnolia: Bloom in the Mist Ender Lillies: Quietus of the Knights Super Metroid shaped my tastes and made me interested in making games. As a child, I was unfamiliar with even the term “sci-fi,” and so I found Super Metroid very exciting. Through that game, I realized even eerie settings can be lovely and beautiful. I’ve been drawn to dark, otherworldly realms ever since. I was particularly impressed by how Super Metroid’s world, story, and mechanics were all woven together to create a unified and deeply convincing world. That desire for consistency continues to influence my own work. Jens Andersson, programmer on Yoku’s Island Express Yoku's Island Express I grew up with the Commodore 64 and Amiga, so I was a bit late to the series, so it’s more Metroid’s contribution to the design space than the games themselves that I adore. But I did play Fusion, then Prime, but also Cave Story, one of the first great indie games, in my opinion. They really highlighted how exploration and gameplay could be so interconnected. Obviously, in Yoku’s Island Express, our Metroidvania pinball game, we drew on the core design pillars: light guidance and a world that gradually opens up as you unlock new abilities. But there’s no combat whatsoever. To me, that was never the best part of Metroid. What I loved was the exploration, and the feeling of mastery of the world I had been dropped into. Even in Indiana Jones and the Great Circle (which I was a design director on), we relied on that way of thinking. It wasn’t much of a Metroidvania, but it was certainly influenced by the same design philosophy, particularly in how the world opens up. We even made it possible to go “back in time,” so to speak, to use newfound abilities in true Metroidvania fashion, even though that didn't make sense for a lot of the people on the team. And you know what? I still haven’t played Super Metroid... Edgar Jesus, game designer on Constance Constance Back when I was a kid, game tips, cheats, and wild theories were passed around by word of mouth. Even before I ever played it, more than any other, one game was talked about in whispers in school hallways, like we were passing along forbidden knowledge. Metroid wasn’t just full of hidden items and passages. What made it different was that even its navigation and progression were meant to be discovered. Add to that a mood drenched in mystery and heavy atmosphere, and it felt existential, like peering into the secrets of the unknown. When I finally got my hands on it, it never let go of my subconscious again. Between my fascination with Samus and the tunic-wearing hero’s adventures, it’s no surprise exploration and self-guided discovery became core parts to my work as a designer, with Metroid always popping up as an inescapable reference regardless of the project. Its influence has left an undeniable mark on our dear medium and, I have no doubt it has done so for the imagination of the other arts, as well. Thomas Mahler, director and writer on Ori and the Blind Forest and Ori and the Will of the Wisps Ori and the Will of the Wisps I played the original Metroid on the NES and immediately noticed the similarities to Zelda. The main character started weak, explored, and got new abilities that then unlocked more exploration. That has always struck me as a very potent formula that just very nicely gives players that power fantasy they're craving. A few years later, Super Metroid launched, and it was a total masterpiece. Then after some time, Castlevania: Symphony of the Night came out, and I'm sure everyone immediately understood Konami was probably pretty big Super Metroid fans as well! And then... nothing happened for a long time. Metroidvanias suddenly got relegated to handhelds. I guess everyone wanted 3D games at that point. That is exactly why we started developing Ori. Because it didn't make sense that this genre got neglected. After we shipped Ori, a lot of publishers and indie devs saw Metroidvanias can absolutely be successful and deserve to be in the spotlight as long as they're done right. Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Saber Interactive Developer: Saber Interactive Release: TBA Released in 2002 for the PlayStation 2, the original Stuntman was a great concept that, for me anyway, proved far too challenging to be fun. Perfection was demanded, and extreme specificity was required for progress. It took all the fun out of controlling a car in a video game, which I imagine is how movie stunt drivers probably really feel after the fifth take of the same sequence, so in that way maybe it was a perfect simulation of the stunt driving experience.But I have always remained enamored with the fun concept. Who wouldn’t want to drive a stunt car in a Hollywood movie without the need to sign personal injury waivers and the danger of bodily harm? I played through four levels of Stuntman: Hollywood at Gamescom, including, perhaps most notably, the recently announced Jurassic World level. The team building the game at Saber Interactive, I believe, took away the same opinion I had back in 2002: if this game were more forgiving, it would be a lot more fun. That isn’t to say Stuntman: Hollywood is a total breeze. With the exception of the opening tutorial level, I had to retry each level at least once, but the challenge doesn’t feel as insurmountable as it did 24 years ago. Perfection is encouraged but not demanded, and specificity is only required in certain places.In practice, the game almost feels like a slowed-down rhythm game crossed with Simon Says. As you’re driving through the sets – perhaps the biggest sets ever constructed for any movie ever – a director shouts instructions in your ear: drift here, get close to that obstacle there, hit that ramp, drive through that other obstacle there. Along with the director’s voice, there are also plenty of on-screen indicators directing you where to go and telling you what to do. The first run through a level is frantic as you try to quickly respond to every directive, and it can be overwhelming, but there is a joy to being successful, and I maybe had the most fun replaying a level after completing it at least once. I played two levels that drove me through cities, one where I was trying to escape a collapsing factory during an earthquake, and finally the Jurassic World level. In that level, I drove a motorcycle away from an angry volcano while dinosaurs ran beside me, ramp-jumping over an angry Tyrannosaurus rex and sliding under the occasional obstacle. I haven’t been on many movie sets, but I can confirm from visiting the film set of Detective Pikachu that the animated monsters are not usually there when filming. My understanding is that they’re usually added later in something called “post”. But what do I know, and who cares? I would rather have the dinosaurs there to race with me and jump over, accuracy be damned. Despite all the bombastic action happening on screen, one of the most notable elements of the Jurassic World level was the fast-paced, percussive remix of the classic Jurassic Park theme. It was bizarre, and you would never catch me listening to it outside of the game, but in context the absurd arrangement worked pretty well.My big takeaway from Stuntman: Hollywood is that the two-decade-old video game concept might have finally found its footing. For those who want to pull off an undeniable take and nail every moment perfectly, I believe that still exists here. But for those like me who just want to participate in an admittedly hyperbolic simulation of being a stunt driver on a movie set, Stuntman: Hollywood is the most welcoming version of the experience the franchise has offered to date.

The Total War series has portrayed numerous conflicts, both historical and fantastical, but with Warhammer 40,000, the series launches into a new frontier with space, bolters and psykers in tow. It means a lot of changes for what Total War is, but its sense of scale remains intact, alongside a little bit of carnage.I played a three-part hands-on demo of Total War: Warhammer 40,000 at Gamescom 2026, which stair-stepped me up through the layers of strategy. My personal experience with the Total War series has been frequent toe-dipping and experimenting, but never committing full-time. I love the chaos of a Warhammer 40,000 video game, though, whether that means directing my forces in Dawn of War or tearing through swarms of Tyranids in Space Marine. So, while I'm reluctant to speak to the changes for this series, I can absolutely confirm Total War: Warhammer 40,000 can sate a thirst for explosions and declarations of loyalty to the Emperor. Four factions are currently planned for the game, and they're the ones you likely expect: Orks, Aeldari, the billions of humans in the Imperium forces, and of course, Space Marines.Part one of my demo put me through a small skirmish, pitting my small Space Marine detachment against a raging warband of Orks. Gradually, the tutorial walked me through the basics: units can be deployed behind a line, and then once battles commence, I can order them around, pausing or speeding up whenever I wish. My Space Marines may have been outnumbered, but their powerful bolters mixed with a good position – on top of a hill and in cover – saw waves of Orks dashed against the proverbial rocks. With enough time – and bombing runs I could call in on a cooldown – it didn't take long for me to route my foes.In the second section, I zoomed out to the larger map and commanded forces around the planet I'd found myself on, working my way towards a nearby Ork army. I made the mistake of sending a smaller force in first, rather than my larger outfit nearby, but it was fun to suddenly find myself in a position where I had to hold out for backup. Did I add unnecessary tension because of my own bad decision-making? Absolutely. But that seems like a fun way to enjoy this kind of game: trying to snatch victory from the encroaching jaws of defeat.  My final section, as a Creative Assembly team member described it, was a battle on the scale of something we'd likely see in the mid-to-late stages of a campaign. I had a sizeable set of units available: some assault units and basic bolter-wielding Space Marines, a number of specialty units like psykers and heavy gunners, and even a few vehicles.I was warned very few demo players emerged victorious from that battle, and I was not able to best expectations either. Despite carefully planning a strategy to hold a small but defensible line and then pincer from opposing flanks after the enemy closed in, it all fell to ruins fast. Partly because my units weren't fast enough to swing around and close in while my core still held, but also because I didn't notice my right flank was blocked off from a faster route by a parked train.Afterwards, I confirmed with a Creative Assembly developer that I could have blown up the train and punched through to the side of my foes, but a simple "move" command sent my right-side units swinging the long way around instead. I chalk it up to user error, though it does highlight a level of destruction and ingenuity players might need to get used to having, in this far-flung future.Getting a sense of the breadth of Total War: Warhammer 40,000's deeper systems and scope in the time I had allotted was difficult. For other games, an hour slice might be enough of a toothpick to see if the cake's baked well, but for something as big as a Total War game, it felt like just a taste.I'd certainly like to see how the other factions and races fare against the Orks and Space Marines, and get deeper into the longer-term progression systems. What I've seen so far confirms to me that Creative Assembly isn't holding back on its first foray into the 40,000 universe.

What was once a relatively niche genre has become extremely popular in recent years, especially as AAA and indie publishers alike explore their own distinct takes on the genre with added mechanics, interesting settings, and worlds to explore. The best metroidvanias are games that provide massive maps to explore, obstacles to conquer, and progression systems with constant "eureka" moments during your journey. There are so many to choose from that it can be hard to find the one that's right for you. We've combed through the massive library of games in the genre, and found tons of examples to help you get started on a 2D dive into this stylish field of games. This isn't meant to be a definitive list of the best of all time, but rather a sampling of great games that are widely available to play right now. The selections below are listed in alphabetical order, and we regularly reexamine this list to add the latest and greatest. And if you're looking for much more about metroidvanias as a whole, be sure to check out all of our coverage Metroidvania Week, a full week devoted to commentary and celebration of this iconic genre.
