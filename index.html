How To Steal And Register Cars With Advanced Trackers In GTA 6NEW
Grand Theft Auto 6 has a massive world, and you can be sure that there are thousands of vehicles driving across the countryside at any given point in time. While the vast majority can be hijacked with...
Grand Theft Auto 6 has a massive world, and you can be sure that there are thousands of vehicles driving across the countryside at any given point in time. While the vast majority can be hijacked without worries--well, aside from cops that might see you--a select few have anti-theft devices that make them an unbearable hassle. Thankfully, there are ways to steal vehicles with Advanced Trackers. Advanced Trackers: How exactly do they work? The Advanced Trackers in GTA 6 can be seen if you scan most high-end vehicles like sports cars and luxury cars. Basically, if it looks expensive and sells for a high price, you can bet that it has an Advanced Tracker. Here’s how the device works: Stolen vehicles with a Standard Tracker ping their location only if (a) they’ve significantly slowed down or stopped, and (b) a police car is nearby. Advanced Trackers, meanwhile, ping their location if you go below a certain speed. You essentially need to put your pedal to the metal when driving them. Otherwise, the police would know your exact location. Given the risk, most fence NPCs in the game won’t even accept stolen vehicles with Advanced Trackers. Well, except for one fella: Randy. The legit method: Do missions for Randy Randy is the one NPC in the game that accepts stolen cars with Advanced Trackers, but there are some caveats: You meet Randy while tackling main missions in chapter 3. He’ll give you a task to steal a Zorrusso, which also nets you the Key Cloner, a tool that allows you to hijack keyless cars. At this point, Randy and various Pay ‘n’ Spray shops will start accepting stolen vehicles with Standard Trackers. He’ll also tell you that he has a “wishlist” of sorts: A dozen or so high-end cars that you need to boost and bring to his garage. Note that some targets do have Advanced Trackers. He’ll only accept these as long as they’re part of his wishlist. Since he’ll keep them, you won’t be able to own/register them at all. Again, you need to drive fast to prevent the tracker from triggering. But you also have to be careful since the sell value of the car will decrease depending on the damage. Now, let’s say you’ve completed all of Randy’s requests. Come chapter 4, Randy will give you one more mission. This time, you need to boost a Mamba GT. After that ordeal, you’ll be able to remove Advanced Trackers via Pay ‘n’ Spray shops in Leonida. Yes, you’ll finally be able to register/own all those high-end vehicles you keep seeing in Vice City. The exploit method: Have your partner drive it There’s also another method if you wish to steal cars with Advanced Trackers in GTA 6. It’s an exploit that involves fiddling around with partner controls. Here’s how to do it: Have your partner tag along while you’re exploring. Locate a car with an Advanced Tracker. Don’t do anything with it yet. Look for a car near it, one that only has a Standard Tracker or no tracker at all. Make sure you unlock that vehicle. Next, unlock the door of the car with the Advanced Tracker… and immediately exit it. Open the partner interface by holding L2/LT. Then, select “You Drive.” This will cause your partner to enter the last vehicle that you interacted with--i.e. the one with the Advanced Tracker. As for your own character, simply hop on to the other car that you jacked. Then, open the partner interface again and select “Follow.” (If your partner is driving the vehicle, they won’t trigger the tracker’s system.) Drive your lowly vehicle to Randy’s shop, and watch as your partner follows behind you at a leisurely pace. When you’re in front of Randy’s garage, simply switch to your partner and deliver the vehicle. That’s it. No police chases, no need to drive over the speed limit, and no panicking due to the tracker’s alarm potentially going off. There was a time when video games were largely linear affairs. You either moved up and down or from left to right in enclosed spaces. As technology evolved, so did the scope of digital worlds. Now players are regularly treated to expansive environments filled with quests, interesting characters, and horizons that stretch further with every console generation. These experiences consume dozens if not hundreds of hours and knowing which titles are worth the time investment has become crucial. To that end, we’ve assembled a list of 10 great modern open-world games available today to help make what can be a paralyzing decision a little easier.Note that these are listed in release date order and don’t necessarily represent the best games of all time. Rather, this will be an ever-changing list of quality titles to arrive in recent years that you should go out of your way to get lost in.   Grand Theft Auto VI PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S Grand Theft Auto is arguably the blueprint for open worlds in video games, and its sixth entry is the series' peak. In a universe steeped in satire and cynicism, protagonists Jason and Lucia are instantly likable figures worth rooting for. Their love story is absolutely worth your time, but even if you made the decision to ignore it, you'd be overwhelmed by Leonida's exceedingly vast playground, ripe for exploration. When we say GTAVI's scope is staggering, we are partially talking about the literal size of the map, but we're mainly talking about its density; every corner is meticulously crafted with people to meet and places to rob. It's one of the most believable spaces in gaming history, bolstered by incredible graphics and populated with characters only a little more zany than you'd encounter in its real-life equivalent of Florida. Its dozens of cars feel great to drive, and its dozens of guns are a thrill to shoot, but if you decide to get really into birdwatching instead, that's fine by us. Grand Theft Auto VI is one of the best games of this generation – it's no surprise it made its way onto this top 10 list. | Our review Ghost Of Yōtei PlayStation 5 In an overly simplistic way, Ghost of Yōtei basically took everything that worked about Sucker Punch’s 2020 open world action game, Ghost of Tsushima, and improved it. Exploration, combat, visuals, and narrative are all major steps up from the previous game, which was already fantastic. Atsu’s journey of revenge, fighting and exploring her way through Ezo, is an always-compelling experience, not because it radically re-addresses or changes the mechanics that made Tsushima so great, but rather because they are carefully iterated on and improved in sometimes subtle ways. And as an open world game, it constantly excels at giving the player worthwhile distractions that all appear to the player naturally. You’re not bouncing between points on an overworld map – you’re literally following the wind where it takes you and always ending up somewhere amazing doing something incredible. | Our review Kingdom Come: Deliverance II PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch 2, PC Kingdom Come: Deliverance II is one of the highest-rated open worlds on this list, and for good reason. If Grand Theft Auto VI is the best modern-day open world, and Cyberpunk 2077 the best future-set open-world game, then KCDII is easily the best medieval open-world game in some time. Eschewing the more fantastical likes of the medieval open-world format – which The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt excellently covers on this list – KCDII does all it can to remain grounded and true to the 15th Century Kingdom of Bohemia land it’s set in. You follow Henry, whom you’ll craft through your decisions and upgrade choices, on a journey of retribution, rebellion, and reflection, and you’ll meet an unforgettable cast of characters along the way. They are the grounding elements to an otherwise wide-open adventure that lets you craft a narrative only you can craft. However you invest your time in this massive, sprawling medieval landscape will be reflected in the Henry you play as and how the world responds around you – there’s no shortage of mischief and chivalry to get up to, and across its dozens of hours, you’ll be taken through one of the most rewarding open games you can play today.  | Our Review Elden Ring PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PlayStation 4, Xbox One, PC FromSoftware proves its punishing yet satisfying style of action RPG absolutely works in an open-world format. Brimming with mystery and danger, Elden Ring allows players to make their own way, providing little guidance or hand-holding, making every discovery feel like a genuinely exciting and well-earned reward. Unlike traditional Souls games, slamming against a difficult boss isn’t the end of the line. You can simply explore elsewhere and fall down the rabbit holes of breaching an impregnable fortress, surviving a treacherous swamp, or taking down dozens upon dozens of other imposing enemies. Quality-of-life additions such as free fast travel and friendlier checkpointing before boss fights alleviate some stress, further encouraging you to hop aboard your spectral mount and uncover every secret of this incredible world. | Our Review Forza Horizon 5 Xbox Series X/S, Xbox One, PC The greatest trick the Forza Horizon series pulls is fooling players into liking racing games by disguising them as open-world RPGs. The latest entry, Horizon 5, serves as the pinnacle of that Trojan Horse-style design. Set in a stunningly beautiful rendition of Mexico, players sit behind hundreds of vehicles to compete in a seemingly endless number of wild races. Whether you're speeding against other cars or a 747 airplane, the sheer variety of events and an over-the-top tone make Horizon more lighthearted compared to most pure racing sims. In addition to traditional races, you can enjoy Horizon 5's entertaining take on battle royale, a zombie-infection mode, and absurd story-driven scenarios (like driving a parade float). A staggering collection of unlockable cars, purchasable homes to store said vehicles, plus cosmetics and goofy dances for your avatar create powerful incentives to score the winner's purse. Best-in-class driving mechanics solidify Horizon 5's first-place standing | Our Review Cyberpunk 2077 PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PlayStation 4, Xbox One, Switch 2, PC Though Cyberpunk 2077’s launch was rough (and that’s putting it lightly), developer CD Projekt Red successfully righted the ship and then some. Exploring rewards components used to build tools, vehicles, and even marine bases to aid your journey, and every upgrade feels like a triumph because of how difficult survival can be. Although Subnautica is not a horror game, few games have moments as bone-chilling as encountering a deadly, building-sized predator you didn’t realize had been lurking below you all along. Subnautica’s ocean hides danger and intrigue in equal measure, making it one of the most fascinating worlds we’ve ever dared to explore. | Our Review Red Dead Redemption II PlayStation 4, Xbox One, PC Few studios craft worlds that feel as lifelike as Rockstar Games, and Red Dead Redemption 2 might be their crowning achievement. In BOTW, one player’s approach to slaying Ganon can be totally different from another’s. Do you complete the four primary dungeons? Seek out the Master Sword? Build up your hearts? Ahead of the holiday season, we gathered some of our favorite video game recommendations focused on family interaction, spoke with Jackbox Games and Halfbrick (creators of Bluey’s Quest for the Gold Pen) about what it takes to make a game that everyone can enjoy together, and even have tips for new parent gamers hoping to introduce kids to their (and our) favorite hobby.From its inception, video games were created to encourage social interaction. Tennis for Two in 1958, arguably the first video game, had two players compete in digital tennis against one another. The single-player video game came later, and as much as we love them, some of our best gaming memories are centered around playing with family and friends. And as we get older, we’re making new gaming memories with our own kids.Making video games is hard. That any game crosses the finish line is a miracle of teamwork and perseverance, and the hardest trick of all is making the final product feel effortless. Arguably, none are more practiced at feeling like effortless joy machines than the games that effectively include players of all ages and skill levels in the experience. And to take it a step further, Jackbox Games is perhaps the best at this sub-genre of video games: inclusive party games that are genuinely for everyone.Jackbox Games studio creative director Arnie Niekamp sheepishly and humbly agrees when I offer this accolade and also says thank you. “It really matters to us,” Niekamp says. “We really value, first, just inclusivity in general, and are always trying to think of how we can make our games playable by as many people as possible, or how we can expand that.” Jackbox Party Pack 12 Games For Literally EveryoneJackbox Games has released its 12th Party Pack by the time you’re reading this issue, and part of the secret of that ongoing success is that its creators stick with the company for a long time. “The person that came in and started making games as a young single person also ages into having a family, having kids, and having that perspective of, ‘Oh wait, it’s also really fun to play these games with my kids, with my family.’”Even if a game is primarily focused towards young players, being fun for everyone is “very important” Halfbrick writer Brendan Deboy says when talking about making Bluey’s Quest for the Gold Pen. “Bluey’s humor, relationships and themes give children and parents reasons to enjoy its stories together. We wanted that shared enjoyment to carry into the game.”Brooke Breit, Jackbox’s game product manager and The Jackbox Party Pack 12’s lead, cites that family element as an important detail to making games for everyone. “We playtest our games constantly throughout production amongst ourselves, and that’s one of the markers of, ‘Are we hitting success within the mechanics? Within how it plays?’” Breit says. “And part of that also involves us playing with our own families as a part of that playtesting process [...] All of our kids have a lot of opinions on what makes a really good Jackbox game.”Breit also points out a metric the team has accidentally discovered over the years that has helped them figure out why a game works for all ages when the creation process happens to be going well. “There’s a little bit of… maybe the younger and older crowd also intersects with the drunk crowd,” Breit says with a thoughtful laugh.Deboy also discusses the importance of family playtesting saying, “Children of Halfbrick parents playtested the game, with ages ranging from four to 10. Their parents reported what they enjoyed most and where they became stuck.” And avoiding the stickiness is crucial. “We wanted challenges to be satisfying without any single obstacle preventing someone from seeing the rest of the game,” Deboy says. “If something is too difficult, they can leave it, try another activity and return later. Harder challenges were often tucked away in harder-to-find places, helping players encounter easier activities first.” Super Mario Party Jamboree No Phones (Kind Of)Jackbox also figured out another secret weapon for great family gaming and gaming with kids: getting away from cell phones. This may be an odd element to cite considering the vast majority of Jackbox games are played collectively on individual phones, but when you’re using your phone as a controller, you’re not using it to check social media.“You can’t get phones out of people’s hands anyway, so you might as well use it as a tool,” Niekamp says. “Engagement is absolutely a huge part of our design, and we talk a lot about what is on your device,” Breit says. “What is on the game screen? Where do we want people’s focus to be at any given moment? And are we giving opportunity and motivation for people to talk to each other and actually engage with each other?”Deboy cites another way to encourage engagement as it did with Halfbrick’s Bluey game. “We wanted everyone to be able to finish the game, while giving players of different ages and abilities things to enjoy. That meant providing choices. Some activities test timing and motor skills, while others reward exploration, searching or logical thinking. Players can choose what suits them without having to complete every obstacle.”When asked what other cooperative and family-focused games are doing right and wrong, Deboy and the Halfbrick team recalled games they played (and got annoyed with) when they were young. “Some of us remembered childhood games where one difficult boss or tricky section stopped us from ever reaching the end,” Deboy says. “You could spend weekend after weekend trying, or replay the opening levels because those were the only ones you could finish. That experience informed our approach.”Niekamp points out his appreciation for games like Mario Party (which he calls brilliant, but wishes it moved a little faster), but mainly says there just aren’t enough fun co-op family games. “I wish there were more,” Niekamp says. “Especially when your kids are younger, there’s a real sweet spot of like… it’s so great when you start playing stuff together, and finding those things is so incredible and awesome, and then you just start to feel like you run through them pretty fast, and you’re sort of looking for more.”We agree with Niekamp, and alongside helpful tips for how to introduce young or new players to video games, you can also read on for some of our recommendations (including a different, but similarly fun Bluey video game) for recent games we think are particularly suited to playing with your family. Sackboy: A Big Adventure How to Introduce Kids to Your Favorite HobbyWhether it’s your friend’s kids, nephews and nieces, or your own children, many of us are eventually confronted with the challenge: How can I share my love of gaming with someone younger who is just getting into the hobby, and enjoy the experience with them? Here are some tried-and-true tips that have served us well.Spectate To StartEspecially with the youngest set, gaming can start with opportunities to watch and contribute as you play. Choose low-complexity exploration games with some freeform choices about where to go, cozy games with building options, or age-appropriate narrative games. You handle the controls, but let your kid choose how to proceed. Talk to them about what you’re doing. Tell them more about the game and why you like it. Help them feel like the cool things that happen are the result of their choices.PatienceIt should perhaps go without saying, but once they have their hands on the controller, accept moving slowly as a rule. Encourage small wins like learning which button does what. Don’t criticize mistakes. Acknowledge that losing and failing, and then coming back to try again, is part of the fun. Choose games with simple control inputs, easy modes, and forgiving restarts on failures. If you want them to eventually be able to play at your level, be patient enough that they want to keep playing.Cooperative WinsOnce they’re ready to actively play together, it’s hard to go wrong with cooperative play. In particular, watch for titles that allow one cooperative partner to help the other out of a bind with revives, carries, or boosts. Celebrate together when you overcome a notable challenge, even if you did most of the work.Don’t Overdo ItStart with short sessions, and follow their interests and focus level. Even if you’re in the middle of an exciting section of a game, take a break when you sense they’re getting frustrated – at least at first. Regardless, don’t play too long. Resilience in confronting really tough encounters, puzzles, or challenges can come with time. Watch for things that are scary – even if they don’t seem like they should be scary – and either divert from that thing, or talk together about the difference between a game and real life.Stay OfflineThere are plenty of amazing online games, and many can be safe to play with kids. But other players add a level of randomness that can lead to major frustration, unsafe incoming communication, and wildly different skill levels. Start with games that don’t require playing with others online, and if you do graduate to online play at some point, stay directly involved in play sessions.Follow The ExcitementWhile you can introduce your favorites, kids will quickly learn what they like. Follow that enthusiasm, and engage with the games and experiences they’re excited about. Ignore the advice above that doesn’t apply to your new gamer; some kids will love competitive play or be eager to confront high challenge and frustration, and that’s just fine.
