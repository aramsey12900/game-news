Some points of interest are more important than others in Grand Theft Auto 6, and airports are always at the top of a player's list of places to see. While airports often play a critical role in a GTA story, they're also useful for knowing where planes are located on the map. We've already covered how to get a plane and other aircraft in GTA 6, but airports are different. Whether you're looking to steal a plane or land one so you can keep it hidden in a hangar, GTA 6 has several airports and airfields you need to know about. You can see where they're located in Leonida in the guide below. However, the map also features unnamed locations that function as airfields you can use whenever you please. These are much harder to find, but we've scoured the map to locate each one. Below, you'll see the complete list of airports and airfields along with their precise locations in GTA 6. Vice City International Airport This is the largest and most obvious airport in GTA 6. VCIA is located right in the middle of Leonida (west Vice-Dale County) and plays a key role in the main story. While you'll visit here a few times during the story, you're also free to break into the airport, inside and out, to see everything it contains. Be warned, though, as breaking into the exterior of VCIA gives you an immediate three- or four-star Wanted level. Mile Zero Air Station This is the other major airport in GTA 6, but unlike VCIA, Mile Zero Air Station doesn't fly commuters out of the city. Instead, the air station is a military base that houses fighter jets, tanks, and armored vehicles. The base is extremely difficult to break into, and you'll be swarmed by guards if you're caught here. However, it's a high-risk, high-reward location that allows you to drive some of the best vehicles in the game. You can find Mile Zero Air Station west of Key Lento, hidden away in the southwestern part of the map. Wild Hog Point This is the only named airfield in GTA 6, and you'll eventually see it by playing through Pilot Pete's side questline. There are free planes for the taking all over Wild Hog Point, and it's fairly easy to get to during Chapter 2. If you're in Key Lento or Vice City, though, it's quite the drive, as you'll find it in northern Kelly County, southwest of Port Gellhorn. Watson Bay Airport Next up is the Watson Bay Airport, which you can find west of Watson Bay and south of Bobcat Key in Mariana County. Not much goes on at this location, but it can be a great spot to evade police, as there's only a single road that goes in and out of the airport. Mariana County Airfield Once again, there's an unnamed airfield in Mariana County that you won't see much of in GTA 6. However, this airfield comes into play during Pilot Pete's questline, and it's more central than Watson Bay Airport. You'll also find Paradise Garage nearby, giving you an easy spot to store a vehicle for a quick getaway. LTF Airfield This is the final airfield in Mariana County, and it's tucked between the Leonida Keys and Grassrivers regions. LTF Airfield doesn't have much to see, but there are some spare planes you can take if you want to head to the skies for sightseeing or use a parachute in GTA 6. Leonard County Airfield We're heading north to Leonard County, and this airfield is situated just north of Bellville. On the map, this doesn't look like an airfield, but rather another subdivision for the Cellerica cult in GTA 6. However, it's an active airfield that just doesn't see much use during your travels in Leonida. Sundown Private Airfield The Sundown Private Airfield is located in Leonard County, just west of Waning Sands. Despite being called a private airfield, we never found a massive amount of security that would take you down for being there. While I doubt that Navi directly inspired this game-design precedent that modern AAA games employ far too often, I do think that The Legend of Zelda’s seminal “annoying sidekick” set a precedent for how games disseminate information to the player. Ocarina of Time rightfully receives credit for its influence on how 3D video games are built and how players navigate through 3D spaces. At the same time, I see Ocarina as the beginning of a trend where grating, unskippable, and unavoidable tutorials come in the form of an actual character. At the time, Navi was a necessity--Shigeru Miyamoto, despite having problems with Navi, admitted so, saying that the game would be “unfriendly to players” without her hints. Gaming technology had advanced significantly, and 3D environments were still a relatively new frontier; help was needed, especially for children or less experienced players. But that doesn’t cancel out the irritation that Navi would spark when she interrupts your gameplay, and the condescension that comes across when she explains what to do as you’re about to do it. The infamous pestering voice clips of “Hey!” and “Listen!” certainly didn’t help. Whereas Navi had faults in the original game, she now feels like a key part of your adventure in the remake. Going into the Ocarina of Time remake with these strong feelings, I admit that I’m surprised I came out after rolling credits not just being fine with Navi, but actually liking her quite a bit. Many of the pain points from the original game were addressed one way or another. For one, Navi wasn’t interrupting gameplay to chime in; the only time you really hear from her in actual gameplay is when you manually press right on the D-pad to receive hints about enemies and certain environmental elements. Her most useful in-game function felt more like a deliberate choice that players could make. But to my surprise, Navi felt like a fully realized character in the remake. Voice acting in cutscenes and the higher production value certainly made this possible. Thanks to added dialogue, Navi feels like a proper companion character to Link, emoting and speaking for him when this silent protagonist obviously cannot. Navi carries much of the weight in conversations with NPCs like the Great Deku Tree and the Sages, advocating for Link and moving exposition along at a nice pace.  I think about the gag when the game’s central Goron character, Darunia, congratulates Link, aggressively patting him on the head and accidentally knocking him out. In the original game, Navi floats around with no reaction, as usual. In the remake, Navi not only checks up on Link, but lightly chastises the bulked-up Goron (“Hey! You’re too strong for your own good, Darunia!”). Like other characters in the remake, Navi received a visual update, despite her simple design. Navi is still chatty in the remake, but Nintendo was wise to make much of her in-game dialogue relayed through speech bubbles, and instead of patronizing tips, they are observations and exclamations that reflect Navi’s curious-but-sometimes-childish personality. Aesthetically, I appreciate that Navi, usually depicted as a plain orb of light, has texture, as though she’s covered in some kind of fuzz. And personality-wise, everyone is a lot more colorful, while still staying true to their original characters. It’s changes like these that make all the characters, Navi included, feel more tangible and grounded, which only helps make the Ocarina of Time remake feel more immersive. This revised Navi still left a little to be desired; I wish I had the ability to prompt Navi to give a hint when I needed it, or at least be able to talk to her the same way you could for Midna in Twilight Princess or even Fi in Skyward Sword. Regardless, Navi turned from a tooltip regurgitator to a real character. If Nintendo could learn how to fix Navi, then hopefully other blockbuster games could do the same for their own talkative, annoying sidekicks.
