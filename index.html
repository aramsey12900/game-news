The Nintendo Switch version of Aliens: Fireteam Elite is now completely removed from sale and impossible to play for people who bought it. Developer Cold Iron Studios announced earlier this year that Fireteam Elite, a game that runs in the cloud and not locally, would be shut down on August 5, and now that day has come. The game was delisted on the Switch's eShop earlier this year, along with other digital platforms. However, the game remains playable on other platforms in an offline state. Because the Switch version is streamed over the cloud, it's now totally unplayable. Fireteam Elite originally launched in August 2021 on PlayStation, Xbox, and PC, before coming to Switch in April 2013 via the cloud. A sequel, Fireteam Elite 2, is coming this August on PlayStation, Xbox, and PC, with the Switch 2 edition arriving later in the year. These kinds of issues, along with physical media going away, has prompted anger and frustration from fans, along with concerns about game preservation. Cloud gaming has been around for years, in various stages, and Take-Two boss Strauss Zelnick recently said he expects adoption to continue to grow. During an earnings call recently, Zelnick said he believes the industry will be in "commercial streaming mode" within three years. He and others have theorized that expanded access to cloud gaming could help bring in more revenue by way of reaching more people. With console prices continuing to go up, people might see cloud gaming as an increasingly attractive opportunity to play the latest games without needing to own expensive hardware. For this to work, though, there needs to be low latency, Zelnick said. People would also need steady and reliable connections, along with internet plans with generous data caps, or no caps at all. The road to cloud gaming has been paved with plenty of issues. Google's Stadia service previously shut down and the cloud versions of Kingdom Hearts games, like Fireteam Elite for Switch, will be shut down in the future. These are issues that could engender mistrust with the entire concept of cloud gaming. In 2019, Zelnick said clouding gaming was not going to revolutionize the gaming industry, nor did he buy into the theory that cloud streaming would open up a whole new market of people who previously didn't play games.

1666: Amsterdam – Hands-On With The Assassin's Creed Creator's Long-Awaited Passion ProjectNEW
...
Platform: PC Publisher: Panache Digital Games Developer: Panache Digital Games Assassin’s Creed creator Patrice Désilets has moved mountains to bring his passion project 1666: Amsterdam to fruition. The witchcraft-focused action-adventure game was originally conceived over 15 years ago while Désilets was still at Ubisoft. Through a series of tumultuous events, the project has changed studios and been at the center of a years-long lawsuit over rights ownership between Ubisoft and Désilets. Now that 1666: Amsterdam will finally see the light of day, I traveled overseas to its namesake city to play an hour-long build Panache Digital Games prepared for media, which I do at the Amsterdam Dungeon, a local horror attraction that’s part Universal Halloween Horror Nights, part history lesson. Amid the muffled screams of guests enduring the dungeon’s creepy rooms and spirited live performers, it creates the perfect atmosphere to dive into Panache’s occult adventure.  The demo begins immediately after the free prologue chapter that launched alongside the game’s re-reveal at Summer Game Fest. I control Noa Brooklyn, a young crimson-clad witch who has just completed her initiation into the Zaindaris, a magical faction that raised her to become a Collector. Noa’s primary purpose is to identify and eliminate entities called Originals, powerful beings who can assume human form to live amongst us, sometimes for centuries. The Zaindaris granted the Originals their power long ago by bestowing Nux, an ancient magic, upon chosen individuals in an attempt to elevate humanity; instead, the Nux corrupted many of them over time, and they now pose a danger to the world. The Zaindaris have since been engaged in a generations-long cycle of training a Collector to reclaim the Originals’ power to restore balance. Now, it’s Noa’s turn. Noa’s journey won’t be the only time period players will experience. In a fitting twist from the mind behind Assassin’s Creed, the game prominently features a more contemporary setting. It’s New Year's Eve in 1999, and a young man named Aaron visits Amsterdam with his lover. Turns out his partner is secretly a Zaindaris. They engage in a sex ritual that somehow transports Aaron’s consciousness to 1666, where he awakens in the body of a cat during the night of Noa’s ritual; Noa chooses him as her familiar, or Union Cat. This begins a fascinating partnership between the steadfast Noa and reluctant Aaron as they must work to help one another. But there’s one more era at play. If you played the prologue, you met a woman named Clio, seemingly in the 2020s, investigating a letter from her father. Clio’s dad, it turns out, is Aaron, and his message recounting his and Noa’s adventure serves as the narration. Clio’s full role in 1666: Amsterdam is unclear, but given she was evidently conceived during the ritual that kicks off the game’s events, it’s clearly important.  After completing the ritual, the gathering is interrupted by witch-hunting militia. Chaos ensues, giving me the first sample of combat. The right shoulder trigger unleashes a basic combo attack, with Noa pummeling foes using her staff. Stunning them at the brink of death triggers a flashy final blow. A dodge allows Noa to evade attacks from the often four or five enemies surrounding her, and she consumes potions to heal. The melee combat doesn’t pack much satisfying impact in this in-development build, but it gets the point across. The enemies also tend to take turns attacking, which feels disappointingly dated and reminds me of dealing with mobs in the early Assassin’s Creed games. Defeating a group paves the way for another mob to take their place, with dramatic camera zooms displaying their shouts such as “Don’t move” and “Blaggard.” It’s a stylish touch, but one I grew tired of after consecutive encounters. I’d rather they just take their lickings without the prerequisite introduction.The only magic attack at my disposal lets me blow up objects such as barrels and crates. This proves much more satisfying, and can instantly kill or heavily damage nearby targets. However, Désilets later reveals in a separate hands-off demo other abilities Noa will gain. For example, she can unleash tendrils that ensnare and suspend foes, and temporarily petrify enemies, among other powers. Gathering Nux energy allows Noa to perform these feats, and they look fun to pull off. Noa also commands traversal abilities, such as the power to glide over water and warp across platforms to quickly get around a freely explorable Amsterdam that, while large, is denser than wide. This scale is partly due to Panache being a smaller team – 70 employees strong – and its goal of ensuring that every building has explorable interiors. This design choice plays into another key part of 1666: Amsterdam: investigating Originals. Finding them involves unearthing clues such as notes (often by scavenging homes and businesses), analyzing your surroundings for helpful details, and speaking with citizens to glean the required information: their appearance, seal, and original name. Investigations occur during the day. Once night falls, it’s time to confront the Original. Another surprise is that missions have a roguelike element. After accepting a contract to hunt an Original, players choose one of three powers from a randomized pool. These can be stat buffs such as increased health or offensive powers like the aforementioned Petrify. Panache gives players the freedom to investigate Originals as they see fit, with many avenues for gathering information discreetly or more chaotically. Sadly, I only get to watch Désilets tackle a full investigation, but finding clever ways to track down Originals looks to be the bulk of 1666: Amsterdam’s gameplay. I hope it’s fleshed out well enough to remain consistently engaging. To explore more safely, Noa can freely shift between her witch and civilian appearances. The latter draws less attention and makes citizens more amenable towards you. This matters, as an attention meter builds whenever you do questionable things, such as peeking through windows in front of others or openly performing magical acts. A filled meter invites the full wrath of the militia, forcing you to either fight your way to safety or flee to one of many safehouses Noa can retreat to in order to extinguish the heat. These safehouses also allow her to craft potions using recipes and ingredients found by searching through spaces. If the militia defeats Noa, she’ll be sent to prison, where you’ll have to escape. However, Panache is still figuring out this particular element and how severely it will punish multiple incarcerations. As for Noa’s partner Aaron, you can switch control to him at any time. As a cat, he can access places Noa can’t. Panache has created buildings that can be accessed in multiple ways. Aaron can leap up and around buildings to enter them, bypassing locked doors and opening them for Noa. Like everything else in this shaky development build, platforming was rough and occasionally unresponsive, but it reminded me of Stray’s deliberate yet graceful traversal. Aaron can also distract certain guards eager to pet him. On the flip side, those fearful of black cats are vulnerable to possession. Approaching a frightened person and hitting R2 unleashes a hiss, followed by a timed minigame where Aaron pounces upon them and scratches their face. This act allows Aaron to hijack their bodies, useful for exploring and letting Noa into restricted spaces. Aaron can also transmute Lux into Nux. I enjoyed the dynamic of switching between Noa and Aaron to figure out how to combine their unique talents to access tricky locations.  The game’s digital Amsterdam is impressively realized. Panache’s environmental level and art direction draw inspiration from 17th-century Dutch painter Johannes Vermeer (historically living during the game’s timeframe), famed for specializing in domestic interior scenes capturing everyday life of the period. 1666: Amsterdam's visual presentation has a painterly flourish that adds flair to the 3D models. When 1666: Amsterdam becomes available in Early Access on August 25, players can only explore the game’s early chapters, but Désilets calls it a “pretty big chunk” of content. He confirms that the game’s story outline, lore, and rules are complete, with Panache polishing and expanding dialogue and gameplay mechanics. Désilets emphasizes Panache’s eagerness to build the game with the community, and says to expect it to remain in Early Access for at least one year. As a self-professed “old timer,” Désilets recalls the days when a game printed to disc was complete and unchanging, flaws and all. He’s excited for players to witness 1666: Amsterdam’s development and, hopefully, provide valuable feedback. Désilets also confirms the game’s reported AI placeholder assets have been removed and no such content is present in the Early Access launch.My demo of 1666: Amsterdam was, as expected, rough around the edges, as an in-development build of a game launching in Early Access. The experience didn’t wow me from a polish standpoint, and I worry if the game will at least feel more stable and fun to play than the build I played when it arrives in a couple of weeks. But there are exciting gameplay and narrative ideas at play with Désilets leaning on some concepts from Assassin’s Creed but reworking them under a cool witchcraft premise. After over a decade of waiting and uncertainty, it’s tough not to feel happy for Désilets and even inspired by his persistence to will 1666: Amsterdam into existence. The project has a long road ahead, and although it could be off to a rocky start, here’s hoping it can eventually realize its enchanting potential.

No Game Has Ever Needed Early Access More Than 1666: AmsterdamNEW
Following its reveal at Summer Game Fest earlier this year, 1666: Amsterdam quickly became my most anticipated game of 2026. While the entire world seems to be pining for Rockstar's next big adventure...
Following its reveal at Summer Game Fest earlier this year, 1666: Amsterdam quickly became my most anticipated game of 2026. While the entire world seems to be pining for Rockstar's next big adventure in Vice City, after that showcase I found myself ensnared by Panache's promise of a dark tale full of witchcraft and stunningly rendered cobblestone streets. Its initial reveal--coupled with the knowledge that one of the key members behind the first few Assassin's Creed titles was attached to the game--left me extremely eager to go hands-on with 1666: Amsterdam. Fortunately, it didn't take long for the studio to extend the offer, and off to Amsterdam I went. Less fortunate, however, was my experience playing 1666: Amsterdam. Though it's important to note that Panache intends to release the game in early access, which certainly justifies its overall lack of polish, what I'm more concerned about are its glaring structural issues--issues that I hope can be addressed but seem so embedded in the overall experience that I am admittedly a bit nervous. I see the vision, I think, and am hopeful that Panache's commitment to player feedback might fix some of the game's shortcomings. But as of right now, 1666: Amsterdam needs quite a bit of work before I can muster up the excitement I once had. A tale of two (or three) timelines During my preview, I played about an hour and 20 minutes of 1666: Amsterdam, starting shortly after the events of the prologue that is currently available to play on Steam. Throughout that time, I played as both the game's main characters, Noa and Aaron, across two of its three timelines, 1999 and 1666, and in both human and Union-Cat forms. I also played, quite briefly, as Aaron's daughter Clio, who resides in that aforementioned third timeline and essentially acts as the narrator of the story as she translates and reads from a tome, referred to as the Noxifer, that details Aaron and Noa's adventure. If that sounds like a lot, it's because it is. And that's just the beginning. Despite featuring dual protagonists, my time spent playing 1666: Amsterdam leads me to believe that it largely centers around Noa, a Zaindaris witch who has been chosen as The Collector. As for what being The Collector entails, we learn that Noa has been tasked with eliminating a cadre of power-hungry figures with extraordinarily long lifespans and the ability to use Nox, a sort of dark magic, in order to restore world order. Paradoxically, Noa relies on Nox as well. Nox and Lux serve as her primary magic sources, each represented by a meter at the bottom of the screen to fuel her various spells. Both energy types manifest as glowing orbs throughout the environment, and her Union-Cat--who is actually her partner Aaron trapped in feline form--can convert Lux into Nox and vice versa, allowing you to manipulate resources on the fly.  Noa in her traditional garb. To take down these corrupted souls, Noa must first collect their seal and discover their real name, the latter of which can prove difficult as most of these individuals have led extraordinarily long lives and assumed various identities throughout them. After gathering these, Noa must cast the Nominem spell on the night of the full moon, as that's when her power is at its strongest. As a matter of fact, as soon as the full moon passes, Noa is rendered completely powerless, and must wait until the next full moon before she can attempt to take down a new target. After casting Nominem, Aaron--still in his cat body--must scratch the enemy to unleash their demonic Amalgam form. Defeating an Amalgam unlocks a coffer containing a Nox bracelet (often randomized), which upgrades the Heeparan--a strange gauntlet on Noa's left arm--with new abilities. After taking down my first Amalgam, I received Erbuilden, an ability that allowed me to reconstruct destroyed objects. But while this specific power felt scripted for progression, Panache founder Patrice Désilets told me that the game's coffers are largely used as a means to incorporate roguelite elements into the game. The wrong kind of puzzling Each moon cycle--or Esbat, as the game calls them--Noa is tasked with taking down one of these demons. Typically, each Esbat begins at Noa's home, where a coffer and a contract are waiting for her. The coffer, placed there by her fellow witches, is filled with an assortment of Nox Bracelets and other boons, altering the abilities you can use. Shortly after my preview, Désilets showcased a later portion of the game, in which I saw him use an ability that gave Noa the ability to create portals for easy traversal, and another that gave her the ability to levitate enemies. According to Désilets, the game will feature several different abilities, though with it being in early access, they didn't want to share an exact number just yet. Regardless, these variations have the potential to inject some variety into the gameplay, which is crucial considering it suffers from some fairly significant issues--particularly when it comes to combat.  In 1666: Amsterdam, encounters open with a bizarre Persona 5-like presentation: enemies shake their fists and spout soundbites, while stylized text reading things like "knave," "justice," and "you will fall" pop up around them. All of this feels completely out of place, given the game's darker tone. An example of what is seen at the start of each combat. You're then notified of the encounter's threat level, though every level I encountered felt similar in difficulty due to the game's rudimentary combat. Without any active Nox bracelets, combat devolves into a combination of dodging and mindlessly swinging a broomstick, making these encounters incredibly dull. Oftentimes, my enemies would get stuck in hit-stun loops, but this felt more tedious than empowering. Amalgam boss fights offered slightly more variety, requiring well-timed dodges and a bit more patience, but all in all, 1666: Amsterdam's combat largely lacks flow and sophistication. The asymmetrical puzzle-solving challenges are similarly underwhelming, with most of them relying on whoever is in cat form unlocking a door so the human character can keep walking. Later in the preview, I unlocked the ability to hiss at and possess NPCs who are afraid of cats, allowing me to assume control of key-weidling janitors or lockpicking thieves. This feature seems like it could be a lot of fun, and when paired with the abilities of your Nox bracelets, I imagine it shakes up combat, exploration, and puzzle solving significantly. I can imagine a world in which these foster Dishonored-style freedom in how you approach objectives, though it feels very far off. However, if 1666: Amsterdam is aiming to be a more stealth-driven game--like Dishonored or perhaps more aptly, Assassin's Creed--NPCs need significant improvements. As it stands, NPCs feel heinously unintelligent, making stealth and concealment lack the needed danger to be compelling. They are also extremely dated in their writing and animations--many of them feel as if they have come straight from a AA action game published in 2007. Noa asks Aaron to go inside a home to unlock the front door. Strange writing, stranger scenarios These flaws were highlighted during an utterly perplexing 10-minute sequence that took place during one of Aaron's portions of the game. After traveling forward to 1999, Aaron is tasked with going to his hotel's courtyard to retrieve an ancient tome so he can figure out a way to cure his possessed, secret-witch girlfriend. The moment Aaron opens his hotel room door to embark on this quest, "Free Bird"--yes, Lynyrd Skynyrd's "Free Bird"--begins playing.  Now, you would think if a game is going to invoke "Free Bird," it would be during some absurd, over-the-top, hyperviolent spectacle, such as how it's used in Kingsman: The Secret Service. But no, in this case, the nearly 10-minute-long song plays at full blast while Aaron, donning only boxers, strolls through the hotel, and Noa, now a cat, unlocks doors for him. Absurd as that is, what makes things even stranger is that the hotel path is lined with completely static NPCs blocking off rooms. It's clear they're meant to prevent you from taking detours and highlight which doors you need to unlock, but the sheer vacancy on display is both laughable and unnerving. And though the game does delve a bit into horror, offering a few flashing screens and jump scares, this particular situation does not feel at all intentional. A mob of expressionless NPCs block Aaron's path. About six minutes into this ordeal, Aaron remarks that he just realized he was walking around the hotel half-naked--that's the kind of state he was in, he jokes. He then tells Noa that people are beginning to freak out about his state--but it's hard to believe that's true when the masses of NPCs remain utterly still and silent. In general, 1666: Amsterdam's writing needs work. Noa's dialogue was fine and her arc compelling, but Aaron's constant tone of utter bewilderment and use of expletives long after it seemed as though he'd embraced the strangeness of his situation were grating. It doesn't help that some portions of Aaron's dialogue were delivered via text-to-speech, replacing his voice actor with a fast-taking robot that took me out of the moment. Hopefully this will be fixed prior to the game entering early access. Not-so-catlike reflexes I also hope that the game will feature far better tutorials and onboarding. I am all for quiet games that emphasize unguided exploration--I am a Soulsborne sicko, after all. But Amsterdam just felt convoluted. At the very start of my preview, I was told to use my Lux to power up a body of water so I could float across it. Great, sounds fun. The game prompted me to go to its settings menu and view the corresponding tutorial, yet that tutorial omitted the actual button inputs. Confused, I went back to the game where I was just prompted to simply press "A," yet as I did, nothing happened. Fortunately, one of the developers saw me sitting in front of my PC, trying my hardest to angle Noa every possible way and visibly confused, and told me how to do it. Shortly after, I was stuck again, as the game also doesn't tell you how to jump over obstacles in the water; the developer had to inform me that navigating obstacles required pulling back on the analog stick, which felt awkward and resulted in terrible camera angles.  Aaron, in cat form, peers down a vivid hall. Movement controls--particularly while in cat-form--are a frequent source of frustration. Instead of free jumping and fluid traversal, feline movement relies on a target indicator: You must position the cat until an "X" prompt appears, then press a button to execute the jump. While intended to prevent missed leaps and tumbles, the system feels clunky and restrictive, turning simple traversal into a tedious chore. Though playing as a sleuthy little black cat was one of the many things that made 1666: Amsterdam so appealing to me, it quickly became my least favorite part of the game. A sight for sore players However, I'd be remiss not to mention my favorite part of 1666: Amsterdam, which is its sheer artistry. He says the fables, legends, and real stories about Musashi influenced a lot of the game’s take on the swordsman.  “There’s definitely a lot of influence from those real stories that went into creating the action [Musashi] uses in the game, both in terms of his reputation as a sword master and him being very skilled with multiple weapons,” Yugen says. Musashi won’t be utilizing his famous dual-wielding style as he’s still early in his journey – in his 20s – and hasn’t yet discovered/created it at this point of his life in Way of the Sword. “Where Musashi is set in this game is before he’s really mastered that, so there’s more focus on that one-sword action, but we did want to include elements of his familiarity with other weapons, which is why we have the Oni Armament skills that come into play.” During my time playing Way of the Sword, I used two different Oni Armaments. The Two Celestials are small blades that deal straight damage, while the Earth-Shaker hammers I later unlock in my playtime inflict heavy stagger damage. Equipping different Oni Armaments based on the enemies before you is a crucial part of Musashi’s combat kit. During a fight with the Nue, which consisted of two Chimera, mythical beasts inspired by various animals, using the Earth-Shaker hammers was key to my (eventual) victory against them. Yugen says stories about Musashi tell of how he used whatever means necessary to achieve victory, which is why his kit includes more than just a sword. You can parry enemies into torches, setting them on fire as a result, or flip tables to use as large shields – after you’re done using it as a shield, you can throw it into enemies, too, knocking them off balance. “These are all elements that were kind of inspired by the real stories of Musashi,” Yugen says. Some stories of Musashi, who is famous for an undefeated record of 62 sword duels and traveling around Japan specifically to reach that number, tell of an attitude somewhere between confident and cocky. That’s apparent in Way of the Sword’s Musashi, who is also searching for the next duel – in fact, he wants to get rid of the Oni Gauntlet on his arm because he feels like using it is cheating in these duels, a far cry from the likes of Samanosuke or Jubei, who want to use their Oni powers to save the world in Onimusha: Warlords and Onimusha 2: Samurai’s Destiny, respectively.  Yugen says he and the team stressed this confidence/cockiness to the motion capture artists Capcom hired to perform the actions of Musashi and his enemies. But, given how early in Musashi’s journey Way of the Sword takes place, they also took great care to make his action “a little rough around the edges” and “not quite as defined,” according to Yugen. One thing that helped was to imagine each fight in the various scenarios Musashi finds himself in as a Street Fighter bout. Leaning on one of Capcom’s other franchises, Yugen says the Street Fighter angle helped them define what Musashi would be thinking about as each fight begins, how he would walk up to enemies and approach them with sword in hand, and more. “It was towards the end of the project that we really started introducing those elements to flesh out the uniqueness of him, even down to the way he walks,” Yugen adds. “We didn’t want him to walk with this tension; he has a bit of swagger to him, and that’s something we really paid attention to in design.” Continuing, Yugen says, “Musashi doesn’t really hide his feelings.
