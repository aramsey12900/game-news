Metroidvania Still Feels Like A Niche Genre That Deserves BetterNEW
The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of th...
The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of the popular genre. This is Metroidvania Week. Within the vast and overcrowded Steam store, there are over 3,100 titles with the “metroidvania” tag. Last year, Hollow Knight: Silksong accomplished a rare feat for an indie game, crashing storefronts upon its launch. And later this year, one of the genre’s namesakes is making a comeback in the form of the 2D action game Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse. And yet, 40 years after the release of the original Metroid and Castlevania games, the genre influenced by those two beloved franchises still feels relatively obscure. The metroidvania or “search-action” genre has long felt like a middle child among the wider family of (arguably arbitrary) game classifications. For whatever reason, much of the gaming industry--particularly at the AAA level--seem hesitant to try their hands with entries to the metroidvania canon the same way they do with other budding genres. The "metroidvania" tag on Steam is packed Pick any time period in the history of games and you’ll recall moments when certain types of games were all the rage. From the cartoon-mascot platformers that plagued consoles in response to Mario and Sonic, to every publisher pushing out a “Halo killer,” and PUBG triggering an onslaught of battle royales, so many genres had their moment. The list goes on, particularly for subgenres spawning from specific games--your souls-likes, roguelikes, and such. Metroid and Castlevania each underwent early peaks and long periods of radio silence. After Super Metroid--which set the template for metroidvanias that came after--a long drought followed, with Fusion and the Prime games reviving the brand, and stinkers like Other M beginning another dark era until MercurySteam’s Samus Returns and Dread. Castlevania followed a similar path, with Symphony of the Night followed with several similarly designed handheld entries until the franchise, after the 3D Lords of Shadow titles, fell into oblivion for over a decade. That isn’t to say that the gaps during their respective hiatuses weren’t filled. The 2009 release of Shadow Complex served as a nice throwback to a then-abandoned genre, and a 2010s resurgence of metroidvanias occurred in indie scenes through games like Axiom Verge, Hollow Knight, Ori, Dead Cells, and many others. What the genre lacked was any AAA support, with big publishers seemingly unwilling to cash in on the interest--possibly because there wasn’t that much cash to be made, based on data. Despite their popularity, metroidvanias have never achieved big-budget success like God of War or Metal Gear An unfortunate characteristic that Metroid and Castlevania share is their lack of blockbuster sales success. The best-selling Metroid game is Dread with a little over 3 million copies sold, while Castlevania’s biggest seller was Lords of Shadow, which didn’t even break 2 million. They’re far cries from Nintendo’s Mario and Zelda or Konami’s Metal Gear in terms of mainstream commercial triumph. While mega-publishers and popular IP have toyed with souls-likes and roguelikes (mainstream IP like God of War, The Last of Us, and even Splatoon have dabbled in the latter), metroidvanias never experienced a similar zeitgeist. It’s hard to pinpoint why metroidvanias don’t have mass appeal. Perhaps it’s a lack of approachability, as these games can often be an unholy intersection of precision platforming and twitchy, unforgiving action gameplay. Maybe metroidvania maps generally being a large, restrictive macropuzzle for players to solve is a turn-off for casual players, as opposed to, say, open worlds with wider spaces to explore. Meanwhile, other budding genres like deck-building roguelikes and “bullet heaven” games like Vampire Survivors took off and contrasted with metroidvanias with their shorter dopamine-producing sessions.  The lack of broad appeal for metroidvanias could be beneficial for the genre and its fans. Modern gaming trends are often victim to oversaturation and dilution--think about every clone of Balatro and Vampire Survivors that has flooded marketplaces. On the Steam store, the roguelike/lite tag has nearly 23,000 related games. While it’s an indicator of popularity and demand, that many games could make it hard for developers to cut through the noise and for players to discover something worth their time and money. If a sizable percentage of new Steam releases were Hollow Knight clones, some of the metroidvania magic would be lost. Ultimately, the metroidvania style of game doesn’t have to have a stranglehold on video gaming to be considered successful. The least of genre fans’ worries should be validation from the larger industry--the main concern should just be having a healthy selection of titles to play. As a massive fan of search action, I wish that metroidvanias would occupy a space somewhere in the middle, where it has more mainstream attention without becoming a fad that gets overexposed and flames out. With more creators putting their own spin on the genre, growth and innovation would follow. I want to see a healthy scene, and one that isn’t overly dependent on the often-absent Nintendo and Konami, and entirely carried by indie developers with fewer resources and lower budgets. I want the metroidvania to have its moment, on its terms.

Metroidvania Games Were The Original Open-World GamesNEW
The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of th...
The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of the popular genre. This is Metroidvania Week. When many people pick up a controller to play a game, they're looking for one thing above all others: escapism. A break from the real world. There's no better way to do that than to immerse yourself in another world. Getting lost in a completely fictional world is true escapism. These days, open-world games like Cyberpunk 2077, Red Dead Redemption 2, and the most recent Legend of Zelda games do a lot of the work here, giving us maps full of icons to wade through. But the original escapist games came in the form of search-action games, colloquially known as metroidvania games by their fans. The term metroidvania has been around since the early 2000s, and mashes together the names of two games that established the tropes of the genre early on, Metroid and Castlevania. A classic metroidvania game drops you at the entrance to an environment that feels massive (especially in the opening hours) and gives you a pretty straightforward quest like "defeat Dracula"--and then throws countless roadblocks in your path on the way to that goal (locked doors, impassable areas, area bosses), forcing you to crisscross a growing map of different, visually distinct areas, often with their own enemies and thematically appropriate challenges. Animal Well, a densely packed metroidvania Those roadblocks are key to making the exploration feel like it matters. You can't go anywhere you like at the beginning; you'll need to unlock ways to open doors and new movement options to let you bypass hazards. When you eventually get through that barrier and into a new biome, that sense of wonder at seeing a new place can set in. Those roadblocks look a little different in open-world games; some games eschew these roadblocks entirely in favor of freedom, while others really only gate specific areas like dungeons and special challenges. But they're still doing the same thing as those metroidvania games--trying to give you reasons to explore and then revisit parts of the map, exploring the world in detail. At a basic level of mechanics and progression, these games are doing essentially the same thing: rewarding you for thorough exploration. It's easy to imagine a metroidvania in 3D--Dark Souls feels like one such example, with a map crisscrossed by shortcuts and riddled with secrets, and each area has a theme and a boss to match. And these themed areas are part of how a game ensures that it's memorable. Elden Ring has the Siofra river, an underground river with a sky full of dazzling stars. Control, a game full of gray blocks, has areas like the Black Rock Quarry and the Astral Plane. Many open-world games, especially those where you pick up quest-mandated weapons and earn new movement abilities, would function as a metroidvania without much change to its gameplay loops.  The Batman Arkham series blended open world and metroidvania progression All of these individual features come together to make a place where you as the player have a unique level of agency to navigate and interact with the game world. You can explore at your own pace, discovering the world one biome at a time, and as you pick up classic abilities like double-jumping and air-dashing that allow you to master the game world, you get that feedback loop of satisfaction: "The hard work I'm doing is paying off." As you acquire weapons and combat abilities, you choose which version of that character you want to be, what build. You have a clear goal and the agency to complete it how you like--something very few games can provide with similar density.  Only these two game genres offer this exact mix of personalization, approach, and achievement. That's why a genre that's been around for 30 years still holds such power for so many gamers.

Their home is in danger of being overrun by a deadly storm, and the only way to protect the community is to gather three reactor cassettes. The duo ventures out in their ship to explore abandoned and run-down space stations in hopes of finding the parts they need. The premise is decent enough to propel most of the game, which prioritizes gameplay over the overarching narrative.That focus shifts in the last half hour or so, however, dumping a ton of story content on the player all at once. Maki and Omura, on the other hand, are (inter)stellar leads. I was still able to finish The Sinking City 2 on the standard difficulty without feeling underpowered.The Sinking City 2 offers a decent take on a familiar formula if you’re itching for survival horror. Though I did find some fun within the madness, its shortcomings keep its potential from fully breaking the surface.  Score: 6.25 About Game Informer's review system
