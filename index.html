Catherine celebrated its 15-year anniversary on July 26, 2026. Below, we examine how this game was an early, if awkward, pioneer in trans representation in video games. When Catherine released in 2011 it was a step outside of the box for developer Atlus. The RPG powerhouse team wasn't as popular as it is today, and while it had built up a solid reputation from developing the Shin Megami Tensei and Persona series, Atlus had yet to quite break through into mainstream gaming. When the team decided to develop Catherine, it did so with the mindset of exploring more adult themes and identities. Chief among them was one of gaming’s few examples of trans representation. Though the intention of this character was well meaning, cultural divides caused a bit of controversy in the West. After the long development of Shin Megami Tensei: Persona 4, the team decided to branch out into a wildly unexpected direction for its next title. The project would reunite Persona series director and producer Katsura Hashino, character designer Shigenori Soejima, and composer Shoji Meguro, and take them into an entirely new genre of game that allowed for more mature storytelling. Vincent and Katherine Catherine tells the story of a man named Vincent. Vincent lives a happy life as a bachelor. He has a one-bedroom apartment, goes out drinking with his close friends nearly every night, and has a beautiful girlfriend named Katherine that he’s lagging on marrying. But his life gets upended when he meets the beautiful Catherine, a woman who is temptation made flesh. Soon after their encounter, he is haunted by nightmares every night in his sleep. The gameplay takes the form of controlling Vincent in his day-to-day life, making decisions in his relationships. This feels like a natural progression of Persona’s school and social systems. But the real bulk of the gameplay happens when Vincent goes to sleep and he’s besieged by nightmares that force him to climb an ever-falling tower of blocks in a puzzle-game fashion. It’s a great inverse of the Persona series--in that series, the gameplay ends at night, and in this one, that's when the bulk of the gameplay begins. Breaking away from the high school setting of the Persona series allowed the team to tackle more mature themes of infidelity, alcoholism, marriage, and adulthood. It also allowed the team to depict characters that you wouldn't typically see in a high-school-set game such as Persona. Vincent goes to the bar each night to drink with his friends and decompress after the nightmare from the previous night. This allows players to progress the story and get some opinions from his friends that they can use to influence their future choices, or just get drunk and listen to some good music on the in-game jukebox. Catherine One of the characters at the bar is a waitress named Erica. Featuring a very feminine design, she acts as the one female voice in the game that remains a neutral point to Vincent’s problems, often lending an ear to him and his friends in a flirty manner and providing a point of respite in an emotionally draining situation. Yet the game hints at her harboring a secret--something that was taboo in video games at the time. Erica is transgender. The game doesn’t go about this reveal in a tactful manner, though. Over the course of the game, Vincent’s friends begin having the same nightmares as he does. Erica chalks it up to a woman's wrath curse, but when she strikes up a romantic relationship with Vincent’s friend Toby, she begins to have the same nightmares: nightmares that only men have. In addition to this clue, there’s subtle hints dropped throughout the game, but the reveal doesn’t fully happen until the “Katherine True” ending of the game. In this ending of the game, Toby discovers Erica’s true past and the game even refers to her as her deadname (her former name). This portrayal is problematic for a myriad of reasons to Western culture, but is exemplified in the “Full Body” version of the game, which features a detransitioned Erica in flashbacks and in one of the endings of the game. While Catherine doesn’t attempt to portray Erica in a malicious manner, the cultural divide between Eastern and Western cultures and their acceptance of trans people is on full display. Japanese culture views gender and sexuality through a wide and varied spectrum that results in various forms of gender expression. For example, in the West when someone is transgender, it is socially expected of them to go through medical and physical transition in order to “pass” through society. Sure, drag subculture is a thing for non-trans people to gender-express, but this isn’t always tied to personal identity. One of Vincent's nightmares This is not the case in Japan, as gender expression often results in crossdressing communities or those who do not decide to medically or physically transition. To them, dressing and presenting as female will suffice. In fact, this could be a result of a law passed in 2003 that will allow a trans person to get a legal gender change only if they pass the hurdles of being unmarried, over the age of 22, undergo gender-reassignment surgery, and have no minor children. One cannot even have gender reassignment surgery if they’re on hormone replacement therapy. This results in Japan’s transgender subculture being more fluid. Perhaps that’s why Erica’s portrayal in Catherine relates so much to her past and not who she is in the moment. While she’s accepted by those around her, in-game it comes with a caveat. In Western culture, perhaps she would be portrayed as her true self with no regard to her past and only looking to the future. That’s what makes Catherine such a complex game. It’s a game that tackles adult themes and issues but never makes light of them. It’s a look into Japanese social culture and how it differs from the West. It’s a game that’s well meaning and never feels malicious despite arguably problematic elements, and it allowed Atlus to venture into uncharted territory.

Splatoon Raiders Owes A Lot To Older Campaigns You Forgot AboutNEW

Praise for something new can often come at the expense of what came before it. One aspect of art and media criticism that I find irksome is that to uplift new works, older ones from the same lineage are often undersold at best, or completely thrown under the bus at worst. It’s a trend I’ve seen most recently with Splatoon Raiders, the first spin-off title in one of my favorite Nintendo franchises. Splatoon Raiders may very well be the best single-player-focused experience in the series, but let’s not fool ourselves into thinking that the campaigns in Splatoons 1, 2, or 3 were significantly inferior, from either a gameplay or story aspect. In fact, they were pretty damn fresh. The wacky campaign of the first Splatoon isn't to be scoffed at. Conventional wisdom is that the older Splatoon campaigns are the least memorable aspect of their respective games--in short, they’re extended tutorials that are light on story, and Splatoon Raiders is the most successful story campaign in the series. Each of these points might be true to some degree, but that doesn’t stop the story modes of the original three games from being fun in their own respect. Splatoon’s single-player stories are short and sweet, infused with Nintendo’s signature charm. Despite their length, these campaigns greatly expand on the franchise’s world and are dripping with fascinating lore (as well as ink, of course). Many of the levels are amusing ways to learn about a range of Splatoon weapons, with clever level-specific gimmicks for players to wrap their heads around in a way that invokes other Nintendo games. And the structure of these story modes are always changing from game to game, experimenting with the very format of how players move between levels. Unpredictability is the format of Splatoon, and each of these older campaigns fed into the foundation that Splatoon Raiders is built on. The first Splatoon’s Octo Valley campaign did some heavy lifting when it came to fleshing out the Splatoon universe. Establishing Octarians as the antagonistic force and detailing the extensive history between them and the Inklings, with hidden Sunken Scroll collectibles describing an ancient war between the factions with colorful and comprehensive visuals. These scrolls even go the extra mile to answer the sickest questions from players about Inkling evolution and biology. The levels themselves were like quick bites, carrying the same spirit that other Nintendo titles like Super Mario Galaxy and 3D World have, centering entire levels on mechanics like inkrails and propeller-powered platforms. In short, variety is of no short supply in classic Splatoon campaigns. And through music, Splatoon campaigns differentiated themselves with energetic, quirky, and straight-up disorienting tunes that reflect the contrasts between Inkling and Octarian culture. Splatoon 2's Octo Expansion took the series to new heights in narrative and progression. Splatoon 2’s Octo Canyon was a solid continuation, keeping the same level structure as Octo Valley while gradually giving players more weapon options to experiment with, letting them take different approaches to the same levels. While the story has an inherently similar premise to the first game, it adds a novel narrative layer by taking the results of the final Splatfest of Splatoon 1 into account, meaning that players’ actions from the previous game actually affected this story. The Octo Expansion DLC took Splatoon story modes to a completely new height, shifting to a challenge-room-based structure and allowing players to tackle levels in different orders based on a subway map. A favorite of Splatoon fans, Octo Expansion might be the most influential campaign within the series. And as a bonus, it contains one of the hardest gaming bosses of all time--Inner Agent 3--who I think deserves to be named alongside any of the grueling opponents seen in a From Software game. Splatoon 3’s Return of the Mammalians begins with what seems to be a retread of Octo Valley and Octo Canyon, even throwing the final boss from the first two games at you very early on. But it then switches to a level structure similar to Octo Expansion, with non-linear progression and a ton of unique levels testing your skills with the multiplayer’s special abilities. And Splatoon 3’s Side Order DLC successfully took elements from Octo Expansion, throwing players into intricate challenge rooms and allowing for numerous upgrade and loadout possibilities. What resulted was a uniquely Nintendo roguelite, and a very strong entry in the larger genre. Splatoon Raiders has hints of all of the series's single-player campaigns before it. Although these campaigns weren’t the main focus for their respective games, they were far from throwaways, and Raiders draws from all of these games in one way or another. On the surface, I’ve noticed the focus of lore and history disseminated through hidden scrolls coming from Splatoon 1, while Raiders allows for the weapon versatility and experimentation seen in Splatoon 2. Octo Expansion’s non-linear nature of level progression echoes in Raiders, while Splatoon 3’s ability tutorials show through Raiders’ Sampler Dungeons. Even the spec-building systems in Raiders certainly resemble the permanent upgrade system seen in Side Order. You can view Splatoon Raiders as a culmination of ideas from the franchise’s decade-plus history: a refinement of the elements from campaigns past into a solid standalone experience. But that isn’t to diminish any titles in the series compared to the others, because they all brought something of their own. Heck, Raiders is my least favorite one, since PvP is why I go to Splatoon--but it’s still a damn good game.  Everyone will have their preference and favor one over the rest, but if there’s one takeaway from Raiders, it’s not that what preceded it was worse, but that Splatoon as a whole is something truly special.

Halo: Combat Evolved is 25 years old now, so it's a marvel that playing its most recent remake, Halo: Campaign Evolved, is still as fun as it is. Everything from the weight of the weaponry, the ambiance of the Halo installation, the screams of fleeing grunts, and the grin-inducing sound of a plasma grenade exploding on an unfortunate target are all here to remind you (or introduce you) to the most important franchise in Xbox history. It's a series that has struggled in recent times, and it's easy to see this reminder of Halo's roots as critical to its future. So why do I keep wanting to go back to its flawed, latest entry instead? Halo Infinite was always going to miss the mark in some way. It was a victim of troubled development, responsible for selling a console that struggled out of the gate, and tasked with being both an end to the tired tale from previous entries as well as the start of a wholly new one. Infinite was pitched as Halo's future; a platform that could be built upon for years to come, telling an evolving tale as new chapters were added to it over a decade or more. None of that came to pass. Halo Infinite did a lot of things, but it wasn't enough to pull the series out of the ditch it found itself in. So instead of committing to it long-term, 343 Industries reformed into Halo Studios, shut down its in-house Slipspace engine in favor of the ubiquitous Unreal Engine 5, and did what was once unthinkable: It brought Master Chief to PlayStation. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7LimYD3Ycwo Yet none of that should overshadow just how much Halo Infinite did to drive forward the fundamental gameplay loop that made its original so captivating in 2001. Halo: Combat Evolved has an incredibly strong opening, thrusting you into the chaos of its intergalactic war and wasting no time introducing you to its striking setting on the surface of the Halo installation. Having wide-open areas to explore as enemies drop in from the horizon was as novel then as it is today. It's a little easier to pick apart the loop--drive to a spot in the world, survive a few waves of enemies, move on and do it again--especially in those opening sections. But it's a rhythm that Combat Evolved, and by extension Campaign Evolved, uses to great effect across its brief adventure, at least by today's standards. Halo Infinite took this idea, one that had been mostly abandoned since Halo 3, and completely modernised it. You're on yet another installation, but this time the map isn't just wide--it's open. Enemies are littered around its landscapes, occupying fortified bases for you to break into, suppressing small squads of lost UNSC soldiers, and patrolling the valleys with vehicles just waiting to be hijacked. You were finally given the freedom to explore and engage with the world in a way that was only simulated in previous games, letting you approach skirmishes in the ways that you saw fit with new weapons and tools that encouraged creativity.  The hookshot, Infinite's version of a wrist-mounted grappling hook, was smartly given to you from the start. It's Infinite's single best addition to its combat loop, letting you move in a way that made Master Chief an even greater force to be reckoned with. Reeling yourself into enemies to deliver that crunching, final melee blow, pulling explosives towards you so that you could fling them into a group of unsuspecting enemies, and throwing yourself around corners and into the air for a surprise attack are just a few examples of how this tool added new layers to the two-decades-old formula that had strayed too far from its roots. It stripped things back to the fundamentals and accentuated them rather than trying to replace them with gimmicky, hero-like abilities that felt at odds with the shooter they were attempting to integrate with. Halo: Campaign Evolved might be looking more behind instead of ahead Halo Infinite was not without its flaws. Its opening is drawn out and takes too long to unleash you into its open world. The larger, explorable space comes at the expense of the varied locales previous games allowed you to hop between, begging for the variety that chapters like The Silent Cartographer, set against sandy beaches and towering cliff faces, are so fondly remembered for. Its story is almost incomprehensible if you hadn't kept up with the narrative beats conveyed through other expanded-universe Halo media that takes place after the events of Halo 5, particularly regarding what happened to Cortana. It muddies its many gameplay enhancements behind a game that was disinterested with new fans, despite its requirement to act as a platform for its immediate future. It's easy to see why, then, that Halo Studios saw fit to start over--to remind people why Halo was important, what its core fundamentals are, and reestablish a new future. But it would be a massive shame if this comes at the expense of the first real refinement that this original formula received just a handful of years ago. Halo Infinite is flawed, but it's also the most interesting campaign the series has delivered since the culmination of the Master Chief's initial story under the stewardship of Bungie. And if Halo Studios seeks to reclaim some of the glory of those days, it needs to take lessons from both its recent and distant past.

You can gesture with your arms, such as lifting them up in the sky, pointing, or raising them out to the side. Sitting plops you down in place, or sends you sliding down a hill. There's jumping, crouching, and even picking up items and kicking them like a field goal. Movement and interaction is delightfully tactile and involved, as you can talk with your hands and express thoughts with your in-game avatar body, literally pointing at things, waving, and doing the Rocky pose when you conquer a puzzle.Distance reduces your voice volume, so walkie-talkies, radios, and even whiteboards became methods for long-distance communication for our group. A flashlight might be useless during the day, but at night you need one to navigate the darkness. Since you can initially only hold one thing in your hands at a time, equipment comes at a premium, so you make interesting decisions about what to take and leave behind. The limitations do prevent, however, carrying and using some of the more interesting and fun tools; the whiteboard is a really clever nonverbal tool to mess around with, for instance, but not as "effective" as a walkie-talkie or radio, which are abundant on the island. But that doesn't mean I didn't love writing "Kick Me" on a whiteboard and sticking it in my friend's backpack as we explored.Going anywhere takes time; not a gameplay resource or number on a meter, but just real-life time. This naturally leads to downtime which facilitates some of Big Walk's best moments. Stumbling across a new puzzle room or gadget leads to hijinks. We argued over who got to hold the megaphone we'd just found, or go fumbling in the dark trying to find where we dropped a key item. If cartoon birds could ever re-enact Stand By Me in the Australian wilderness, it'd look a lot like Big Walk.A north-star objective underpins all of this: small red objects, which look a little like those rubber Kong toys you fill with peanut butter for dogs, which we took to calling "gourds." Usually, to solve these, we'd need to figure out the solution to an ambient puzzle, which had a delightful range including blindfolded obstacle courses, pitching machines, egg timers, mazes, lights and pictures, and many, many wooden blocks with symbols to hold up and nod or shake our heads at. Big Walk loves to play with means of communication in these rooms too, and its best puzzles take full advantage of your buy-in to its requirements. Soundproof rooms forced our team to figure out how to communicate nonverbally. Shorthands evolved around specific puzzle pieces. Shouting "turn 10 degrees to your left and scooch forward" to my pal, as they're wearing a giant helmet that muffles their voice and blocks their vision, was genuinely fun. These gourd puzzles feel like summer camp activities in the best ways. Gourds can be placed in specific locations and, once enough have been placed, they open a secondary puzzle, which then unlocks a new feature on the island. I'm being intentionally vague because, after the initial tutorial, they become Big Walk's big reveals. Each one introduces a new feature that recontextualizes a piece of the island, which is impactful and exciting.If anything, that's where Big Walk starts to slow its pace a bit. As we focused on unlocking more of the island, completing more puzzles, and figuring out what lay at the end of it all, the "walk" of it fell to the background. Walking itself is sometimes a bit too finicky; jumping up terrain and ledges, in particular, can be frustrating to manage. The expanse of the island closed in a bit, even as we uncovered more and more secrets, and found a larger post-credits puzzle chase meant for those who want more. I wanted more silly opportunities to play and find goofy levity in this large experience.In the nine hours it took my crew of three to see the end of Big Walk, though, it felt like we had made a real journey together. We laughed a lot and came up with many absurd puzzle solutions that still somehow worked. Big Walk's excellent finale effectively brought the message home: more than anything, this was our shared walk.Sharing that journey is the point of Big Walk. It asks you to isolate, to put yourself in a siloed-off game station on proximity chat, so you can experience what it feels like to journey together in its virtual world. It provides tools for you to venture apart, to go vast distances, and still reconnect to gather your gourds and unlock more of the island. House House lays out all the ways your group could go, and lets you loose. Look for an ending, seek out puzzles, search for mysteries or climb for adventure; no matter what, you're all going on a memorable walk together, and that's a pretty good way to spend a few evenings together with friends.
