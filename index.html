The Incident At Galley House Review – A Clever Blast From The PastNEW
Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PC Publisher: Evil Trout Developer: William Rous, Evil Trout Release: July 14, 2026 A big part of me wants to forgo explaining what makes The Incident of Galley House great for fear of revealing too much; to just say “trust me, play it. It’s good.” Unraveling the truth behind the unsolved deaths of a group of people gathering at a mansion one stormy night in 1936 is engrossing and inventive, with a tale that keeps you guessing until its final moments. Although I didn’t get all of the answers I wanted, I was immensely impressed with the revelations I did uncover and the journey to find them.  The Incident of Galley House is a reimagined take on the 2025 horror text game, Type Help, by William Rous that has been remastered and expanded by Evil Trout, creators of The Roottrees Are Dead. Players control a researcher who visits the condemned Galley House where multiple dead bodies were discovered decades prior; the causes of their deaths are unknown. Using a machine capable of reconstructing scenes from the past to watch in the present, you piece together the conversations, actions, and whereabouts of each person during the ill-fated hours leading to their demise. The present-day premise was fascinating enough to draw me in, but the events of the past hooked me. A man is invited to Galley House by someone named Rupert Galley, but none of the residence’s many guests have heard of this supposed host. This begs many questions: Who is Rupert Galley, and why did he invite this man? Why have these visitors gathered this night? And, most importantly, why and how is everyone dying? Like The Roottrees are Dead, The Incident at Galley House quickly becomes a fun, all-consuming exercise of gleaning context clues from conversations and physical evidence to uncover new information that gradually reveals the full events of a 26-segment timeline. Doing so requires engaging with an inventive system of correctly entering numerical codes that indicate the location, time, and people (who are only identified by a number until you discover their names) to find scenes matching those criteria. The experience of deducing who did what and where reminds me of Clue; I got a kick out of figuring out if, for example, two characters were in the living room during a specific time based on information I learned from a different, chronologically later scene I happened to unlock first.I appreciate how much flexibility The Incident at Galley House allows for filling out the timeline. You can follow each character’s night one at a time, focus only on reaching the end of the timeline by advancing numerous character threads (and backfill missing scenes later), or do your best to learn every character's whereabouts chronologically before moving too far ahead. I enjoy the agency in how to approach the investigation, and the game is constructed to allow multiple paths to solving its problems. The story is an immaculately well-written web of clues and references that rewards you for paying attention to not only what’s being said (both at face value and the subtext) but also the visual elements of a scene and the layout of Galley House's many areas, which in themselves must first be discovered. Revisiting a curious line of dialogue with new perspectives on the person or situation to find information hidden in plain sight never stopped feeling satisfying or, in some cases, mind-blowing. Since you follow 26 scenes for each of the game’s dozen or so characters, it’s easy to become overwhelmed by the amount of dialogue and information you have to keep track of. Thankfully, The Incident at Galley House offers many helpful tools to make sense of it all. The notepad from The Rootrees are Dead returns, letting you take in-game notes and save quotes directly from dialogue transcripts. Scenes can be paused so you can re-read and highlight key text before resuming them. A search engine surfaces conversations based on keywords, and you can even place markers on the timeline to indicate events, such as a character death, at a glance. The Incident of Galley House also communicates how many locked cinematics remain discoverable in each scene, which was one of my favorite time-saving features from The Rootrees are Dead. Though I didn’t need to use every tool often, if at all, I was happy to have them.You don’t need to find every scene to solve the mystery, but I was so fascinated by not only the overarching story, but also the smaller questions surrounding Galley House’s cast that I obsessively played until I was sure I’d seen everything. The voice performances are generally good, save for some weirdly muted reactions whenever someone learned of another’s death that sometimes took me out of the moment. I enjoyed getting to know Galley Houses’ colorful visitors and grew to like some of them enough that I felt a growing existential dread knowing they wouldn’t make it out of the house alive. Speaking of dread, the game’s eerie vibes and presentation add a welcome layer of creepiness to the already dark subject matter – and the game taps into its unsettling atmosphere for some effective hair-raising moments.The Incident at Galley House constantly made me reevaluate my theories and approach, and several big twists spurred my search for answers throughout many late-night sessions. Sadly, I found the conclusion to be a mixed bag. The story purposely withholds the biggest explanation I’d been searching for while still offering enough significant ancillary explanations to avoid falling completely flat. It’s an artistic choice that may resonate with some, but after so many hours of searching and concocting numerous wild theories, I wanted a more concrete answer than what the game provides. Still, I had a blast figuring out the fates of these doomed souls, and I’d wager any puzzle or adventure game fan will too.   Score: 8.75 About Game Informer's review system

Splatoon Raiders Review - A Fresh TakeNEW
Reviewed on: Switch 2 ...
Reviewed on: Switch 2 Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Nintendo Release: July 23, 2026 Though known primarily for its competitive multiplayer gameplay, Splatoon’s single-player content has been engaging and creative since the 2015 debut entry. This side of the series offers obstacle courses, shooting galleries, puzzles, and boss battles with continually novel gimmicks. Splatoon Raiders amps up the combat part of that equation, delivering a multitude of intense encounters. The result is a more focused PvE, looter-shooter-inspired Splatoon game that sunk its hooks into me until long after the credits rolled. Even though the competitive multiplayer element is absent from this package, Splatoon Raiders maintains the vibrant visuals and excellent gameplay feel that popularized the mainline titles. Gliding through a colorfully inked surface while you reload remains smooth and responsive, and the weapons and abilities – of which there are a ton – allow you to blast your way through each bite-sized encounter however you’d like. Though I struggled to find the same balance of weapon utility I typically discover in the multiplayer titles, I loved experimenting with how different character loadouts interact with the various challenges Splatoon Raiders threw my way. Tinkering with my build quickly became a favorite activity between missions. Splatoon Raiders gives you upgradeable tanks, which serve as character classes. The Speed tank offers mobility options and ranged attacks, while the Power tank grants abilities centered on aggression. My personal favorite, the Tactical tank, lets me place turrets, ink vortexes, and trip-mines to help me even the odds. Each of these ability gadgets is upgradeable and customizable, and when you combine the vast number of modifications with the huge arsenal of weapons, not to mention the three selectable Ultimate abilities, I’m still tweaking my loadouts even hours after rolling credits. Thankfully, a rewarding post-game campaign ensures Raiders will remain in my rotation for the next several weeks.The story is a silly and forgettable treasure-hunting tale, but it serves as an appropriate catalyst for the quick-hit raids you take on in rapid succession. Whether in single-player or cooperative play, you embark on missions involving multiple levels of timed battles, linear stages full of baddies, or open areas built for free-form traversal; though I appreciate the assortment of map layouts, by the end of the campaign, the repetition began to set in, both with the mission types and the disappointing soundtrack. But whether you’re talking Power Eggs to charge your Ultimate meter, enemy parts for crafting, Spirhalite Shards for currency, or new weapons and gadget parts to customize your loadout, you are constantly gathering items to boost your chances at success. Crucially, the item collection process is smooth and doesn’t disrupt the intuitive weapon-use/reload-swim rhythm Splatoon has always delivered. And that’s a good thing, because the intense battles against the wide array of combatant creatures keep you on your toes. Whether you’re blasting Smallfry and Chum minions or one of the more than 10 unique Salmonid types that pop up, each raid combines similar ingredients to deliver unique experiences. And since each powered-up Salmonid poses unique threats and possesses distinct weaknesses, the enemy spawns help alleviate some of the mission-type fatigue. Though I miss the more puzzle- and platforming-based excursions of other Splatoon PvE content (here, those are mostly relegated to side-mission tutorials involving specific gadgets), I can’t deny the tightening of my grip and leaning in my posture each time the Salmonids flooded the map and forced me to adjust my approach on the fly. That’s even more true for the more powerful variant enemies and the impressive set-piece boss battles. Splatoon 3’s bosses lacked the originality of other Splatoon PvE content, but Splatoon Raiders proves that was merely a minor hiccup. If your firepower doesn’t prove to be enough to take on the Salmonids, you can invite other players to help your cause. Testing the multiplayer features on pre-release servers, I had a smooth experience partying up and taking on Splatoon Raiders’ missions alongside other players. The levels all feel appropriately designed and scaled for single-player play, but if you need any extra help, you can also send out a beacon for a random player to join your session for one mission, with special loot awarded upon completion.Splatoon Raiders is the kind of game I struggle to put down. Most raids only take minutes to complete, and the steady feeling of upgrading your abilities constantly tempted me to play “just one more” to test out the latest tweaks I made. Though Splatoon’s single-player content has always been underappreciated, Splatoon Raiders makes it difficult to ignore, shining a bright light on the series’ oft-slept-on side.
