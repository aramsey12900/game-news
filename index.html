Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Sega Developer: Ryu Ga Gotoku Studios Release: January 15, 2027 Stranger Than Heaven is one of the early 2027 releases we're most looking forward to, thanks in large part to it being an ambitious title that tells a prequel story following the same man over the course of 50 years. Players control Makoto Daito, a half-American half-Japanese man stuck between two cultures, struggling to fully fit in with either. In 1915, he stows away on a San Francisco ship bound for Japan in an attempt to escape the prejudice he experienced in the United States as an Asian man. However, once he arrives in Japan, he doesn't quite find the culture of acceptance he expected. As he wanders the streets, pulling off jobs for Orpheus (portrayed by rapper Snoop Dogg), I hear the folks Kokura, where Makoto landed in Japan, jeering him behind his back. It's only one local, Tae Matsumoto, who seems to see Makoto for who he is. Makoto goes to the bar where Tae works, in an attempt to undermine a rival gang, but once he spends an evening with her, he confesses the entire thing before asking Tae out on a real date. Unfortunately for Makoto, Tae is the mistress of the Oki Clan, and when Oki finds out that Makoto is spending time with his mistress, he puts a hit on Makoto and steals Tae away. Makoto leads a siege against the ironworks mill where Oki has stolen Tae, resulting in the most climactic clash of my three hours with Stranger Than Heaven: a battle against the hulking Shimura. There, I really put my mastery of the unique combat system, which corresponds the shoulder buttons to your arms and the triggers to your legs for a more direct control of the combos you throw. Since Shimura uses a huge melee weapon in his second phase, I also used my weapon of choice: a knife. Using the nimbleness afforded by a lightweight weapon, I dodged his advances and finished him off to save Tae. But Kokura in 1915 is hardly the only setting of the game, as players will follow Makoto's exploits across five decades, visiting Kure in 1919, Osaka in 1943, Atami in 1951, and, finally, Shinjuku in 1965. I had a chance to free-roam explore both Kure and Osaka. I experienced the fighting arena of Kure, where you challenge increasingly difficult opponents (and can re-challenge defeated foes with special win conditions), and stumbled upon a masked serial killer who gave me the toughest fight of my life. I also found a composer, who can help craft songs based on sounds from which Makoto pulls inspiration; during my playthrough, Makoto found inspiration in everything from a crying baby in a house he walked by to the clang of his metallic melee weapon cracking against a thug's skull. In the Osaka sequence, the attitude toward Makoto has shifted dramatically; men and women alike run up to and greet him with bright smiles due to his work in organized crime and music showbiz. To that end, I use this gameplay section to live in that world. After negotiating with local business owners to buy ad space on their billboards, I have Makoto put on a show at a local theater. Here, I can choose the program, performers, and then take part in an interactive performance.I left my hands-on session extremely impressed by the many layers present in Stranger Than Heaven. It was something more of we always wondered while making Yakuza and Like a Dragon games how would someone create such an organization, like a yakuza organization, and that could be an interesting narrative to focus on. In the Yakuza franchise, we have the Tojo Clan or the Omi Alliance, but we never actually told why or who would create such a thing. So, the starting point was from there, and then, as we were developing the narrative, we added all sorts of elements.One of the greatest ideas for the game is making the main character of mixed heritage of Japanese and American. I think that decision really opened up a whole new narrative to tell for the studio. So, from there, we did want to tell the beginning of such an organization, but it wasn't like we wanted to tell a prologue of the Yakuza or Like a Dragon series. We already had the mind of, like, "Okay, we want to make this as something brand new and like nothing that we have made before." And with the theme of biculturalism relating to Makoto Daito, why was telling the story of existing in that "in between" space where maybe you don't feel like you belong in either culture such an important story to tell with this game?MA: At the beginning, it wasn't a story about mixed heritage; it was just one Japanese guy who's trying to create an organization. But we wanted to make sure that the character has a strong motivation for creating such an organization, and we felt like it would be a great narrative to tell if we made the main character as a mixed heritage of both Japanese and American. That really gave us the creative freedom to add an American character and do a multilingual setting where American characters speak in English and Japanese characters speak in Japanese, which is such an interesting thing. And I don't think I've ever seen that in a video game, so it really opened up a lot of creative ideas from there. I feel like those changes of identity really affects how people react to his title, and we carefully created how that affects Makoto as gameplay. But not just gameplay, but with all the cutscenes, we try to make sure the player has the sense of how the people are looking at Makoto. So, when we have all the people walking by, you can tell Makoto is seen as someone who's unwanted there, or maybe in another scene, you can see that no one's really given too much care to him, so all the background acting for the cutscenes was really crafted based on how Makoto's identities are at the time. I really feel like the player can experience the environment of Makoto through both the gameplay and the narrative.YS: Again, I see that it overlaps with my personal experiences. Daito, at first, he's very young. He's scared. He doesn't know what to do exactly, but he's sort of forced to assimilate. As you progress through the story, each era takes place in a different Japanese city. Why were these specific eras tied to these particular cities?MA: Those cities were chosen based purely on the narrative that we wanted to depict. Again, the beginning was that we want to depict why someone would create such an organization, and from there, we started to realize that we'll need a lot of time to tell this narrative. In our previous games, like Yakuza or Like a Dragon, the narrative only revolves around a week, maybe, or somewhere around there. But you wouldn't be able to tell a narrative of someone creating such a huge organization within a week or a day. Then, we settled with, "Okay, it would be cool if we could depict a man's life journey on creating this place that he wants to call home." That was the starting point of how we decided we want to depict a man's life, and we already had in mind that we could start the narrative from 1915 Kokura, because back in the day, Kokura was a huge industrial city, and it was really natural for someone to end up in Kokura [after] smuggling onto a ship. The starting point of the narrative was already fixed and, for spoiler reasons, I can't talk too much, but the ending point was 1965 Shinjuku from the very beginning. From there, we had two dots, and now we selected the era and the city based off of how interesting it would be to put Makoto in a situation that those eras and cities could offer. For example, 1929 Kure; Kure is a famous city within Japan for being the city that was used in the famous yakuza movie called Battle Without Honor and Humanity, and it was a great fit for if we wanted to tell a story about the yakuza. We thought it would be really interesting if we could put Makoto in that situation in Kure because, again, he has this identity crisis of whether he wants to be Japanese or American. Yakuza is such a Japanese organization, so we thought it would be really interesting to put Makoto in that city. Also, because it's 1929 Kure, back in the day, there were a lot of foreign people within the city because it was such a big military port. That already makes it have this build up of telling a great narrative of someone who thinks they're Japanese and they're in the yakuza organization, and yet he still has this foreigner side of himself, and he has a lot of foreigners within the city to connect with.How much work did it add to the team's plates to depict not only different cities, but different eras?MA: You can't imagine how hard it was! [laughs] It was hard! We did five cities in the past with Yakuza 5, but this time, because we weren't able to reuse any assets between the cities, and also because no one knows what it was like back in 1915... We had to do a lot of research, and there was some stuff that I, as a Japanese, didn't even know existed! There were times when we were just running around old bookshops and looking for a magazine that was popular in those days. We did a lot of research based off of those, but the goal was never to recreate Japanese history, so we didn't have this historical accuracy concept from the very beginning. So, we took a lot of inspiration from what we have researched, and we wanted to convert that into what we wanted to depict as a game and [gamify] it, so it's in this range of entertaining people. That research took a lot of time, and we did go to all the cities and looked up really old photos and took that research, and had this creative freedom on changing it into something that we wanted to depict in the game. As you go through these eras between 1915 and 1965, you're landing in some eras that were not only quite dramatically different from one another, but some were also quite tumultuous for Japan. How do you go about addressing those global and national events within the story?MA: So, we do touch some of those historical events that are happened. For example, 1943 Osaka. The setting itself is during wartimes. However, the narrative really leans toward Makoto Daito's life, and so there's not this crucial depiction of those historical events, and that gave us a lot of creative freedom to focus on Daito's life. The events are happening, right? We can't ignore that. But the big events – for example, having a war – it does affect the live of who is living in that city, but they have their own lives, and I think it's worth telling that life more than what history was actually like. And so, the main goal was the really focus on Makoto Daito's life in that situation.Music plays a huge role in Stranger Than Heaven, but what other pieces of art inspired you and your team that pushed the direction of the game?MA: I studied a lot of film, and I had a lot of interest in movies. Growing up, I always wanted to make movies, and I even went to film school. The knowledge I acquired there was, I did watch a lot of classic movies, and I also studied a lot of film media and camera lenses. And because I had this background, I took a lot of inspiration from how it was created back then. We did a lot of research on these vintage lenses that were used back then, or this film media that was used back then. It wasn't particularly this movie, but it was more the technique they used back then,  for something that was really hard. Let's say for example, putting text on a video; these days, where you just put it in the editing software, but back in the day, they had this, like, glass, and then you wrote on it, and then you had to print it on a film. I took a lot of inspiration from them, as well as ninkyo movies, which is a Japanese term for a yakuza-themed entertainment, and of course, Bunta Sugawara, who is in our game, is a legendary actor from back in those eras, so we took some inspiration from there, as well. Reviewed on: Switch 2 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: Square Enix Developer: Square Enix Release: October 22, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ RPG publisher Square Enix has been doing the Lord's work lately by rescuing their discontinued mobile games and rehydrating them into full-fat RPG experiences. Last year's Octopath Traveler 0 is one such triumph, and it's safe to say Final Fantasy Resonance is another victory for reusing assets instead of throwing them in the garbage. More importantly, it's a great RPG experience with a fun cast and character customization options that will make fans of the Final Fantasy V job system rabid with delight. Resonance is based on the first season of the now-defunct Final Fantasy Brave Exvius mobile game, which ran four chapters in total from 2016 to 2025, but Resonance is no mere port. For starters, it received an HD-2D injection that gussied up Exvius's already-impressive sprites. Resonance wears the HD-2D aesthetic confidently; Square Enix has obviously listened to past criticisms of its beloved graphical style, and it paid off. Resonance is an HD-2D game, but its highly detailed character sprites and occasional jaw-dropping pixel-based "cinema" scene differentiate it from the Octopath Traveler series and the Dragon Quest HD-2D remakes.  Resonance's distinctions are its greatest strength, in fact. It's easy to assume at first blush that its story about a boy and his best friend is nothing but a table to prop retro Final Fantasy references upon. It's true that a key part of Resonance's gameplay involves summoning "Visions" into battle, said visions being projections of characters from other Final Fantasy games. But that's all they are: Projections. They teach transferable skills, à la Final Fantasy V's job system. They can offer characters permanent stat boosts as they level up, like Final Fantasy VI's espers. And when conditions are met, they can be summoned to perform a super attack via a CGI spectacle that will remind you Square Enix have been masters of game cinema direction since day one.Each Resonance character can equip a Vision that lends them their powers in a fight, and also grants passive upgrades and skills. Rain, for example, is a heavy hitter with innate fire attacks, but if he wears Cecil Harvey as a backpack, he also gets access to light and dark magic. Cecil gets stronger as they fight together, and bestows skills that can be equipped as wanted. If you're a Final Fantasy V job-head, you've come home.But Final Fantasy Resonance is Rain's story despite its many callbacks to past games. Yes, Rain is an amalgamation of past Final Fantasy heroes, from Cloud's spiky yellow hair to Tidus's daddy issues, to Zidane's sunny attitude (with a hint of shadow), but that just makes him easier to root for. Rain's roommate, Lasswell, contains notes of my beloved Kain Highwind from Final Fantasy IV, but there's a troubled nobility to him that's purely his own. They're gradually joined by a cast of characters who click with each other (and thus, with me) thanks in part to comedic side-discussions delivered with quality voice acting. References to past Final Fantasy games are doled with clever subtlety. As a Final Fantasy XIV fan, I had to peel myself off the ceiling more than once when I encountered some choice shoutouts. Resonance's battle system grants it another distinction. It's a classic turn-based system that shows off who's next in line to attack, like Final Fantasy X. Every character brings a fighting style and elemental flavor to the party. Lasswell is a samurai who favors ice, Jake is a gunner attuned to the wind, and Lid will rain the fattest of steel chocobos upon your head in an earth-based attack.Resonance is a snappy game, which might be its most welcome trait. Fast travel is generous, and dungeons are conquered in just the right amount of time. You're looking at a 40-hour experience, give or take, which is downright digestible for an RPG in this day and age. On the flipside, Rain's world lacks depth. Its towns are beautiful, unique, and filled with characters and quest-givers who are worth talking to, but the overworld connective tissue is thin and its dungeons lack pizzazz. An auto-battle option would lessen the frustration of random encounters, but said encounters don't pop up nearly as often as they do in ancestral Final Fantasy games. Plus, the random battles are spaced in a way that makes grinding unnecessary unless you're maxing out a Vision, which I appreciate, but sometimes you're just trying to get to a place.I went into Resonance expecting to like it for its salute to Final Fantasy characters across the decades, but I wound up loving the game for showing me a whole new canon. In a time when mainline Final Fantasy is still figuring itself out, Final Fantasy Resonance is easy to recommend. It has one foot planted on the solid foundation of Final Fantasy's history, but with its rich graphics and likeable cast of characters it builds up from that history instead of merely pillaging it.  Score: 9 About Game Informer's review system
