Silent Hill: Townfall Review - Addressing The TraumaNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: Konami, Annapurna Interactive Developer: Screen Burn Release: September 24, 2026 Rating: Mature Much like the franchise itself, for years Silent Hill existed as an opaque figure embedded in fog, with only crackling radio static confirming its existence. Konami wasn’t sure what to do with it beyond Pyramid Head cameos in kart racers and Bomberman party games. But, like a repressed memory that suddenly manifests as a monster compelling you to address your unresolved trauma, Silent Hill is back, and Townfall, the third Silent Hill game in as many years, continues the impressive return-to-form streak. Simon Ordell’s journey through the Scottish town of St. Amelia in 1996 is not without its shortcomings, but the experience is an affecting one buoyed by the first-person perspective. Silent Hill has dabbled in first-person before with Silent Hill 4: The Room, 2024's experimental The Short Message, and the whole P.T. debacle. We also, of course, all definitely played and remember the on-rails shooter, Silent Hill: The Arcade. Townfall, however, is the first time the franchise has fully embraced the perspective. The view makes the game unique within the Silent Hill oeuvre. Through Simon’s eyes, St. Amelia is overbearing and imposing in all the intended ways. That the game looks great also helps. The claustrophobic interiors are full of details that reward inspection, and you can practically taste the fog while exploring outdoors. The peeking mechanic is also expertly integrated and makes it feel like you truly are cowering and exposing as little of yourself as possible while staying quiet and keeping an eye on monsters.The broad story, at least structurally, will be familiar to Silent Hill players, which I offer as a compliment. Townfall may take place in St. Amelia, but there is no question that this is a Silent Hill game. Simon and others are confronting their inner demons in a foggy town, and what exactly is happening or why the streets are littered with monsters is never truly clear. You do know, however, that something is off and the only way out is through. Uncharacteristically, compared to past Silent Hills, the individual plots that have led to the emotional distress of Townfall’s cast are more transparent. There are fewer questions about why characters feel what they’re feeling. It eliminates some of the dreamlike qualities that make these games ethereal and mysterious, but I am not opposed to more clarity, as sometimes information is withheld unnecessarily. I like the narrative balance Townfall strikes.Simon has an era-appropriate portable television that lets him track monsters through the screen’s static, and it does a good job of letting you monitor creatures’ whereabouts while maintaining intensity. Combat is heavily discouraged, with perhaps the fewest number of bullets in any Silent Hill game (that feature guns, anyway) and melee weapons that expire quickly. As a result, Townfall is a stealth game, but I struggled with understanding what alerted enemies to my presence. Sometimes crouching and moving slowly behind cover was enough. Sometimes enemies saw me anyway. The inconsistency made me reload saves often, which diminished the mood of the encounters. I got particularly frustrated with one invincible, instantly-kill-you-if-caught monster who never seemed to walk away no matter how long I tracked him on my TV while hiding in a closet. In these moments, I was bored and annoyed instead of scared.What I have enjoyed so much about Konami’s renewed efforts with Silent Hill is how it is finding developers like Screen Burn Interactive that understand what makes Silent Hill so intriguing and are managing to produce unique experiences within an admittedly undefined structure. The franchise is about loneliness and trauma and how that affects your emotional state of mind. Townfall presents these emotions well, and my only frustrations arose when I was forced to grapple with some of its unbalanced video game mechanics. Score: 8.25 About Game Informer's review system

Silent Hill: Townfall Completes The Series’ Comeback With Another Great EntryNEW

Only a few years ago, the Silent Hill name was considered a relic of the horror genre. Its best efforts were its earliest from 20 or more years ago, and it had since lost its way. Lately, though, it's found its footing again, first with Bloober Team's remarkable remake of Silent Hill 2 in 2024, then with 2025's brilliantly rule-breaking Silent Hill f. Now, with Screen Burn's Silent Hill: Townfall, the comeback feels complete. Townfall is the third memorable entry in as many years, reviving a legendary series for a new era. Like Silent Hill f, Townfall defies tradition and takes the series outside the United States, this time to Scotland. There, an American man named Simon Ordell washes up on the shore of a blue-collar island town, St. Amelia. Right away, Townfall captures the all-important tone of a Silent Hill game. It's more than the fog that's become synonymous with the series: It's the unsettling stillness, the sense of sadness lurking in every shop and down every alleyway that lingers like a harbinger of the dread that will certainly follow. Though it adheres to some of the series' touchstones, it's a fully standalone entry. It works just as well if this is your first Silent Hill game. As you explore the town, it becomes clear early on that St. Amelia has suffered a tragedy so severe that the community was shuttered almost overnight. Heartfelt memorials in the town square dedicate pictures and words to loved ones taken too soon, and the displays are only matched in number and emotion by the many protest signs demanding accountability. The finer details, of course, aren't yours to understand for a long while. Like the fog and its accompanying melancholy, Silent Hill also serves as the stage for startling truths that only emerge late in a character's journey.  But if you think you know where it's going, I bet you don't. Screen Burn's founder and Townfall's writer Jon McKellan once ran a fansite dedicated to this series, and that sort of lifelong devotion is clear by the end of Townfall. He and his team understand what Silent Hill has been, but also--and more importantly--what it can be. The rules of this universe are played a bit fast and loose, and it's better for it. Is Silent Hill a physical place, or an idea? Is everyone found there sent to suffer? While some themes re-emerge like somber notes on a mandolin, some of Townfall's best parts are how it toys with expectations, twisting them into new shapes and sometimes rejecting them. Townfall feels a lot like Silent Hill 2 early on. The original music, as great as it is in its own right, deliberately recalls the series' heyday, and you'll spend some early hours navigating through the roundabout corridors of a hospital and an apartment complex. But this is more than a "fan game" liberally reusing what's worked before. Screen Burn makes it its own. That's most obvious in how it's played. Townfall is presented in first-person, as few games in the series have been before it, and introduces a new gameplay element that relies on a handheld piece of analog tech--the sort of physical contraption this team made its name on with games like Stories Untold and Observation. Whereas many games in the series use a radio to passively alert you to nearby threats, Townfall's CRTV is closer to Alien Isolation's motion scanner and demands active participation. With it, Simon can see hazy outlines of the fleshy monsters left shambling around the streets of St. Amelia. As the player, you'll need to tune it often, picking up the signals of these creatures, then swapping between them to carefully track their movements and avoid their gaze. Early encounters made this tech feel like an afterthought, but once Simon is outnumbered three to one--or worse--it proves its usefulness. Combined with the way Simon can lean around corners and over cover, this is certainly the most stealth-driven game in the series. You can also distract enemies by turning on radios or throwing objects before hiding in places like lockers, waiting for enemies to give you an opening. In some areas where enemies are especially numerous, I found these lockers to be too helpful, but because enemies were best avoided, I used them often, which made those semi-scripted moments lose their effect before long. Simon's handheld CRTV device acts like an evolution of the series' static on the radio. But such moments weren't the norm. Much more often, you'll need to carefully pick your openings to sneak past enemies, or else face them head-on. The music bears down on you in those close calls, creating urgency and the right kind of stress befitting any horror story, and when you do get spotted, facing them only piles on that tension. Despite being in first-person, combat in Townfall reminded me a lot of the Silent Hill 2 remake, because each encounter demands focus and well-timed attacks, and it's best avoided whenever possible. It's extremely tense. Enemies eat a good chunk of your health with each hit, and though enemy design could be more varied, they do express some important differences that make controlling a crowd of even just two of them a breathless fight to the death. I spent more than half the game completely avoiding combat whenever I could, and about three-quarters of it never firing my gun, because doing so runs the risk of alerting more enemies.  Like the fog and its accompanying melancholy, Silent Hill also serves as the stage for startling truths that only emerge late in a character's journey.  Townfall's combat is usually soaked in desperation and had me bracing for bad outcomes in all the ways a horror game should. This changed in the latter third of the game, where I was handed more than enough resources in a few different instances, trivializing some encounters. But these moments of empowerment weren't the norm, thankfully, and Townfall maintained its creepy vibe from start to finish. Another benefit of the first-person view is how much detail it lets Screen Burn pack into Townfall. Approaching desks or shop windows, it's almost always the case that you can read anything on those surfaces. You can find many notes to collect in a way that signifies they're highly plot-relevant, and many even help you solve the game's environmental puzzles, all of which feel right at home for a Silent Hill game, though they are never as convoluted as the series' early era.  Even more abundant than these plot-relevant collectibles are the smaller details the game doesn't call out. Noticing these details isn't crucial to enjoying Townfall's story, but they're no less engaging, filling in the world's history and teasing snippets of the game's central mystery. Everything is purpose-built, from the particular hue of the Otherworld sections (this time called the "Different") to the design of the monsters, which you can't easily notice before you kill them. I was so worried about Simon's place in the story that I found myself dodging combat not just to save on health kits, but to not earn the ire of the game's hidden ending calculator, and that stemmed from the way this game invites you to take a proverbial microscope to it. Otherworld sections return, though with a new look owed to important symbols in the story. As is often the case with Silent Hill, it's only when Townfall's final cards are turned over that much will make sense, and even then you can be sure there are additional endings to chase down in subsequent playthroughs for added context. But even on a first pass, Townfall is able to stand out from the shadow of the series' best games and earn its place beside them. While it doesn't reach the peak this series has previously found, Townfall's story gives me a familiar, welcome headache as I try to piece everything together.  Thematically, it does well to stand in two universes at once: feeling like an homage to the series' best while still sharing a perspective that is painfully timeless. Townfall is set in 1996, but its expressions of cruelty are alive and well today. At the same time--and maybe this will sound strange once you've played it for yourself--it's also one of the more hopeful entries in the series. It doesn't trade discomfort for hope, by any means. It's still a dark story that expresses some uncomfortable coldness the way this series is known to do, but it finds a way to let just a little light in without tearing out its jet-black heart. Townfall, like Silent Hill f before it, expresses what I love most about the comeback. As the idea of Silent Hill is uprooted and moved all over the world, we're seeing it through new cultural perspectives in a way we never have before. We spent nearly 30 years discussing and debating what Silent Hill was and is. Even the teams assigned the daunting task of replacing Team Silent seemed intent on doing their best impressions of the earliest and most beloved games. Townfall doesn't just prove that studios like Screen Burn know where Silent Hill comes from; it shows us where Silent Hill can go.

Orbitals Is An Antidote For Hazelight’s Miserable CharactersNEW
Something core to Orbitals stuck out to me as I played the Switch 2 exclusive. Compared to the similar co-op games from Hazelight Studios, the major difference in Shapefarm’s two-player 3D platformer ...
Something core to Orbitals stuck out to me as I played the Switch 2 exclusive. Compared to the similar co-op games from Hazelight Studios, the major difference in Shapefarm’s two-player 3D platformer was that the two main characters, Maki and Omura, actually seemed to enjoy each other’s company. While that might seem like an inconsequential detail, it was actually a breath of fresh air after sitting through Hazelight's co-op hits A Way Out, It Takes Two, and Split Fiction, which all centered on troubled, angsty, and often-combative protagonists. Hazelight's games blazed a path for Orbitals, refining a template of required two-player cooperative play, forced split-screen, and puzzles and action sequences that emphasize teamwork. While the Hazelight inspiration was clear in Orbitals (the game director is even a Hazelight alumnus), Shapefarm’s contribution to the genre contains a warmth and affability that better represent the spirit of what a cooperative game can be. For better or worse, Orbitals doesn’t bog you down with the backstories of Maki and Omura. It’s clear from the game’s opening that they’ve known one another since they were children, living together on a space station ravaged by a cosmic storm. The dual protagonists are basically blank slates with little motivation outside of their drive to save themselves and everyone on the station. And while that left a little to be desired in terms of history and lore, that minimalist approach ultimately served the gameplay experience. Maki and Omura's positive dynamic proves that teamwork truly makes the dream work. Maki and Omura may appear shallow on the surface, compared to the likes of escaped prisoners Vincent and Leo from A Way Out or soon-to-be divorcees Cody and May in It Takes Two, but the magic of Orbitals shows in dialogue. Through the 6-7 hours of the game, the duo imbues the story with infectious positive energy, bantering the same way long-time childhood friends would. They trade jabs, and players can actively spray water or shoot a metal claw at one another, with the characters reacting with playful mock-threats. They go back and forth with cheesy puns (“Our teamwork is getting... rock solid,” Maki exclaims as they lift rocks to use as platforms. “Even though we had a... rocky start,” Omura responds.) and tease one another about the state of their bedrooms. Most importantly, they explicitly trust one another through the entire story. Hazelight’s story formula uses a markedly different approach, taking cues from buddy films in which the two protagonists are far apart in personality. Naturally, that leads to heads butting, constant bickering, and downright hostility--until the story takes them through an arc in which they’re able to gain respect for one another and solidify their teamwork. It’s a sound way of crafting a story for a movie or a book, but featuring characters who aren’t in sync is far less effective in a co-op game. If you’re going to invest hours in a game’s story, I imagine you would play something like It Takes Two with a close friend or a romantic partner. Chances are, your relationship with your gaming partner is healthier than the one in It Takes Two. The persistent negativity from a story like that starts to turn into background noise to skip when you’re just trying to play a fun video game together. The absence of artificial conflict makes Orbitals a lot more palatable for co-op partners. We take on many different roles when we play video games; while some games allow us to craft a character’s personality and background, more often than not, we play as characters who are different from who we are as people--sometimes drastically so. The escapism of living fantasies and inhabiting different worlds is a prime attraction of video gaming. The main goal of a cooperative game is to have a joyful and mentally stimulating experience, working together to gain fulfillment from successful teamwork--and when the game’s story has discord as a foundation for its characters’ relationship, that makes it hard to get invested in the game as a whole. That isn’t to say that Hazelight’s output lacks any positivity. As irritating as Mio and Zoe’s dynamic could get in Split Fiction, there were sequences with fun and whimsy, and seeing the two eventually put aside their (otherwise contrived) differences and bond together created some catharsis. Even though It Takes Two was fraught with conflict and arguments, the minigames allowed for a break from the drama and opportunities for playfulness. Even A Way Out has wholesome moments such as these, with minigames letting Vincent and Leo play Connect Four and competing to balance themselves on wheelchairs--activities that provide fun distractions before the pair brutally guns down the boss of a crime syndicate later in the game. Hazelight deserves a lot of credit for being a pioneer in modern cooperative game design. Yet, it’s other games that follow that studio’s gameplay structure, like the upbeat Orbitals, that get to the heart of what makes playing a game with someone important in your life so special. It would be absurd to suggest that every co-op game in this mold has to be exactly like Orbitals and replicate its approach to characterization. Stories traditionally need tension, conflict, and contrast to compel audiences, and any fictional relationship that completely lacks any of those elements would appear insincere. But one thing that I hope Hazelight and other storytellers making co-op games learn from Orbitals is that if you want players to feel good, consider having the characters they play experience that positivity as well.

I have no idea if it will be good or bad, but I would be lying if I said I wasn’t excited for the Zelda movie next year. But I love that the emotional experience of playing a Resident Evil game has been so expertly translated into a film here. It officially unseats my previous favorite video game film adaptation, which was, of course, Josh Ruben’s 2021 take on everyone’s favorite Ubisoft game, Werewolves Within.
