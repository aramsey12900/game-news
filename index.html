After Beating Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse, Help Dracula Rebuild In This Awesome RoguelikeNEW
Let’s face it, if you are a Castlevania fan or just love metroidvania games in general, you’re probably sinking your teeth into Belmont’s Curse. And why not? After all, it just got a glowing review fr...
Let’s face it, if you are a Castlevania fan or just love metroidvania games in general, you’re probably sinking your teeth into Belmont’s Curse. And why not? After all, it just got a glowing review from our own Steve Watts, and like plenty of the rest of you, I’ve also been eagerly waiting for a new Castlevania adventure for over a decade. One indie team in particular seems keen to grab your attention once your time with Belmont's Curse is over, having recently released a demo for their Castlevania-adjacent roguelike. That game is Draftula, which asks, "What if Dracula commissioned you to build his labyrinthine castle?" Draftula’s demo is available on Steam for everyone to try right now. It plays like a deckbuilder, but instead of collecting cards, you earn tile pieces that are castle rooms. The goal of each run is to place each room tile in such a way that you build a castle tall enough to prevent the vampire hunter from knocking it down at the end of the night. You can also inhabit each room with monsters, which can further slow the vampire hunter down. Each piece has a coin cost so you need to be strategic in how you construct Dracula's castle, and depending on your performance each run, you can earn hearts that can be spent between runs to unlock permanent upgrades. It may sound easy, but the catch is that Dracula’s powers are somewhat weakened; he’s just woken up from a long slumber. Because of his diminished capabilities, you can't just immediately build the castle as a tower--to overcome certain height restrictions, you'll be required to use specific tiles to break the wards blocking you. Runs can conclude as quickly as several seconds or take as long as a few minutes, depending on which rooms and monsters show up and how much gold you can earn with each tile placement in order to place more rooms or monsters.  Of course, all of this is in vain. After each night, a heroic knight named Sir Del Montes ruthlessly tears down Dracula’s newly built castle. Players will then go to the hub area: a cliff overlooking the building site, where Dracula and various vendors reside. Some of these vendors sell new castle pieces for the hearts you collect over multiple runs, which in turn open opportunities to expand the castle beyond its current limits. Some building pieces are given temporarily, such as the one in the current demo build: the innkeeper, who wants his room surrounded by at least five other tiles. Del Montes even has some gentle words of encouragement for you, though he also says that he’ll knock down whatever you put up! So far, almost every part of Draftula is meticulously calculated, from the roster of available parts to its pleasing retro vibe and chill music. The demo isn't very long, consisting of only a handful of runs before the lack of available room tiles halts progression--you just can't reach the next height requirement. However, of what's there, Draftula feels like a roguelike that rewards planning, and I hope the full game expands on its awesome gameplay loop. My one big criticism is that Draftula needs an undo button just in case you make a mistake. It can be frustrating to accidentally misclick where I wanted a tile to go, so hopefully a way to circumvent that will be included in the full game.  But don't let my one complaint put you off trying out the game; the Draftula demo is great and it's only getting better. The demo has just been updated with a new Nightly mode, for example. This update adds a set number of tiles, a map, and mods that change the rules. These include bonuses that are granted whenever you place a room, such as randomly putting an Evil Eye room (which re-adds all other rooms in its row back to your available pieces) on the board after every fourth room you place, not being able to place more rooms in the same column of space during the turn you already placed a room there, and randomly distributing a copy of one room you’ve just played. The scoring method in Nightly mode is also predetermined, with bonuses calculated for the number of rooms placed, the castle’s height, and any coins and gems earned for that run. It’s a good way to keep practicing before the game’s release. You can retry as many times as you like, but you can only submit your score to the leaderboard once.  There’s currently no release date set for Draftula. However, if you fancy a chill construction game on theme with the latest Castlevania adventure, or just need a break from its many difficult boss battles, consider giving this demo a go!

How To Get Minior Minior is a little different from other common Pokemon in that they won't just randomly spawn in the wild; you'll only have a chance to encounter one on evenings when a meteor shower is taking place. During these specific nights, Minior will spawn on the map between 5 PM and 11:59 PM local time. From there, it's simply a matter of waiting for one to appear, then tapping on them to initiate a catch encounter. You can increase your odds of successfully catching the Pokemon by first feeding them a Razz Berry. Minior Shower Schedule Minior will appear in the wild during the following meteor showers: Orionids October 19 5-11:59 PM local time Leonids November 14 5-11:59 PM local time Geminids December 11 5-11:59 PM local time One Minior color will debut in the Northern Hemisphere, and another color will debut in the Southern Hemisphere Eta Aquariids May 3 5-11:59 PM local time Southern Delta Aquariids July 28 5-11:59 PM local time Perseids August 10 5-11:59 PM local time According to the official Pokemon Go website, a different Minior core color will debut during each of the aforementioned events, so you'll need to play the game during every meteor shower if you're hoping to add all seven core colors to your collection. A couple of bonuses will also be available during the meteor shower events. You'll receive extra Stardust for catching Minior, and you'll see shooting stars streaking across the in-game sky from 5-9 PM local time. The game will also offer a free Timed Research that rewards you with a Star Piece and additional Stardust when completed. How To Change Minior's Form Minior doesn't evolve into any other Pokemon, but the meteorite Pokemon does come in several different variants.  Every Minior you encounter will initially be in Meteor Form, with the same brown outer shell. Once the Pokemon sustains enough damage during battle and their HP drops below 50 percent, their shell will break to reveal a different colored core. There are seven core colors in total, one for every hue of the rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, and violet. One core color will debut during each meteor shower. Can Minior Be Shiny? At the moment, shiny Minior is not yet available in Pokemon Go. Shiny variants are typically introduced several months after a Pokemon first makes their debut, so shiny Minior may arrive down the line as part of some future event. However, nothing has been announced yet. We're coming up on the end of October, but Pokemon Go still has a few events lined up for this month, including its annual Halloween celebration. The Kindo'jan labyrinth, arguably the patch's biggest feature, saw players running into issue after issue. The AI-fueled demand for components and memory is to blame, as is the war in Iran that's driving up oil prices. Instead of eating the costs (while they make huge profits and collect tariff refunds), they are charging people more to maintain their margins. The Switch 2 is among the cheapest ways to play MW4 this year, with that console starting at $500 (up from $450 at launch). The PS5 now starts at $600 (up from $400 at launch) and the latest Xbox consoles are more expensive, too. The Series S now goes for $500 (was $300) and Series X for $750 ($500 at launch). In short, you're in for major sticker shock if you're buying a new console. For PC players, it's more expensive than ever to build a capable rig, but your price will vary depending on the setup you want, preconfigured or made yourself. Release: March 4, 2027 Eternal Anima is the time-twisting RPG revealed during the big September Nintendo Direct that looked like a Square Enix HD-2D game but isn’t. It is also the game with the “Never Strive For A Better Tomorrow” splash screen. After playing the game for roughly an hour within developer Marvelous Inc.’s Tokyo office, I eschew that line – I will, in fact, strive for a better tomorrow, if only so I can play more Eternal Anima one day.  The world has essentially come to its final act in Eternal Anima. Urvis Ael is the last remaining bastion of civilization in a flooded world. That said, living in Urvis Ael isn’t all that bad; just don’t strive for a better tomorrow or really any kind of change. Protagonist Wade breaks those rules and sets out on an adventure, feeling deep down that a better life is possible. Wade is bestowed with the power of Psychometry, which lets him look into the past, view memories, and inherit abilities and classes for use in combat. My hands-on time with Eternal Anima is mostly focused on combat, and for good reason: it rules. It is turn-based combat, but with a time-turning twist. Wade also has the power of Future Sight, which means he already knows exactly what every enemy will do each turn, reflected in the UI. Every ability has an action speed shown on the timeline at the bottom of the screen. You can always see an enemy’s upcoming action, who they will target, and even the outcome.  Combat in Eternal Anima is less about hitting enemies harder and more about hitting them smarter. When you attack an enemy's weaknesses with Wade or other party members, you knock them back on the battle timeline, pushing their predecided actions back in the move queue. Your goal is to push them back enough that you kill them before they can attack, or, for bigger enemies, knock them off the timeline in what’s known as a Break. This cancels their action, opening up your party for another round of attacks. The battle system doesn’t feel as active as a standard turn-based RPG; instead, it makes you feel more like a god playing with action figures. You decide what happens, then sit back and watch it all unfold. Everything is depicted in a beautiful, bokeh-enhanced art style that’s unique to Eternal Anima, despite the initial HD-2D impression. I love how the world and enemies look, which makes it even more disappointing that the characters are rendered as chibi. It doesn’t gel with the game’s painterly visuals and clashes with its heavier thematic tones.  As I explore Eternal Anima’s miniature settings, which resemble closed-off dioramas placed carefully on a larger world map, I take part in fishing minigames, collect items, defeat enemies, and encounter NPCs from the past, like Fletcher. After I interact with a glowing sword on the ground, an animated flashback plays, detailing Fletcher’s story and ending with a notification that I received a bow from his past. As I continue through this world, some two centuries before my time, I encounter a man from this period who’s trying to save his forest hometown from flooding. Of course, I already know this will happen in my timeline because the entire world is flooded, but Wade suspects that if he can convince this person of the looming threat, he can change the present by altering the past. My demo ends shortly after, but now, a month removed from the preview, I’m still thinking about Eternal Anima, specifically its combat. A lot of turn-based combat makes you feel powerful. But Eternal Anima’s makes you feel like a genius, and that’s smart and novel. I’m left striving for March now.

You will soon get the chance to experience that brutality yourself because developer ProbablyMonsters is releasing a demo on Steam soon on October 19, and it will be available through October 26. Ahead of that demo launch, ProbablyMonsters also released a new trailer highlighting the game's narrative and villainous Hyena troupe of Nazi leaders as part of today's Galaxies Autumn Showcase. These Hyenas are at the center of protagonist Vano's Nazi hunting, and they are a notorious squad of Nazi agents whose extreme brand of cruelty made them feared across occupied Europe and the world, according to a press release. Players will control Vano as he kills countless Nazis in violent and bloody ways while tracking down each of the Hyenas (who he will presumably also kill violently and bloodily). Check it out in the latest Nekome: Nazi Hunter trailer for yourself below:  Game Informer also spoke to Nekome: Nazi Hunter game director Jeronimo Barrera about the game in the lead-up to this trailer and the incoming Steam Next Fest demo. In the interview, which took place over email, Barrera talks about the game's politics (or lack thereof), the Hyenas, visual inspiration, and more: Game Informer's Wesley LeBlanc: How did the team decide on the Hyenas as the antagonists/villains of the game, rather than say, the obvious choice of Hitler? Barrera: As we developed the narrative, we had already committed to pursuing the grindhouse aesthetic that would drive the presentation. We needed a disgusting set of villains that would be fun to hunt down and murder. Although we are rooted in real-world events, the player fantasy we are after is to hunt down and murder as many Nazis, both big and small, and that needs to deliver over-the-top fun. Can you tell me about the Hyenas? What are their differences from a narrative perspective, and what are their differences from a gameplay perspective? Does each Hyena approach the hunt for Vano in different ways? We introduce the Hyenas almost immediately in the narrative. After that, the player won’t be confused as to why they need to hunt them down, not the other way around! Vano hunts them down, and each Hyena boss fight is a unique challenge that pushes the players' abilities.  It sounds like Vano will be hunted across occupied Europe and beyond? What can you tell me about that beyond?Our research into how we can tell a new story and give a new experience has given us a lot of surprising locations to explore. For example, we were shocked when we realized that we could have our first encounters with Nazis on U.S. soil! Vano’s adopted home is New York City. It’s hard to believe, but there were Nazi Party rallies in New York. This is where Vano starts his hunt through a tragic coincidence, which then takes Vano to Europe. But he’s not being hunted; he is the hunter.Is there anything larger than life or supernatural or demonic happening narratively, or is it Vano versus some Nazis that are led by the Hyenas? I ask because scenes in this new trailer almost imply some monstrous transformation, or maybe even something vampiric, as blood drips from the Hyenas' faces (though I recognize that could just be a style choice).The Nazis were hideous monsters. We play on those themes for our abilities and how we present them to the player. There are themes we explore that could be considered metaphysical. But with that said, it’s up for people to interpret the narrative on their own. How would you describe the art style/visual design of the game? Our biggest inspiration came from the grindhouse film genre. Turning the stylized pulpiness and violence of movies like Inglourious Basterds into a video game was a challenge that the team was excited to tackle, and we believe this grindhouse spirit gives our game a distinct visual style.Graphic novels were another major influence. Our goal with the art style was to create something over the top and entertaining, while respecting the serious subject matter. The game would lose its sense of fun if it looked too real, and graphic novels helped us find a look that is visually interesting yet able to handle the story’s darkness and violence. How long has the team been working on the game? The team began working on the game in 2024. The genesis of the game was a meeting inside ProbablyMonsters where we started quoting Inglourious Basterds, and we all became set on the idea of making a game about hunting down and killing Nazis through an untold Second World War story.How big is the team? Nekome: Nazi Hunter’s development is led internally at ProbablyMonsters by Brett Bourbin, Brand Inman, Sergei Kuprejanov, Kay Willis and me, with assistance from co-development partners. But that’s just my line of thinking – I’m curious where the team lands on this discussion now that some additional time has passed.  I think we're overcomplicating this. Nazis are bad. That shouldn't be a political statement, just common sense. Nekome is a revenge story about people who suffered horrific atrocities at the hands of Nazis. We're not making a statement about modern politics. We're telling a story with villains who are unquestionably evil. If someone feels politically attacked by a game where you kill Nazis, maybe they should take a long, hard look in the mirror. Anything I missed you’d like to touch on? We created Nekome because we saw an opportunity to tell a Second World War story through a perspective that games have rarely depicted. World War II is one of the most common settings in media. And yet, there are almost no games that explore the persecution of the Roma and Sinti during that period.That is a real, deeply horrible part of history, so our story also comes with responsibility. The team has worked with historians and cultural consultants to help us approach Vano’s identity, his community and the historical context thoughtfully and credibly. We promise to tell Vano’s story honestly and approach that history with care.
