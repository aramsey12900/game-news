A Look Back On The First Two Onimusha Games With Planner And Director Motohide EshiroNEW

Onimusha: Way of the Sword is Capcom’s return to its samurai action series after a 20-year hiatus, and it’s also the latest game to grace the cover of Game Informer. I traveled to Capcom’s Osaka HQ to play the game for hours, interview its various developer leads, and explore neighboring Kyoto to learn how it inspired the game. I was also able to speak to Motohide Eshiro, the director of Onimusha 2: Samurai’s Destiny and its recent remaster. Though he’s not directly involved with Onimusha: Way of the Sword, Eshiro tells me he serves as a producer these days. But rather than being a head producer on a project himself, he supports up-and-coming producers on a variety of projects. Before directing Onimusha 2: Samurai’s Destiny, which launched in 2002, he worked as a planner on Onimusha: Warlords, which Capcom released in 2001.  Game Informer Capcom producer Motohide Eshiro Eshiro tells me he’s been “very surprised and impressed” with the way video game graphics, particularly across the Onimusha series, have evolved alongside the action genre over the past two decades since Onimusha’s start. Having just finished the remasters of Onimusha: Warlords and its sequel prior to this June trip, I was curious to discuss the series' “Sengoku Biohazard” origins because Warlords, especially, is so clearly a Resident Evil game with a samurai skin (and it still rules today). “One of the producers at the time behind the Resident Evil series was looking at what they were able to achieve with the pre-rendered graphics for Resident Evil, which has a focus on gunplay and long-distance combat, but felt that it would be quite interesting to see what they could do in the vein of a close-range action-based game, and that’s where that idea was born from,” Eshiro tells me. “Setting it during the Sengoku period, the Warring States period, would provide them a good setting to essentially have this close-range combat system with swords, so that’s where that [setting] and code name originated from.” Eshiro says he joined the development of Onimusha: Warlords after it had already begun, so much of the game was set in stone already. But he was promoted from planner to director for the sequel, Onimusha 2, and says his main goal was to expand the scale.  “The first game was a very complete package, but it was also quite simple,” Eshiro tells me. “So, in approaching the next project, the sequel, we wanted to expand on the scale and really make players feel that the world was more alive and more liveable. [That’s why we] introduced elements such as the friendship system and having allies, so you get a bit of human drama there to get closer to the characters and understand them better. But we also wanted to improve the visuals as well.” I still adore pre-rendered backgrounds, and though the indie space in 2026 is still using them, triple-A has abandoned the practice. Eshiro tells me it was a case of limitations back in the day, but says full camera control gives players additional layers of immersion. The tradeoff, however, is “with pre-rendered, you can have these really curated and crafted settings and scenes, so there’s definitely space for both beliefs.” Onimusha: Way of the Sword director Satoru Nihei adds, “the camera does allow for more freedom but there’s the downside that you can have these perfectly crafted scenes where you have this historic building, like Kiyomizu-dera Temple, that you want people to focus on, but because people can control the camera, they’re not really going to necessarily do that.“There are definitely some downsides to having cameras, but in terms of pre-rendered, yeah, there’s the same kind of strengths.” At the end of Onimusha: Warlords, Samanosuke takes on an Oni Form in a cutscene. But in Onimusha 2, Jubei can transform into his Oni Form at will. Eshiro says the team knew players thought the Oni Form was “cool,” and really wanted to double down on the cool factor in the sequel. Capcom reached out to Japanese film director, screenwriter, and character designer Keita Amemiya to create Jubei’s form. His rendition of Jubei’s Oni Form was cool, and that was that.  At this point in the interview, it was time to ask Eshiro the most important question of this entire cover story trip: Did the Resident Evil 4 team steal the jetski idea from the Onimusha 2 team? “I really doubt it,” Eshiro says, probably practicing some professionalism instead of saying, “Of course they did,” like he probably wants to. Similarly, I then ask Eshiro about robots and TVs appearing in Onimusha 2. He says the designs came from Amemiya and were put in the game to further push the dark fantasy aspect of the game and push for modernism or realism – I guess TVs and robots were all the hotness in 2002. Of note, Nihei tells me we won’t see TVs, robots, “or anything like that” in Way of the Sword. I close out my interview with Eshiro by asking him why he thinks Onimusha was a success back in the early 2000s. He points to the use of real-world stars in the games. “There are quite a few elements, but one of them is that at the time, in particular, it was quite rare to use real-life movie stars and actors as main characters in a game, and that was one of the big selling points for [Onimusha: Warlords] at the time,” Eshiro says. “But as well as that, the gameplay was also quite unique at the time. There weren’t really many other games out there doing the same things, necessarily. So, for instance, the soul absorption mechanics, or the Issen with Onimusha 2 – we introduced the Chain Issen and Charge Issen to kind of really flesh out that system. And even with pre-rendered background, which you know were features in Resident Evil, a lot of people maybe saw those pre-rendered backgrounds in Onimusha and thought, 'Oh, it’s just like Resident Evil,' but then saw our unique spin with the swordplay and the setting. So, all of those elements combined contributed to the success of the original games.”  You can read about how Capcom landed on using the late Toshirō Mifune’s likeness for Musashi’s appearance in Way of the Sword here. Since the release of Onimusha: Warlords in 2001, the series has gone on to sell more than 9 million copies, and Onimusha: Way of the Sword is set to push that number further when it launches this September. I’ll be diving deeper into Capcom’s return to Onimusha as part of Game Informer’s month of Way of the Sword coverage. Phasmophobia is full of spooky, funny, and downright weird easter eggs that ghost hunters should keep an eye out for. From props that reference classic horror movies like The Ring and Friday the 13th to hidden pieces of lore and nods to popular Phasmophobia streamers, there are plenty of secrets for eagle-eyed players to uncover on select maps. After all, ghost hunting doesn’t have to be scary all of the time. While finding many of these secrets offers you nothing but sheer joy, there are two main map easter eggs that present you with a series of head-scratching puzzles. However, if you manage to solve them, you’re rewarded with a special ID Card and Badge. So, below, we break down all the hidden references and secrets to look out for in Phasmophobia, and how to solve the Point Hope and Nell’s Diner easter eggs so you can claim your nifty prizes. Phasmophobia's Maple Lodge Campsite features the most easter eggs of all the maps, and many of the game's coolest, with several referencing classic horror movies. If you walk to the end of the pier near the lodge house and look in the water, after a few seconds, a hockey mask similar to the one Jason Voorhees wears in the Friday the 13th movies will appear. This references how Jason drowned in the water at Camp Crystal Lake before returning for his revenge on unsuspecting camp counselors years later--fitting for a campsite map. Slender Man Ever feel like you're being watched? Because you probably are. This easter egg can be found in the Maple Lodge Campsite and Camp Woodwind maps. If you look into the woods surrounding the campsite, you may spot the familiar--and unwelcome--figure of Slender Man: a pale-faced, thin, and unnaturally supernatural figure wearing a suit. Slender Man is a fictional character that originated as a creepypasta meme, but has now become a digital urban legend. Slender Man won’t always appear, but make sure to check the forest for him, particularly around the area the van is parked, each time you play these maps. The Ring TV You don't even need to insert the VHS tape. Another horror movie reference in the Maple Lodge Campsite can be found in the main lodge near the pier. Enter the lodge using the key found on the ‘Welcome’ mat outside, and find the TV in the corner of the ground floor. Turn the TV on using the remote control on the coffee table. The TV screen will turn on, but be fuzzy, and then the phone in the lodge will begin to ring. This is a reference to The Ring (Ringu), in which, after watching a supernatural videotape, characters receive a phone call telling them they’ll die in seven days. Corpse under the cabin You might need a Tier 3 flashlight for this one. Leave the lodge with The Ring easter egg in it, and turn right to follow the cabin around to a fence, next to a gap under the lodge. Shine a flashlight (ideally Tier 3) under the cabin, angling to the left a bit, and you should see a corpse wrapped in a sheet hidden deep underneath the cabin. The Blair Witch Project stick figure This might be more immersive seen through a video camera. When you enter the Maple Lodge Campsite, turn left before the reception building to enter Campsite A. On the left of the campsite, behind the BBQs, you should see a familiar stick figure hanging. This is a Twana,  a humanoid stick figure that is the symbol of the Blair Witch, from the classic horror movie The Blair Witch Project. Potato with a face  Let's start with the weird potato with a face. This item can be found in two places. The easiest place to find it is Tanglewood, where it's hidden in the kitchen, in the right-side cupboard under the counter with the spilled grocery bag on it. You can also find it in Sunny Meadows, in the Bake House, in the top oven, the second in from the left. The potato face is a reference to Phasmophobia streamer PsychoHypnotic, who used to use the potato filter regularly. When you drop the potato, it makes the same sound as his Twitch alert. Ridgeview basement photos Kinetic Games HQ isn't far from the New Forest. In the basement of 10 Ridgeview Court, you may spot a board covered in photographs. The photos were taken by Jack Skinner, Kinetic Games' art lead, and are of the New Forest in the U.K., close to the developer's headquarters in Southampton. Willow Street's cats If only ghosthunters had nine lives too. The reworked Willow Street map features many cat-themed items, including cat toys, climbing frames, and a hidden Schrödinger-esque cat in a laundry hamper in the closet (that may or may not actually exist). In the art room of the house, you can find a wall in the corner behind the door featuring many cat sketches and photographs. These cats all belong to members of the Kinetic Games team. In addition, Willow Street is named after Willow, the late cat of Kinetic Games art director Corey Dixon (cjdxn). Jojo was here An easy-to-miss streamer reference. In the main Phasmophobia lobby, walk over to the Event Board and look at the table below the board. On the front-right side of the table (closest to the filing cabinet) is etched text that reads: “JOJO WAS HERE.” This is a reference to Twitch streamer JOJOsaysbreee. Werewolf howl It's not just ghosts that lurk around Bleasdale. When using a Parabolic Microphone on the Bleasdale Farmhouse map, you may hear a werewolf howl. This is rare and doesn’t always occur. The O'Bake Off Wonder if it's written by Bloody Mary Berry? Another Bleasdale Farmhouse easter egg can be found in the kitchen. On the counter, next to a sign that lists groceries, you can find an ‘O’Bake Off’ cookbook that references one of Phasmophobia’s ghosts, the Obake, and The Great British Bake Off (also known as The Great British Baking Show in the U.S.), a British television baking competition. Developer pet plates The Kinetic Games team loves to include their pets in Phasmoiphobia. In Bleasdale Farmhouse, go to the pink tea room and look at the pet plates in the corner. The pet pictures are of real-life pets of Kinetic Games developers. Attic posters I can't wait to see The Poltergeists in August. Another one in Bleasdale Farmhouse. Go up to the attic and through the beaded door to enter the purple attic room. On the wall opposite the entrance, you’ll find posters that reference Phasmophobia maps, ghosts, and elements, including a festival poster featuring bands such as “Edgefield,” “Jinn,” and “Sanity Drain.” Lilith the spider Keep an eye out for Lilith in the attic. In Bleasdale Farmhouse, in the same room as the posters, you’ll find three terrariums, each with a photograph of a spider and its name. Except the photograph that’s meant to be on one of the terrariums (of “Lilith”) has fallen below. Lilith has managed to get free, and you can find her wandering around the attic if you look closely enough. Score 666 in basketball Only 666 shots to go. This is a tricky one. Big Walk Review – A Walk To RememberNEW
Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PlayStation 5, PC, Mac Publisher: Panic Inc. Developer: House House Release: August 4, 2026 Seeing a game lean as heavily into its core ideas as Big Walk does is rare. Untitled Goose Game studio House House built an experience filled with activities: puzzles, physics, basketball and music, lights and sounds, as well as secrets and foreboding premonitions of future discoveries. But the larger experience is mostly a lot of walking with your friends. And it's a pretty good walk, too.Big Walk requires co-op; puzzles adjust to lobby size, from two to three, to four-plus. You can have up to 12 players in a lobby – puzzles will still be sized for four players – but you cannot play alone. And while there's no real way to enforce this approach, House House strongly encourages you to use proximity voice chat in-game, rather than hopping on a Discord call or playing local split-screen. These requirements can seem stringent compared to other co-op adventure games, and it makes coordinating a Big Walk session feel akin to setting up a board game night. But what you get in exchange for following those rules for play, though, is entirely worth it. Each player gets their own orbish, bird-like in-game avatar. Tools littered in the tutorial area let you customize your character, learn the controls, and gradually understand the verbiage. You can gesture with your arms, such as lifting them up in the sky, pointing, or raising them out to the side. Sitting plops you down in place, or sends you sliding down a hill. There's jumping, crouching, and even picking up items and kicking them like a field goal. Movement and interaction is delightfully tactile and involved, as you can talk with your hands and express thoughts with your in-game avatar body, literally pointing at things, waving, and doing the Rocky pose when you conquer a puzzle.Distance reduces your voice volume, so walkie-talkies, radios, and even whiteboards became methods for long-distance communication for our group. A flashlight might be useless during the day, but at night you need one to navigate the darkness. Since you can initially only hold one thing in your hands at a time, equipment comes at a premium, so you make interesting decisions about what to take and leave behind. The limitations do prevent, however, carrying and using some of the more interesting and fun tools; the whiteboard is a really clever nonverbal tool to mess around with, for instance, but not as "effective" as a walkie-talkie or radio, which are abundant on the island. But that doesn't mean I didn't love writing "Kick Me" on a whiteboard and sticking it in my friend's backpack as we explored.Going anywhere takes time; not a gameplay resource or number on a meter, but just real-life time. This naturally leads to downtime which facilitates some of Big Walk's best moments. Stumbling across a new puzzle room or gadget leads to hijinks. We argued over who got to hold the megaphone we'd just found, or go fumbling in the dark trying to find where we dropped a key item. If cartoon birds could ever re-enact Stand By Me in the Australian wilderness, it'd look a lot like Big Walk.A north-star objective underpins all of this: small red objects, which look a little like those rubber Kong toys you fill with peanut butter for dogs, which we took to calling "gourds." Usually, to solve these, we'd need to figure out the solution to an ambient puzzle, which had a delightful range including blindfolded obstacle courses, pitching machines, egg timers, mazes, lights and pictures, and many, many wooden blocks with symbols to hold up and nod or shake our heads at. Big Walk loves to play with means of communication in these rooms too, and its best puzzles take full advantage of your buy-in to its requirements. Soundproof rooms forced our team to figure out how to communicate nonverbally. Shorthands evolved around specific puzzle pieces. Shouting "turn 10 degrees to your left and scooch forward" to my pal, as they're wearing a giant helmet that muffles their voice and blocks their vision, was genuinely fun. These gourd puzzles feel like summer camp activities in the best ways. Gourds can be placed in specific locations and, once enough have been placed, they open a secondary puzzle, which then unlocks a new feature on the island. I'm being intentionally vague because, after the initial tutorial, they become Big Walk's big reveals. Each one introduces a new feature that recontextualizes a piece of the island, which is impactful and exciting.If anything, that's where Big Walk starts to slow its pace a bit. As we focused on unlocking more of the island, completing more puzzles, and figuring out what lay at the end of it all, the "walk" of it fell to the background. Walking itself is sometimes a bit too finicky; jumping up terrain and ledges, in particular, can be frustrating to manage. The expanse of the island closed in a bit, even as we uncovered more and more secrets, and found a larger post-credits puzzle chase meant for those who want more. I wanted more silly opportunities to play and find goofy levity in this large experience.In the nine hours it took my crew of three to see the end of Big Walk, though, it felt like we had made a real journey together. We laughed a lot and came up with many absurd puzzle solutions that still somehow worked. Big Walk's excellent finale effectively brought the message home: more than anything, this was our shared walk.Sharing that journey is the point of Big Walk. It asks you to isolate, to put yourself in a siloed-off game station on proximity chat, so you can experience what it feels like to journey together in its virtual world. It provides tools for you to venture apart, to go vast distances, and still reconnect to gather your gourds and unlock more of the island. House House lays out all the ways your group could go, and lets you loose. Look for an ending, seek out puzzles, search for mysteries or climb for adventure; no matter what, you're all going on a memorable walk together, and that's a pretty good way to spend a few evenings together with friends.
