There’s Never Been A Better Time To Play Fire Emblem: AwakeningNEW
The world is a complicated place, but, regardless of political leanings, I think we can all agree that in 2016 I was much younger than I am in 2026. The younger me was a much more patient person, espe...
The world is a complicated place, but, regardless of political leanings, I think we can all agree that in 2016 I was much younger than I am in 2026. The younger me was a much more patient person, especially when it came to playing video games. I relished the opportunity to go on whatever ride designers and developers had spent years meticulously crafting. These days, however, I find myself frantically looking for the shortest and quickest path to satisfaction. Time is a commodity and I am not rich in it.  Maybe it's my patience wearing thin, or maybe it's the children who are wrong, but I find that games these days are working overtime to put as much distance between the player and the thing that makes playing it satisfying and worthwhile. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave is undoubtedly the best recent example of this happening. I love tactics games. I'm not great at them, but nevertheless, I relish the opportunity to slowly move units around squares, compare stats, and then watch them bonk each other. To me, they are the video game equivalent of waking up on a Saturday morning and sitting down to do a sudoku puzzle or fill out a crossword. Like I said, OLD. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave, however, has been the equivalent of being old and waking up on a Saturday morning to find I've got bills to pay, family members to call, meals to prep, mandatory exercise sessions to do, six different gods to appease, and a neighbor that accosts me in the hallway to tell me about their mundanity-riddled week.  Each time I turn on Fortune's Weave, I find that the thing I want to do most is buried underneath a laundry list of chores that, while beneficial to my ongoing existence, are also incredibly tedious to the extent that by the time I get to the bit where I can make my little people bonk each with different damage values, I don't even have the mental energy to enjoy making my little people bonk each other. Fortune's Weave has excellent tactical gameplay. It's very engaging and more intricate than any other entries in the past, but the mountain I have to climb in between these moments of tactical joy are so steep that, recently, I decided to take a different route entirely and fired up Fire Emblem: Awakening for the Nintendo 3DS and was reminded of how damn good that game is. Not just because the tactics gameplay is incredibly approachable and developing the bonds to strengthen characters and deepen their capabilities is so rewarding, but because it's all there pretty much immediately.  Awakening–along with Fates and Echoes, the other two 3DS Fire Emblem entries–were made before the series pivoted to being a Persona-like and gated the core tactics gameplay behind a layer of role-playing mechanics focused on doing daily activities and micro-tasks. So, to my mind, and as someone who comes to the series first and foremost for tactical gameplay, it is modern Fire Emblem at its best. And it holds up so well.  Everything in Awakening drives toward the many tactical skirmishes that make up the bulk of the experience. Although there are conversations and other narrative beats that guide the story along, they are trimmings around the gameplay that largely serve to contextualize battles and invite some investment in characters. The lack of mandatory social activities with everyone on your squad, I think, doesn't diminish how much I cared about each unit because my attachment to units is largely fueled by how well they charm me through character design and the precious few lines they have to say now and then. Of course, how well they perform in battle also contributes, but that is also impacted by the writing.  Take Donnel for example. He is a wide-eyed kid that clearly shouldn't be on the battlefield. He's very ordinary, has grown up as a farmhand, and has no aspirations of being a hero or saving the world. He just happens to be in a position to fight for his village and does what he can to preserve his quaint little life with his family. Dungeons & Dragons and World of Warcraft are two of the biggest fantasy games around, even though they exist in completely different mediums. As such, a crossover between the two is a natural fit (I bet there are plenty of D&D groups who also run raids in World of Warcraft together), but the form of that collaboration is less certain. In a private presentation breaking down the upcoming Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft project, the designers made their approach clear: this book is a toybox. "This book is absolutely a toolbox or toybox that you can use to fill your D&D game with these sorts of adventures, and I think the dungeons represent that really well," senior game designer Joey Haeck said. "There are a lot of tools at your disposal that I think make it really easy for a DM to explore the world of Azeroth with their players."This isn't a linear, pre-written campaign, but a set of curated crossover locations, character options, magic items, and monsters for players to build their own version of events. Of course, as Haeck mentions, the book also includes some of the most iconic dungeons from World of Warcraft's history, and if your DM isn't interested in coming up with a narrative framework to tie everything together, you're welcome to just storm some castles together. The book also includes advice on building campaigns around World of Warcraft villains, many of which have stat blocks included.Each dungeon is designed for parties of a certain level, defined in D&D as tiers of play. You can venture into the Deadmines and Shadowfang Keep at levels 1-4; Blackfathom Deeps at levels 5-10; Dire Maul at levels 11-16; and Azjol-Nerub at levels 17-20. There's also the Scarlet Monastery, which is designed for levels 5-10 or 11-16 depending on which wing you're exploring, and Onyxia's Lair, designed for characters level 17-20 and centered around a boss fight with the dragon who dwells within. Yes, this is a dungeon with a dragon; as I said, the crossover is a natural fit. Dungeon layouts are pretty loyal to their MMO counterparts, though Haeck mentions they've added new secrets and shortcuts to some, which will be a surprise even to players intimately familiar with the maps. And while most problems in World of Warcraft are solved with combat, the D&D adaptation allows for alternate solutions, like stealth and negotiation, where it makes sense. Still, if you want to go in guns blazing, you're more than welcome to. Some dungeons include random combat encounter tables for that very purpose.The foes on those tables are a mixture of newly designed Warcraft monsters and existing D&D monsters, some of which are re-skinned to fit the Warcraft universe. For example, there's already a stat block for a water elemental in the 2025 Monster Manual, but if you want players to face off against a Murloc, the book includes rules to convert a table of humanoid stat blocks by adding the Amphibious trait and a swim speed. Meanwhile, characters like Jaina Proudmoore, Sylvanas Windrunner, and the Lich King Arthas each get their own unique stats, rated at a challenge rating of 22, 23, and 25, respectively. And while there are plenty of dragons in D&D, their abilities don't line up one-to-one with those in the Warcraft universe, so the crossover will include Green, Blue, Red, Black, and Bronze Dragonflight Dragons to square up against.While the presentation was more DM-focused, it ended with some player-centric mechanics. Tool proficiencies (which can fall to the wayside in many D&D games) are repurposed to correspond to World of Warcraft's professions. There's guidance on how gathering professions can be used to collect resources and how production professions can be used to craft new items.Finally, we saw some of the game's factions, which parties can gain affinity with using D&D's existing Renown system, found in the Dungeon Master's Guide. Raising your Renown brings mechanical advantages – allies of the Earthen Ring can use the faction's portals, and the closest allies of the Valdrakken Accord are granted a wish. It's a toybox with plenty of options, and I'm excited to see it all laid out, but it's a pretty daunting project.
