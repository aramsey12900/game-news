There was a time when video games were largely linear affairs. You either moved up and down or from left to right in enclosed spaces. As technology evolved, so did the scope of digital worlds. Now players are regularly treated to expansive environments filled with quests, interesting characters, and horizons that stretch further with every console generation. These experiences consume dozens if not hundreds of hours and knowing which titles are worth the time investment has become crucial. To that end, we’ve assembled a list of 10 great modern open-world games available today to help make what can be a paralyzing decision a little easier.Note that these are listed in release date order and don’t necessarily represent the best games of all time. Rather, this will be an ever-changing list of quality titles to arrive in recent years that you should go out of your way to get lost in.   Grand Theft Auto VI PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S Grand Theft Auto is arguably the blueprint for open worlds in video games, and its sixth entry is the series' peak. In a universe steeped in satire and cynicism, protagonists Jason and Lucia are instantly likable figures worth rooting for. Their love story is absolutely worth your time, but even if you made the decision to ignore it, you'd be overwhelmed by Leonida's exceedingly vast playground, ripe for exploration. When we say GTAVI's scope is staggering, we are partially talking about the literal size of the map, but we're mainly talking about its density; every corner is meticulously crafted with people to meet and places to rob. It's one of the most believable spaces in gaming history, bolstered by incredible graphics and populated with characters only a little more zany than you'd encounter in its real-life equivalent of Florida. Its dozens of cars feel great to drive, and its dozens of guns are a thrill to shoot, but if you decide to get really into birdwatching instead, that's fine by us. Grand Theft Auto VI is one of the best games of this generation – it's no surprise it made its way onto this top 10 list. | Our review Ghost Of Yōtei PlayStation 5 In an overly simplistic way, Ghost of Yōtei basically took everything that worked about Sucker Punch’s 2020 open world action game, Ghost of Tsushima, and improved it. Exploration, combat, visuals, and narrative are all major steps up from the previous game, which was already fantastic. Atsu’s journey of revenge, fighting and exploring her way through Ezo, is an always-compelling experience, not because it radically re-addresses or changes the mechanics that made Tsushima so great, but rather because they are carefully iterated on and improved in sometimes subtle ways. And as an open world game, it constantly excels at giving the player worthwhile distractions that all appear to the player naturally. You’re not bouncing between points on an overworld map – you’re literally following the wind where it takes you and always ending up somewhere amazing doing something incredible. | Our review Kingdom Come: Deliverance II PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch 2, PC Kingdom Come: Deliverance II is one of the highest-rated open worlds on this list, and for good reason. If Grand Theft Auto VI is the best modern-day open world, and Cyberpunk 2077 the best future-set open-world game, then KCDII is easily the best medieval open-world game in some time. Eschewing the more fantastical likes of the medieval open-world format – which The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt excellently covers on this list – KCDII does all it can to remain grounded and true to the 15th Century Kingdom of Bohemia land it’s set in. You follow Henry, whom you’ll craft through your decisions and upgrade choices, on a journey of retribution, rebellion, and reflection, and you’ll meet an unforgettable cast of characters along the way. They are the grounding elements to an otherwise wide-open adventure that lets you craft a narrative only you can craft. However you invest your time in this massive, sprawling medieval landscape will be reflected in the Henry you play as and how the world responds around you – there’s no shortage of mischief and chivalry to get up to, and across its dozens of hours, you’ll be taken through one of the most rewarding open games you can play today.  | Our Review Elden Ring PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PlayStation 4, Xbox One, PC FromSoftware proves its punishing yet satisfying style of action RPG absolutely works in an open-world format. Unlike traditional Souls games, slamming against a difficult boss isn’t the end of the line. You can simply explore elsewhere and fall down the rabbit holes of breaching an impregnable fortress, surviving a treacherous swamp, or taking down dozens upon dozens of other imposing enemies. I finally did it.As Princess Zelda held the transformed, monstrous Ganon in place with her magic, I delivered the final blow with the Master Sword. The Demon King was sealed away and Hyrule was saved. I found myself getting emotional, because this was the first time in 28 years that I had beaten The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time.  For many, that would mean it was also the first time they had played Ocarina of Time, but this wasn't the case for me. I first played the game, if you can call running around Kokiri Forest "playing the game," back around launch in 1998, when I was four years old. It was my older brother's Nintendo 64, and his copy of the game, which he had already completed. My adventures in Hyrule primarily consisted of exploring Hyrule Field and switching between the child and adult timelines. I even loved flipping through my brother's strategy guide to the point that my mom got it spiral bound at Kinko's.  But I never made any progress. The animation of Link pulling the Master Sword from its marble resting place was mesmerizing to me, but to be completely honest … Ganondorf terrified me too much to want to actually face him in the climactic battle. I distinctly remember a moment at summer camp a short time later, when each camper was asked to share a fear. Some were afraid of snakes. Some picked spiders or the dark. I said "Ganondorf" without a second thought, and I meant it. He was terrifying! His evil smirk when looking down on Link as he chased Zelda and Impa out of the castle was burned into my brain.  Terrifying I started and ultimately fizzled out on a few more playthroughs on Nintendo 64, and eventually picked up a new version of Ocarina of Time for GameCube a few years later--the Legend of Zelda: Collector's Edition, which was pretty much the same experience but with a better controller. Still, I found myself struggling to get any further, and I put the game aside in favor of Super Smash Bros. I sure wish I hadn't opened it, too, considering how ridiculously valuable it is today.  It would be many years before I made another attempt, this time on 3DS. This version was marketed as a big improvement over the original Nintendo 64 release, and it's hard to deny that, even from the game's early moments. The motion controls on 3DS made aiming the slingshot remarkably easy, and the much-needed visual overhaul made the characters seem more like people. But still, despite loving what I played, I drifted away. This time, it was shortly after finishing the Forest Temple, and though I planned to go back and finish the rest of the game, that never happened.  As the years passed, I had resigned myself to the fact, or what I thought was a fact, that I would never finish Ocarina of Time. I still had a great appreciation for what it brought to the medium, but I wasn't champing at the bit to give it another try.  That was, of course, until Nintendo decided to remake the game again, this time on Nintendo Switch 2. Caught up in the excitement of so many of my friends and colleagues playing Ocarina of Time again, I decided to give it another shot--and to be completely honest, I wasn't sure things would be any different this time.  Maybe it was everyone around me getting caught up in the excitement of playing Ocarina of Time together. Maybe it was being able to sing the songs instead of having to press the buttons. It might have just been that the game had less friction this time, with the motion-controlled aiming and movable camera just making it more enjoyable to play.  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_xMM7L2XoMc It was likely some combination of all of these factors that kept me hooked past when I would usually burn out and move on to a different game. After making it past the Forest Temple, Fire Temple, and Water Temple, it occurred to me that I was actually nearing the final battle with Ganondorf. A few hours later, I had all the Sages on board, and I made my way into Ganondorf's imposing tower.Just from absorbing gaming pop-culture over the years, I knew to expect his aggressive organ music as I climbed to the top, but it still felt monumental. Frankly, I even already knew the strategy for beating him, but finally getting to do it myself--and then deliver the "real" final blow against his monstrous Ganon form--was like finally closing a chapter in my life. Ocarina of Time was one of the first video games I can remember watching someone play, and I probably would never have finally seen the credits roll if this remake didn't exist.  Thanks, Nintendo.

Ahead of the holiday season, we gathered some of our favorite video game recommendations focused on family interaction, spoke with Jackbox Games and Halfbrick (creators of Bluey’s Quest for the Gold Pen) about what it takes to make a game that everyone can enjoy together, and even have tips for new parent gamers hoping to introduce kids to their (and our) favorite hobby.From its inception, video games were created to encourage social interaction. Tennis for Two in 1958, arguably the first video game, had two players compete in digital tennis against one another. The single-player video game came later, and as much as we love them, some of our best gaming memories are centered around playing with family and friends. And as we get older, we’re making new gaming memories with our own kids.Making video games is hard. That any game crosses the finish line is a miracle of teamwork and perseverance, and the hardest trick of all is making the final product feel effortless. Arguably, none are more practiced at feeling like effortless joy machines than the games that effectively include players of all ages and skill levels in the experience. And to take it a step further, Jackbox Games is perhaps the best at this sub-genre of video games: inclusive party games that are genuinely for everyone.Jackbox Games studio creative director Arnie Niekamp sheepishly and humbly agrees when I offer this accolade and also says thank you. “It really matters to us,” Niekamp says. “We really value, first, just inclusivity in general, and are always trying to think of how we can make our games playable by as many people as possible, or how we can expand that.” Jackbox Party Pack 12 Games For Literally EveryoneJackbox Games has released its 12th Party Pack by the time you’re reading this issue, and part of the secret of that ongoing success is that its creators stick with the company for a long time. “The person that came in and started making games as a young single person also ages into having a family, having kids, and having that perspective of, ‘Oh wait, it’s also really fun to play these games with my kids, with my family.’”Even if a game is primarily focused towards young players, being fun for everyone is “very important” Halfbrick writer Brendan Deboy says when talking about making Bluey’s Quest for the Gold Pen. “Bluey’s humor, relationships and themes give children and parents reasons to enjoy its stories together. We wanted that shared enjoyment to carry into the game.”Brooke Breit, Jackbox’s game product manager and The Jackbox Party Pack 12’s lead, cites that family element as an important detail to making games for everyone. “We playtest our games constantly throughout production amongst ourselves, and that’s one of the markers of, ‘Are we hitting success within the mechanics? Within how it plays?’” Breit says. “And part of that also involves us playing with our own families as a part of that playtesting process [...] All of our kids have a lot of opinions on what makes a really good Jackbox game.”Breit also points out a metric the team has accidentally discovered over the years that has helped them figure out why a game works for all ages when the creation process happens to be going well. “There’s a little bit of… maybe the younger and older crowd also intersects with the drunk crowd,” Breit says with a thoughtful laugh.Deboy also discusses the importance of family playtesting saying, “Children of Halfbrick parents playtested the game, with ages ranging from four to 10. Their parents reported what they enjoyed most and where they became stuck.” And avoiding the stickiness is crucial. “We wanted challenges to be satisfying without any single obstacle preventing someone from seeing the rest of the game,” Deboy says. “If something is too difficult, they can leave it, try another activity and return later. Harder challenges were often tucked away in harder-to-find places, helping players encounter easier activities first.” Super Mario Party Jamboree No Phones (Kind Of)Jackbox also figured out another secret weapon for great family gaming and gaming with kids: getting away from cell phones. Where do we want people’s focus to be at any given moment? And are we giving opportunity and motivation for people to talk to each other and actually engage with each other?”Deboy cites another way to encourage engagement as it did with Halfbrick’s Bluey game. “We wanted everyone to be able to finish the game, while giving players of different ages and abilities things to enjoy. Choose low-complexity exploration games with some freeform choices about where to go, cozy games with building options, or age-appropriate narrative games.
