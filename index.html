Phantom Blade Zero Review In Progress – Shaping Up To Be One Of The Best Action Games I've Ever PlayedNEW

Phantom Blade Zero is an astounding achievement for the action genre. So astounding that I feel pulled to experience everything it has to offer… and that means instead of rolling credits, I’ve been busy completing story-critical side quests and more to get the most complete ending. That’s another way of saying I am enjoying this game very, very much but still need more time to render a final verdict, which is why you’re reading a review-in-progress of Phantom Blade Zero rather than a scored piece. Though I’m not interested in guesstimating a score right now, I know it will be high because Phantom Blade Zero impresses and then some. It is one of the best action games I’ve ever played, and I’m shocked to be writing that. This is a debut of sorts for developer S-Game, which has dabbled in mobile games and smaller-scale titles; it’s the team’s first triple-A release, and its first game of this kind. This studio is so clearly a dedicated student of the genre, analyzing what works here and what works there in other action greats to create something of its own. It looks forward at what action games can do, can play like, can look like, while looking back at the games before it and understanding why those games – games like Devil May Cry, Elden Ring, Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice, Bayonetta, and just about every other quality action game you can think of – continue to be ones we hold so highly. Should it stick the landing, Phantom Blade Zero will join that echelon of action pedigree.  Protagonist Soul is a quiet, mostly stoic martial arts master who prefers to let his blades (of which there are so, so many) do the talking. The Order he’s sworn his life to betrays him, and, in an effort to right wrongs, he discovers a villainous conspiracy to seize full power of the blood-soaked Chinese martial underground known as Wulin. As a result, puppets, secret sects, powerful god-like creatures, monstrous beasts, and much more prowl Wulin’s various biomes. I haven’t kept count, but there are a massive number of enemies to take down, both standard mob variants and bosses, and the game stays fresh throughout. It's even fresher when you consider how many weapons you can acquire on Soul’s journey. Every weapon is wholly unique, like picking up a new character in a fighting game, each with its own moveset and abilities. Upgrading these weapons through various tiers unlocks additional abilities, and you can even Soul Temper weapons to add one final, game-changing ability or perk. A fully upgraded weapon can have a moveset that uses the majority of buttons on your controller. You can do the same with Phantom Edges, special tide-turning weapons with limited uses (before you build up additional charges). Combined with different accessories and medicine types, Phantom Blade Zero offers hundreds, perhaps thousands, of combat kit permutations. You can play as a drunken fighter who switches between sober, tipsy, and drunken states, with his range and damage output changing as a result; you can focus on a bleed build, ripping enemies to shreds with charged attacks that utilize Soul's own HP to deal extra damage (that can then be earned back with bleed); you can use lightning, wind, fire, and ice to create elemental storms against enemies, and you can use a sword that switches between different elemental states on command as well. You can also just pound enemies to pulp with Phantom Edge fists, too. I’ve struggled to stop using these fists since acquiring them because they’re so satisfying.  Forty-plus hours into Phantom Blade Zero, it feels like I’ve played as dozens of different Souls because I've discovered so many build types. S-Game smartly pushes you to try new builds and weapons as you get them, doling out generous upgrade materials and currency; it encourages you to try everything, and the next weapon might just become your new favorite. Finding these materials (and additional fights) is great fun, too, because Phantom Blade Zero’s various open-area biomes are a genuine joy to explore. You are always rewarded for taking the optional path, and those rewards are always worthwhile. You might find a healthy pile of upgrade materials, or a boss with a weapon you’ll fall in love with. The way buildings, alleys, caves, cliffsides, and other environmental features weave in and out of each other evokes FromSoftware’s architectural design and the subsequent shortcut “aha!” moments. That said, Phantom Blade Zero is definitively not a Soulslike, though you’d be forgiven for assuming as much given its challenging suite of bosses, interconnected world, and Phantom Bell checkpoints, which are this game’s equivalent of bonfires. However, its challenge is much more surmountable (with various difficulties and accessories to ease parry and dodge timing), and its world progression is materially different. Kill an enemy and it’s dead for good, which to my surprise, adds a sense of worldbuilding I didn’t know I wanted. Progression through this journey feels tangible because I’m actually removing traces of evil with my weapons, traces that don’t respawn when I interact with checkpoints. It’s a nice change of pace that narratively speaks to Soul’s efforts. Phantom Blade Zero In-Game Screenshots: Along the way, countless reading materials shed light on Phantom Blade Zero’s intertwining stories, and tracking down the next memory, scroll, or book entry rewards you if you’re interested in the full picture of this Wuxia story. I’d argue the game’s story is its weakest element, but only when compared to the rest of the package. It doesn't break any new ground narratively, but it provides enough mystery and thrill to keep things moving. And while performances are great, talking to characters is still mostly just two people standing across from each other talking through a new quest. Fortunately, cutscenes pick up the slack with dramatic villains, heartfelt revelations, raw tension, and more. Production value is high across the board, thanks to incredibly impressive animation and a beautiful rendering of this dark fantasy China. Faces are lifelike, nudging up just shy of the uncanny valley as close-ups reveal porous details, lip quivers, and more, bringing an excellent cast of Chinese performances to life. The English voice acting has its standouts, but Soul’s voice is boring, so I highly recommend the Chinese vocals. Boss fights draw clear inspiration from Chinese cinema and boast a wondrous sense of awe and scale that only the best in the genre typically offer. There are a ton of bosses, too, with very few repeats (repeats do happen in segmented challenge trials), and it’s impressive to think back on just how many I defeated, and just how many of them delivered a unique fight. I appreciate the checkpoints between boss phases as well. There are so many bosses – nearing 100 or more if I had to guess – and having my progression saved through phases removes some of the repetition I often feel when, say, phase two of a boss continues to kill me and I have to blitz through phase one again and again before taking another stab at it.  That scale and on-screen excitement carry through almost every fight in the game, down to even the simplest mob fights, though these are starting to feel a touch repetitive as I’m barreling toward the game’s end. Soul moves like the world’s greatest martial artist, but your controller inputs make it happen. If you mash the attack button, it looks nice… but if you use each weapon’s prescribed inputs and abilities, Phantom Blade Zero delivers a level of detailed action I’ve seen in few other games. You direct the game’s action, and this lets you turn what might otherwise be just another group of fodder enemies into an electric bout of spectacle.To say I greatly enjoy this game greatly undersells my feelings – writing this review-in-progress is frustrating because I want to get back to Phantom Blade Zero. I expect to reach credits in the coming days, so please be on the lookout for my final verdict this week.

Phantom Blade Zero’s Combat Is So Good, You Won’t Care The Story Makes No SenseNEW
S-Game, the Chinese studio behind Phantom Blade Zero, clearly understands the core tenets of character-action games. If your melee combat isn't tight, responsive, and oozing cool, then what's the poin...
S-Game, the Chinese studio behind Phantom Blade Zero, clearly understands the core tenets of character-action games. If your melee combat isn't tight, responsive, and oozing cool, then what's the point? Some games might have better combat, but few can match Phantom Blade Zero's style.  Every moment feels like you're in the middle of a Chinese martial-arts movie, pulling off moves that shouldn't be feasible--moves that go against what we expect from third-person action games--yet are baked into the mechanics. The game is not without issues, and they do hold it back from reaching the genre's upper echelons, but the highs are stratospheric. Character-action games aren't typically known for telling riveting stories, and Phantom Blade Zero is no different. You play as Soul, a stoic assassin who dies in the game's opening moments when a sword tears through his heart. Soon after, a mysterious figure gives him a chance to come back from the dead, forcing him to choose between two artificial hearts. One will allow him to live but strip away all of his emotions, while the other will let him retain those emotions but only give him 66 days left on this mortal coil. Soul chooses the latter, setting up a 66-day quest to find a cure, unravel a conspiracy, and prove that he was framed for his master's murder. It's a fairly ridiculous opening, but one that doesn't reflect the rest of the game. Much of Phantom Blade Zero's narrative unfolds through background machinations: in clandestine conversations, hidden notes, and secondhand information. Multiple organizations, people, and malicious schemes are in play, but it doesn't do a particularly good job of filling you in, so I found it tough to keep track of who's who and what's what. Some aspects eventually come into sharper focus toward the end of the game, but this feels like too little, too late. You're essentially dropped into an established world where even the protagonist knows more than you do, referencing past events and showing familiarity with characters new to you. All you can do is attempt to piece together some kind of understanding through the notes and loading-screen tooltips, but even then you'll still be in the dark for a good chunk of the game's dialogue.  There are some cool storytelling moments here and there, such as the way it uses hand-drawn watercolor animation whenever it flashes back to Soul's childhood. One sequence also features traditional Chinese shadow puppets (or Piyingxi) to explore a character's backstory. Repeating or explaining plot points during loading screens feels like a concession to its failures elsewhere, though, and ultimately, I found it difficult to latch onto anything that would make me care about the story.  Unsurprisingly, combat is Phantom Blade Zero's bread and butter. It's also unlike anything else I've played, essentially translating the choreographed action of films like Hero and Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon to a playable medium. Both movies are known as Wuxia, a Chinese genre of fiction and low fantasy that blends martial arts with fantastical elements. Wuxia fight scenes are known to be incredibly fluid and dynamic: half combat, half choreographed dance, with both sides attacking and defending at the same time, creating a calculated but frenetic spectacle of clashing steel.  Phantom Blade Zero The choreography isn't something you can replicate in video games without resorting to quick-time events, but Phantom Blade Zero still makes it look and feel like you're engaged in a cinematic, free-flowing dance to the death. You have light and heavy attacks at your disposal, with combos alternating between the two. The key is that these attacks require no commitment.  There's never a point where an attack begins and locks you into an animation until it ends. Switching to defense--either to block or parry--is instantaneous, even if you're mid-combo. Some attacks will even deflect incoming strikes automatically, recreating the back-and-forth, simultaneous attack-and-defend action Wuxia is known for. The whole thing is antithetical to the call-and-response philosophy of so many other action games, but it works when enemies test your abilities. Sometimes, particularly during boss battles, you still need to focus strictly on offense or defense--maybe because you've created an opening to counterattack or need to lock in and parry multiple strikes in quick succession--but the delineation isn't as common as it is elsewhere. Most combat encounters achieve this satisfying flow through speed, aggression, and how combos and parries gracefully intersect. Sure, you could sit back and wait for your opponent to attack, or you could rush in and deal damage while still watching for their attack cues. Every action you take is fast and responsive, and even the biggest, heaviest weapons are swung at a rapid pace. Much of this fluidity comes from the game's excellent motion capture, which showcases each movement with the smoothness and intent you'd expect from seasoned martial artists. It's also complemented by an eclectic soundtrack that infuses the action with everything from Chinese rap, metal, and pop to somber piano pieces and electronic beats. There's mechanical depth beyond the surface panache, too. These mechanics are more par for the course, but familiarity is important when trying something new. Sha-Chi, for instance, is Phantom Blade Zero's stamina system. The meter drains when you hold down the block button or perform heavy attacks, but successful parries will severely deplete your opponent's Sha-Chi instead, gradually breaking their guard and leaving them vulnerable to a flurry of offense. Blue and red flash attacks also denote special, telegraphed moves you need to either parry or dodge, with both tightening up the window for you to react. Failing to do so will eat into your health bar, but depleting your Sha-Chi meter also takes you out of action for a few seconds. To counteract this, switching weapons quickly replenishes your Sha-Chi, letting you maintain the initiative with a different armament. You can equip two primary weapons to alternate between, which not only helps you manage your Sha-Chi, but also gives you more combat options when you consider how varied Phantom Blade Zero's plethora of swords, axes, and spears are. Each one feels unique when executing your standard light and heavy attacks, and they all have various intricacies that unlock as you level them up. For a good chunk of the game, I used a sword called the Outlaw Warblade. With this two-handed weapon, heavy attacks deal increased damage by eating into your health, which switching to standard attacks then restores. It can be a risky but effective weapon to use, but one I wasn't afraid to equip knowing I could quickly switch to another weapon. Whether it's a broken blade that transforms into a ranged weapon after drinking--while also making you move like Jackie Chan in Drunken Master--or a pair of swords that summon the power of both wind and thunder, the weapons on offer are incredibly fun to use. They're flexible, too, with an upgrade system that lets you refund and recover the materials you've spent, so you never feel locked into using a particular weapon just because you've spent time upgrading it. You're free to experiment and jump from one completely different arsenal to the next, which is a more than welcome choice.   Each weapon also has a secondary attack that breaks the non-committal rule. Landing heavy attacks builds Sha-Chi Essence, which you can then spend on weapon-specific techniques called Power Surges. While some of these might enhance your offense for a short time, others unleash devastating combos that you can't cancel out of, forcing you to consider when the opportune time to utilize them is.  Phantom Blade Zero The same is true of Phantom Edges: secondary weapons you can use a limited number of times before needing to replenish their stock. These range from heavy axes that can break through shields and armor to ranged weapons, chained sickles, flying guillotines, and a magical bell that can summon puppets to fight by your side. Both Power Surges and Phantom Edges add another layer to Phantom Blade Zero's flashy combat, upping the ante in desperate situations or providing that extra oomph you need to finish off a boss. Boss battles are where the game's combat truly sings: where spectacle and cinematic action are pushed to the forefront, with free-flowing and thrilling mechanics to back it all up. Whether it's a Lion Dancer that breathes fire and hops between wooden plinths, or a three-faced puppet summoning giant paper snakes from the earth, the bosses are distinct and built on spectacle. It's no surprise that these conflicts represent Phantom Blade Zero at its most challenging. It might not be a soulslike, but From Software's influence is felt here and elsewhere.  The world, which blends a traditional kung-fu aesthetic with steampunk machinery and body modification, is all S-Game. The studio calls this style "kungfupunk," and it's an accurate descriptor, melding pagodas with industrial iron and billowing smoke.  There's variety within this conceit, too. At one point, you traverse a once-verdant forest that's now crimson, stained red after a brutal battle, "as though the forest itself were weeping blood." Another area sees you using a steam-powered boat to navigate a vast lake dotted with islands, while you'll also venture into a town whose denizens have been transformed into wooden puppets, and a winding valley with waterfalls and cliffs overlooking its crystal-clear streams.  It's a strongly realized world, but the level design is distinctly soulslike, with circuitous areas full of branching paths and looping shortcuts. It's one of the strongest examples of this type of level design, making each locale a treat to explore, though it doesn't always feel entirely necessary.  Soulslike checkpoints let you replenish health and upgrade weapons and items, but crucially, they don't respawn enemies as you would expect. It's an odd choice, especially since enemies stay dead even after you die. This changes the dynamic of enemy encounters, essentially letting you whittle down a group of enemies even if they get the better of you. This speaks to Phantom Blade Zero's overall leniency, though. Many of its boss battles contain multiple phases, but if you reach the second phase and die, you don't have to repeat the first. I'm a fan of this decision, because if I've already demonstrated I can beat the first phase, forcing me to do so again can be frustrating.  There are also three difficulty levels to choose from. You can swap between them at any time, which is something I took advantage of to make some of the regular enemy encounters more interesting. This is Phantom Blade Zero's biggest flaw, as even on the hardest difficulty, it's too easy to simply button-mash your way to victory against most enemy types. Groups still gave me trouble on occasion, especially since switching lock-on targets in these instances is overly cumbersome, but as I mentioned earlier, taking out a few enemies is beneficial even if you die.  As someone who plays these kinds of games a lot, this might not be the same experience for everyone. Yet the bosses still posed enough of a challenge that I had to bump the difficulty back down to medium, so it feels more like a wildly unbalanced swing than anything else. It's disappointing because, under the right circumstances, Phantom Blade Zero's combat is spectacular. Its Wuxia-inspired action nails the free-flowing, back-and-forth nature of its kinetic influences with aplomb. Being able to cleave through dozens of enemies who barely put up a fight isn't a fatal flaw, but tedium, and the fact you know how thrilling combat can be makes it a more glaring issue. It's this misstep that holds Phantom Blade Zero back, but it's still a fantastic game that any action aficionado should lap up.

Putting Control Resonant Under A Microscope Is Rewarding In An Unexpected WayNEW
Over many years, I've written tens of thousands of words covering the Remedy Connected Universe, the Finnish studio's world tying the Alan Wake and Control series together. This is a team that loves r...
Over many years, I've written tens of thousands of words covering the Remedy Connected Universe, the Finnish studio's world tying the Alan Wake and Control series together. This is a team that loves rewarding players who notice the smaller details and go off the golden path in search of morsels of understanding. Connecting dots is often much of the fun in a Remedy game.  To my surprise, that side of the developer wasn't on display much in Control Resonant. There are definitely a few nods, and one chain of side quests in particular seems to have greater implications for the studio's future connected-universe games, but overall, I was taken aback by how disinterested Resonant was in tying itself to Remedy's bigger picture. However, it was by looking for those hidden details that I discovered something else I ended up loving about the sequel in a different way. Even before I played Control Resonant, I was up to my old habits of streaming its trailers in 4K, freeze-framing, rewinding, and dissecting every scrap of detail I could pull out. What's its significance? Where have I seen this name before? What might this logo reference? Time after time, I couldn't find anything relevant to the lingering plot threads we were left with from 2024's Alan Wake 2 and 2019's Control.  In retrospect, it makes perfect sense. Control takes place in the Oldest House, a bureaucratic yet mystical space that is one part House of Representatives, and one part House of Leaves. The place was understandably littered with clues about the grander story being told in Remedyland.  But Control Resonant breaks down those walls and sets protagonist Dylan Faden out into the borough of Manhattan. Sure, like the Oldest House, the city is under siege from its own paranatural threats, but prior to that, it was just a city. So why should every inch of it be hiding more details about what Alan Wake or Saga Anderson are up to?"It was important for us that our version of Manhattan has a character of its own," Remedy concept artist Mikko Kinnunen told me. "We were aiming for a timeless feel that plays with archetypal New York elements from movies such as Taxi Driver, avoiding direct comparisons to modern-day reality. [...] It was important for the city to feel like a character that has a life and history of its own, and we did not lean heavily into fourth-wall-breaking elements for this reason." Remedy said some signs around the city are purposely mundane... It was only when I stopped searching for things like hints of what Wake's agent, Barry Wheeler, is up to--like Alan Wake 2 provided--that I could appreciate what Resonant's finer details did offer. In place of puzzle pieces, I found a wide-ranging voice in the city's billboards, posters, flyers, and more.  Even better, none of it seemed to be done with generative AI, which Kinnunen confirmed, adding that there's "a feeling of cohesion you get when everything is authored by artists with a shared creative intent. It’s not just what the artwork looks like, but why is it there in the first place?" Of the 60+ hours I've seen put into Resonant, I've several hours closely examining the city's signage. I've come to appreciate how many of them use photos of the developers, which Kinnunen said were the result of an "internal lighthearted casting round" in which Remedy developers had their likenesses photographed, then used in scenarios that fit their vibe, such as gym staff and restaurant employees.  In another game, signs and billboards using real-life photos of people might be jarring, but Remedy has been mixing media forms as long as it's been around, so I didn't think anything of it other than, "who are these people?" and "it's so cool that they did all this instead of taking an AI shortcut." Similarly, he told me the animals featured on the game's signs are the companion animals of Remedy's staff, as you might have guessed.  ...while others are there for a specific purpose. I noted the wide-ranging styles of the various billboards. Resonant's version of Manhattan wisely does not maintain one consistent voice. Instead, some posters are funny-looking, like an enraged man who was "fired over nothing" advertising a law firm, or a plethora of signs that cleverly use the word "control" as a minor wink at the player.  It reminded me of when the studio disguised the @ControlRemedy Twitter account as a brand of medicine until it was ready to flip the switch and announce the game at E3 2018. Remedy has always loved using names for irony, juxtaposition, or signs of nominative determinism. Just ask Max Payne. Speaking of juxtaposition, I also love how the Federal Bureau of Control's messaging changes slightly when the shadowy organization finds itself public-facing. Kinnunen explained the intention behind that."If you look closely, the FBC has a different tone of voice towards the public than what they do internally. The internal, retro-style posters from the first game have made a return, yet we also have expanded upon them. Some PSAs aimed towards the civilians try to play it straight, yet the FBC still manages to make them creepy and funny by being slightly out of touch with modernity." Does anyone else love fake movie posters in media? I sure do. In every corner of the virtual city, I found intentionality, even as the point was sometimes merely to be plain, as was the case with the names of some businesses, the artist revealed. I could've saved a lot of time early on had I stopped trying to connect non-existent dots between Armistone building company and the Blessed Organization, a mysterious group in Control lore that I was sure would turn up more in Resonant--until it didn't. "We wanted to mix a grounded feel of authentic-sounding names with more bespoke, authored ones. For example, [the in-game art studio] 'Könstig' has a few meanings in the Swedish language. Konst means art, and Konstig means strange (among other translations). 'Taikoo House' [sushi bar] is a favorite of mine," he teased, noting that he didn't want to spoil why.Mirroring my colleague Tauriq Moosa's recent feature on the theme of contrast in Control Resonant, Kinnunen told me how many of the signs players see around the city have a purposeful counterpiece somewhere else, such as the politics-tinged billboards that, in one case, boast that there's "nothing more American" than oil with one that, in another case, demand climate action. "There are multiple themes going on in various Zones that feature similar contrasts as these 'satirical jabs' mentioned. The key is to evoke a feeling or atmosphere without pushing any specific agenda. Growth versus sustainability is just one of many topics in the advertisements," he said, adding that "the themes are there to complement the setting of each Zone. The wealthy financial district of the Evacuation Zone feels different from the more blue-collar Central, for example. Everything is there to make the world feel a certain way, rather than trying to tell the audience what to think." The billboards urging climate action purposely play off of the others that celebrate America's dependence on oil. From theater marquees and coffeehouse chalkboards to sales flyers and calls for political action, the game is loaded with easily missed details, clever nods, and thematic relevance. It's all there, inviting players to inspect it, only with far different results than I assumed when I first began studying Resonant's trailers like the Zapruder Film months ago.  Remedy games have trained me to take a closer look for 16 years now, so my methodology didn't change when I first jumped into Control Resonant. But as clues to my favorite puzzlebox all but dried up completely, the purposeful, human-made details that were there instead became symbols of a new appreciation for one of video games' most creative studios.
