Why Are Fortnite’s Player Numbers Falling So Hard This Season?NEW
Ever since Fortnite started publicly posting player numbers back in 2023, the doomsayers have come out in full force any time there's a decline in concurrent or average player numbers. We're in exactl...
Ever since Fortnite started publicly posting player numbers back in 2023, the doomsayers have come out in full force any time there's a decline in concurrent or average player numbers. We're in exactly one of those situations right now, as player counts for the game's main mode, Battle Royale, have fallen significantly since Chapter 7 Season 4 started on August 14. After the summer season saw a big bump in engagement, thanks to some combination of the sprite-focused gameplay and the nearly entirely new map that Epic dropped at the start of Season 3, player counts have been heading back towards the sort of numbers we saw in Season 1 and 2. In other words: Not great, Bob! According to fortnite.gg, which collates players numbers from the Fortnite API, the average number of players in BR is down 37% in September from August so far. We had the usual big spike of players right at the start of Season 4 in the middle of August, but the falloff has been steady ever since, and the "Fortnite is dead" crowd has emerged once again. But what's going on, really? Is Fortnite dying, or is this just the usual seasonal ebb and flow of player traffic? It's not an easy question to answer from the outside, even with the data we have. To get a better idea of what's going on, we looked at four potential factors. School's back This is one of the more obvious factors we could blame–the kids and college students who had lots of free time to game all day during the summer are now busy with school again. Typically, though, the biggest difference in terms of player numbers when comparing the summer versus. the rest of the year is not that more people are playing, but that they're playing at all hours instead of being concentrated at peak times. For most of the year, there's a big difference in the number of concurrent players between peak hours and the middle of the night, but during the summer, the highs and the lows each day are closer together.  So being back in school doesn't seem to stop people from playing--it just makes them play more in the evening. The data we have for past fall seasons supports this idea, as average numbers largely stayed flat between the summer and fall seasons in 2023, and the averages took a nice jump up for the Marvel-themed Chapter 5 Season 4 in 2024. Player numbers have fallen pretty hard both in Chapter 6 Season 4 and in this year's gaming-focused Chapter 7 Season 4, but it seems like we'll need something other than school schedules to blame for that. Keeping the sprites Fortnite introduced sprites as the main gameplay gimmick in Chapter 7 Season 3, and since player engagement was way up, Epic decided to keep them around in Season 4. This is pretty much unheard of in modern Fortnite, where each season introduces some major new tweak to the Battle Royale formula to keep it fresh, before shuffling it out the door for something new when the season ends. It's not hard to understand the thinking there, however. Fortnite's player counts had been dropping steadily during Chapter 7, to some of the game's lowest numbers ever in Season 2. Then, the sprite-collection gameplay was added for Season 3 alongside a new map, and player numbers went back up. Clearly, the people liked that mechanic, and it was something the powers-that-be could point to as being "something that works."  But there's a reason they basically never keep the same gimmick two seasons in a row–by the end of the season, players are usually over whatever the bit is and ready for something new. That was certainly the case even with sprites as we got to the end of Season 3, but Epic pushed ahead with a new batch of sprites anyway.  Anecdotally, players in my matches rarely seem to engage with the sprite-collecting mechanics in the new season--I rarely hear extractors going off in matches anymore, and my random-fill teammates and I tend to treat the sprites as just another type of loot to find rather than a core mechanic. It wasn't like that at any point during Season 3, even in my overly sweaty matches--folks were grinding those sprites to the very end last season. Too many gimmicks Season 4 has a bit of an identity crisis, because it introduced an entirely new season gimmick while also keeping the one from last season. In a normal season of Fortnite, the new map changes, the override consoles--which let players change the rules of the game mid-match--and the new loot would be more than enough to shake things up. But in Season 4 we have all that and a new set of sprites, each with its own new ability. Having two seasons' worth of gimmicks at the same time has made Season 4 feel harder to comprehend, because it's so unnecessarily busy. With so much going on, it's easy to lose a fight without really understanding what happened or why. It's exactly the kind of situation that overwhelms a lot of players, which then inspires a new generation of Redditors to claim everyone who beats them in a fight is cheating because they're frustrated. And since the sprite-collection gameplay is more appealing to those casual players who are put off by the overly complicated gameplay, Season 4 ends up feeling overstimulating and lacking cohesion.  We saw the same thing happen in the Helldivers-like Chapter 6 Season 4, when the map was filled with alien bugs that made it more difficult to track other players. The numbers fell pretty hard that season, too. They've been relying too much on engagement farming  In 2026, Fortnite has been trying some new techniques for drumming up engagement, the most notable being the Power Hours the game runs in Battle Royale each weekend, which adds some wacky quirks to gameplay, like big head mode, and makes it easier to find and extract sprites. It's essentially a marketing tactic to coerce people to play at a specific time each Saturday so they can get a nice big spike in the concurrent player count, and drag the average player numbers for the rest of the week upward a little bit. But that sort of thing is a bald manipulation, a new layer of FOMO on a game that already has too many of them. At some point, this kind of thing can easily change from being a nice bonus to feeling like an obligation, especially if they continue to center these events around sprites, which--again--was last season's gimmick, instead of coming up with new ideas for them. Maybe we've already reached that point--the August 29 and September 5 Power Hours were the only ones to draw under a million players since the beginning of June, when Epic first started doing them. All this means that the rest of the year will be pivotal for Fortnite. There are several big opportunities for the numbers to recover in the next few months--Fortnitemares in October, the expected mini-season in November, and the start of Chapter 8 in December. The last time Fortnite was able to generate a big, sustained spike in players was in 2023, with the original OG season and the start of Chapter 5. But if Epic wants to pull off that sort of recovery this year, especially with the juggernaut that is GTA 6 looming in November, it'll have to come up with something genuinely special that people actually want to play without having to be coerced into it.

Are you planning to disappear into Vice City when Grand Theft Auto 6 arrives in just over two months? You’re not alone and doing so definitely isn't uncommon. For decades, fans have been calling in sick or taking vacation time when a new game in a series they love drops. For franchises like Call of Duty, which receives new titles on an annual basis, this theoretically adds up to a lot of time off. GTA games, however, are so infrequent that it’s almost become a generational event for one to arrive. It’s only natural that some players don’t want to wait any longer than necessary to experience it. AAA games are more than just singular luxury purchases. They’re an investment of time that the vast majority of people can’t easily make without cutting into their normal lives. That’s why a growing number of gamers have figured out that one of the best ways to fully experience a title like GTA 6 is to make sure you’ve already carved out some time for yourself to play it. Instead, Link simply had to run toward a ledge and hopefully use this momentum to land on a nearby platform without plummeting to oblivion.  While the game was ahead of its time several decades ago, the lack of a jump button is awfully frustrating to anyone hoping to revisit it in 2026. How Exodus Makes Its Choices MatterNEW
The moment one mentions a narrative-driven game, one question springs to the mind of any interested player: Do my choices matter? Video games of all genres are filled with illusory choices me...
The moment one mentions a narrative-driven game, one question springs to the mind of any interested player: Do my choices matter? Video games of all genres are filled with illusory choices meant to make the player feel like they're impacting the world, even when major moments and mechanics are scripted, and that's often not a big deal. But in a game like Exodus, a choice-driven RPG that aims to follow in Mass Effect's footsteps, impactful choices can make or break someone's experience.  I played a few hours of Exodus at developer Archetype Entertainment's Austin offices and came away with a good sense of how the studio intends to give players agency. The systems are mostly what you'd expect from a game like this, but there's one major wrinkle: time dilation. It's a real scientific principle, and the backbone of Exodus' worldbuilding. Essentially, the closer you travel to the speed of light, the slower you experience time. As Jun Aslan, Exodus' protagonist, you'll embark on multiple lightspeed journeys (fittingly called an Exodus) that last months, years, or decades and only experience a few hours, days, or maybe weeks. However, the people back home on the world of Lidon experience time normally, so when you return, a lot will have changed. Your decisions in Exodus can have major ramifications for civilization down the line, and you must live with the consequences of your actions. It's a lens that reframes every decision you make.For example, at the smallest level, you can impact your companions' opinions of you through dialogue. This is pretty standard for RPGs, with pop-ups in the corner of the screen saying "Salt approves" or "Elise disapproves" based on what you choose to say in conversation with them, or with other NPCs while your companions are nearby. You can also choose which companions to bring with you on each mission, giving you more opportunity to raise or lower their opinion of Jun, the protagonist. However, time dilation can impact your relationships in major ways. According to narrative director Drew Karpyshyn, "Not every companion will always be with you at all times [...] And considering what happens when you're gone for years and how these relationships may be maintained is a huge part of the game. It doesn't happen on every mission, but the missions where it does happen – these key Exodus missions that are major points in the story – you have to consider all this."I'm not sure what specific scenarios he might be referring to here, but it's not hard to speculate that there may be decisions where you have to choose which companions to bring with you and which to leave behind. In fact, the damage caused by leaving someone behind like that is the whole pitch of the movie Interstellar, one of Exodus' major influences. It's also possible that you don't get to choose who accompanies you on a journey, and if that's the case, you'll be leaving companions on a planet on the path to a future you've already shaped.To clarify, many major decisions in Exodus involve Remnants, highly advanced pieces of Celestial technology you'll encounter on your adventure. In my playtime, I recovered one called the Logos, which is essentially a highly advanced encyclopedia. Lidon, your home, is a planet built on Celestial technology, and Jun has the choice to either lean into the information in the Logos to build on the planet's foundation or use the Logos to reverse-engineer new tech, making society independent but less advanced. This is what Archetype refers to as a breakpoint decision. You have to pick one of two significant options, and since Jun has some minor precognitive abilities, you can see abstract glimpses of the future before deciding on one or the other. The option to reject the Celestial tech has an image of some dome-like ships, while the option to embrace it shows a preview of some kind of giant snake. Ostensibly, after picking one, I'll see the impact of that choice after an Exodus journey. And if I'm leaving a companion behind, they'll have to live in whichever timeline I've committed to.Jun's precognitive abilities are best seen in the Dynasty tab in the menu, which has a timeline depicting past events and Lidon's ultimate fate. In the early hours we played, Lidon is doomed to be consumed by the Rot, which makes sense – it's the pressure that sets the game in motion. But the screen also shows points earned in three categories, each depicting elements of Lidon's society: green for culture, blue for science, and red for military. Players will face different story outcomes based on each of these levels, and the previews for breakpoint decisions give you a heads-up on which points you'll earn for each choice. All of this is to say that Archetype has injected each breakpoint decision with a lot of variables, and I'm excited about the weight that potentially provides. You're considering the immediate consequences, the long-term story consequences, how it'll impact your Dynasty points, and how it might affect your relationships. It's up to the player to decide which of these factors matter most in the moment, and whether the blowback in other categories might be worth it.However, while my hands-on time gave me a full picture of the factors involved in making decisions, I never saw the long-term results of one. I understand exactly why – Archetype doesn't want to spoil its game's biggest moments months before release – but I can't fully endorse the story's malleability without seeing it for myself. NBA 2K27 Review - Double-EdgeNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: 2K Games Developer: Visual Concepts Release: September 3, 2026 Rating: Everyone The NBA 2K series is perpetually the most frustrating sports franchise for me. On one hand, NBA 2K delivers the best gameplay of any sports franchise year after year; truly, its ability to replicate the action of its corresponding sport is unmatched even by its strongest contemporaries. Unfortunately, the other side of the coin is that its egregious monetization makes the premium product feel more akin to a free-to-play game. NBA 2K27 continues to deliver (and even refine) the series’ incredible gameplay and unrivaled feature set, but like a star player who can’t shed their bad habits, it too often gets in its own way.As with every entry since the series’ debut on Dreamcast, I couldn’t wait to step onto the court with the latest rosters. Outside of times when my teammates weren’t aware enough to avoid stepping out of bounds, basketball has rarely felt smoother in video game form. Calling plays on the fly, setting screens, and finding the open teammate to drain a clutch three never ceases to excite. And you’ll need to ensure you’re using all the tools at your disposal to find the best shot possible if you hope to succeed in this year’s entry. The new rhythm shooting mechanic, where you must release your shot at the exact right time to sink it, adds a new layer of challenge to nailing jumpshots. I struggled at first, but I eventually got the hang of it. Still, I wish it wasn’t an all-or-nothing model; I would vastly prefer it simply buffing or nerfing your shot percentage, rather than outright making you miss if you don’t hit the green part of the meter. Thankfully, if you’d prefer, you can turn it off in most modes and rely solely on your players’ attributes instead. Scoring is made even more challenging by round defensive boosts, which give insight into where the pressure on the ball handler is and let defenders more aggressively contest shots. The offense earns a bump with a revamped Takeover system that rewards strong play with attribute buffs and special skills, as well as more precise ball handling, but even as my games typically ended with sky-high stat lines, NBA 2K27 feels like the pendulum starting to swing back in favor of the defenders.The incredible gameplay extends to the most impressive suite of modes in the genre. MyNBA continues its run as the best Franchise mode in all of sports, with options to play in the modern NBA or various eras spread across the last four decades, complete with fairly accurate rosters, draft classes, rules, presentation, and more. I loved jumping into the Jordan Era and seeing if I could craft a team able to take down the legendary ‘90s Bulls. Regardless of if you’re playing in the modern era or one of the retro periods, MyNBA benefits from improved free agency, contract negotiations, and trade mechanics, improving the gold-standard long-form mode. Though you can't play in other eras of the women's game, I'm happy to see many of the features of MyNBA implemented into the MyWNBA mode as well.On the player-focused MyCareer front, 2K27 delivers a compelling narrative starring a high school prospect who derails their career in a car accident and must rebuild in the NBA G League. When it’s at its best, the cinematic story interweaves elements of the off-the-court narrative into gameplay, such as the point guard who won’t pass to you or a 3v3 streetball tourney against NBA stars at Rucker Park, but it quickly nosedives once it introduces your Running Mate, a player meant to be the Robin to your Batman; I thought I’d be rid of him once I escaped to the NBA, but the story twists to land him on your NBA squad too.  Soon, most in-game goals revolve around monitoring your Running Mate’s stat lines as well; it quickly felt like I was responsible for two careers, and it made me wish I had taken the other MyCareer options and played in a retro era or simply skipped the narrative altogether. I play these modes to feel like I’m controlling a young player who is part of an actual NBA squad, but when a random fictional player shadows you at all times, it shatters the immersion. Speaking of destroying an otherwise immersive career experience, the moment you step outside of your apartment or arena and into the shared hub of The City to either buy new gear or access the online portion of MyCareer, you’re inundated with ads, players zipping around on hoverboards dressed in ridiculous outfits, and even sponsored questlines like one starring Jake from State Farm. It all points towards NBA 2K’s unhealthy relationship with being unsatisfied with the $70 (or more) you already dropped on the game; it also wants you to spend money on VC or, at the very least, see some ads that will earn it additional revenue. Unfortunately, VC serves as one of the key points of frustration for any multi-mode player.To level up your MyCareer player – even if you’re just in single-player – you must use VC. And just like past games, VC is a shared pool for upgrades and purchases across all game modes. I hate deciding between improving my MyCareer player’s ballhandling skill, buying a new pair of in-game shoes at the Nike store, or spending it in the marketplace in MyTeam.   It’s inevitable that certain modes in modern sports games are built around microtransactions and convoluted currencies, but it’s annoying when they break containment to taint largely single-player modes like MyCareer. This isn’t anything new for the series, but it’s disappointing to see the trend continue year after year. In isolation, MyTeam is a fun mode built around the cards you collect from various NBA eras and the WNBA over the course of play. Whether you play online or offline, MyTeam provides myriad ways to add to your collection on top of the obvious “spend VC to acquire cards” route, but at the end of the day, those who do spend the VC will have the better teams.NBA 2K27 aptly builds upon features in prior entries, even when parts of that foundation are less sturdy. The gameplay remains stellar, and MyNBA stands tall as the reigning champion of Franchise modes. Unfortunately, MyCareer’s stumbles and the pervasive level of microtransactions cause me to caveat an otherwise easy recommendation. Score: 7.75 About Game Informer's review system
