Wild Blue Skies Review - Bland HomageNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: Humble Games Developer: Chuhai Labs Release: August 13, 2026 (PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC), 2026 (Switch) Rating: Everyone 10+ Wild Blue Skies is not shy about the video game experience it is trying to emulate. You are Bowie, an anthropomorphic dog in a spaceship making your way through a series of levels, most on rails, some taking place in small open areas, to defeat the evil Grimclaw, an anthropomorphic tiger with a giant cyborg head. Developer Chuhai Labs and Vitei Backroom Inc. are transparently presenting their take on Star Fox 64 – a noble and welcome endeavor – but many shortcomings prevent it from reaching the nostalgic heights of its inspirational arcade action game. Mechanically, controlling your ship feels okay, but it dips into slog territory more often than I would like. The perspective of the reticle makes it hard to see what you’re firing at (and there is no first-person option), and hitting the occasional wall bounces you around like a pinball. It can be especially frustrating on the open-ended levels that are not on rails. Locking on and firing your standard lasers are also mapped to separate buttons, meaning you have to choose between the two actions, which is limiting. Also, the range to lock on to fire an extra powerful homing shot is too short. By the time I locked on to enemies, it was often too late, and the enemy had disappeared off-screen.The look of Wild Blue Skies is attractive, though, with bright colors and cool ship designs. I like the look of the main cast and their performances, along with the performances of all the bosses and secondary characters. Everyone’s characterization, however, leaves a lot on the table. The bosses all boast and yell with unfounded confidence, and I felt bad when teammates complained when I accidentally (allegedly) shot them. But even with the quality acting, everyone is undefined and generic. Dialogue could be swapped among the characters, I don’t think I would even notice.The levels offer some visual variety, but are also generic. Moving from a desert level to an ice level is a nice change of pace, but little has been done to make them feel novel. Also, many of the on-rails levels begin with characters pointing out the limited visibility, whether in a storm or from snow. Narratively, I like flying through a hurricane in a game like this, but when the elements keep making things hard to see, it feels like a story excuse for poor draw distance. When you do make it through the hailstorm, or the tornado, or the lava flows, the bosses at the end arguably take too long to defeat. Like its Star Fox 64 inspiration, there are incentives to replay the campaign to chase different challenges and see the alternate paths. Seeing the game to credits is completely doable, but I got frustrated by moments of unfair challenge. Health drops are infrequent, and deaths are often the result of running into a stray enemy, bouncing against walls, or getting overwhelmed by screen-filling danger that I didn’t feel equipped to avoid. It made many defeats feel like the game’s fault rather than my own, which is especially annoying when burning through lives sends you back to the beginning of the campaign.Bowie and pals’ adventure successfully impersonates the Star Fox 64 experience, but it’s an underwhelming impression. Nostalgic moments and satisfying action appear occasionally, but it pays homage to a fault and doesn’t find space to present its own personality. It has the unfortunate timing of releasing and being compared to this year’s Star Fox on Switch 2 – an excellent remake of Star Fox 64. But even if I did not have that game in my immediate memory, I would be hard-pressed to identify anywhere that it has improved on the genre with design lessons learned since 1997, or even risen to meet the ones that already existed even 30 years ago. Wild Blue Skies Is Star Fox For ExpertsNEW
The "fine, I'll just do it myself" approach has served indie developers well, capitalizing on the hunger for games that emulate beloved classics. Indie metroidvania games, boomer shooters, and retro-s...
The "fine, I'll just do it myself" approach has served indie developers well, capitalizing on the hunger for games that emulate beloved classics. Indie metroidvania games, boomer shooters, and retro-style platformers all owe their rise to the same trend of homage for underserved genres. Wild Blue Skies isn't the first game that aims to fill a Star Fox-shaped hole in gamers' hearts, but it is the one that feels most like pure homage. It could practically be an expansion. Wild Blue Skies follows the formula set by Star Fox 64 incredibly closely, down to even specific details like including alternate paths through the map, all-range mode stages, and a rival company of evil pilots who hound you throughout the story campaign. The very first alternate path is revealed by one of your copilots challenging you to follow them around tricky obstacles, just like Falco in SF64. Even the world map forms a similarly tilted oblong oval with stages jutting out from the easy, medium, and hard paths. The stage design reflects the classic Star Fox sensibility impeccably well, as a fast-paced rail shooter with swarms of enemies and tight spaces to maneuver around and through.  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TdRggRKhNMk Stop me if you've heard this one: A group of hotshot anthropomorphic animal pilots thwarted the evil plans of a megalomaniacal scientist, who then went into hiding for years. Then suddenly the scientist reemerges, declaring war on the peaceful society, and it's up to a new generation of the pilots to stop him. This time, your fighter crew is called the Blue Bombers (no relation), headed by a German shepherd named Bowie, who fights alongside Roe (a deer), Chuck (a bull), and Thorne (a frilled lizard). The evil villain is called Grimclaw, and your own army leader is a toad, I think, named General Harding. It's all very familiar. There are a few key differences, though. Wild Blue Skies takes place on one world, not a series of planets, and so your ships are built to look more like fighter jets than spaceships. Moving through the map takes you across a series of different biomes, so functionally exploring a cold area of the map is similar to exploring an ice planet, but it does feel nicely cohesive here. And even though you aren't actually in a starship, there are moments when your fighter jets make clever use of the space, like in a canyon stage where you pull up hard and fly vertically up a waterfall while dodging boulders falling from the cliffside.  Wild Blue Skies Other differences are less superficial. Your regular fire and charged shot are mapped to two separate buttons, as compared to Star Fox using just one, which is an adjustment I found actually worked really well once I got used to it. The control change required a little more split-second planning to decide when I wanted to use one fire mode versus the other, instead of just holding down the charge shot, but it also made a cleaner division of those responsibilities and less accidental charging. I also turned on the auto-fire from the settings menu so that my regular fire rate was a continuous stream, to differentiate it from the more deliberate charged shot.  Similarly, the brake and dash functions are mapped to the trigger buttons, giving them a feeling more akin to a racing game's brake and acceleration. The change feels a little more natural than mapping these controls to face buttons by default in Star Fox, and allows for a little more fine control to adjust your speed while you're also firing your weapons. Like Star Fox, each stage has a number of medals to collect, on top of your regular score multipliers. These stages are meant to be plumbed repeatedly and extensively, as in the game that inspired them, so there are lots of secrets to find and score challenges to unlock for expert pilots.  And that brings me to one last major distinction. Wild Blue Skies is hard. In a year without a remake of Star Fox 64, I would have assumed I had simply lost my touch, but having recently played Nintendo's title, I feel confident this isn't a skill issue. Instead, Wild Blue Skies is like Star Fox got juiced up to hard mode. Even trailing Thorne to open the first alternate path in the first stage took me several tries. The maneuver takes you through a series of waterfalls, and hitting the exact points where you can pass through, without bumping into the nearby rocks, was difficult to do without very precise maneuvers that took practice.  Wild Blue Skies Your allies are also extremely vulnerable and will fall to enemy fire quickly, so it takes a few times through a stage to know exactly when they'll need bailing out. The all-range mode stages would almost always result in at least one or two of my wingmen going out for the following stage.  This year's Star Fox was a treat for longtime fans looking for a nostalgic fix, but it was a remake through and through. For veterans, all of the old skills translated and the map layouts were identical. If that game left you wanting new maps to memorize and an even tougher challenge, Wild Blue Skies is it. This is about as close to a Star Fox 64 expansion as it's possible to get, with a few smart tweaks to help finesse the expert-level difficulty. It's a very straightforward homage, but in this case, that's to its credit.

Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls Review - The Fighting-Game Comeback Marvel DeservesNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: PlayStation Studios Developer: Arc System Works Release: August 6, 2026 Despite its origins as a comic book publisher, Marvel is fighting game royalty on par with the likes of Mortal Kombat and The King of Fighters. But after the disappointing Marvel vs. Capcom Infinite fell massively short of expectations, and with the release of Arc System Works' Dragon Ball FighterZ giving hyper tag-team fighting game fans a new home, Marvel's reign in the fighting game world felt at its end. Just shy of a decade since Infinite, however, Arc System Works delivers Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls, a fighter that is as much of an advancement to the genre as it is an homage to the Marvel vs. Capcom era.Marvel Tōkon is a 4v4 fighting game, but it’s no ordinary tag fighter. Each team shares a single health bar, but you can effectively play this as a 1v1 fighting game where your team's only purpose is to come in for assists. This helps retain the high-octane fighting fans are accustomed to without the need to memorize four entire movesets. Super and special moves are also assigned to simple button presses alongside more standard fighting game inputs, making it easier to pick up any fighter.Matches have a unique rhythm as they start with your main character and one assist, then you gradually gain access to your additional teammates. Not only does this style allow drastic momentum shifts, but it encourages you to be on the attack to gain control of your entire arsenal as soon as possible, making matches exhilarating at times.  While the hard-fought matches I played online aptly demonstrate the combo system’s depth, it’s still approachable. Despite my typical struggles with longer combos, Marvel Tōkon’s character-agnostic control uniformity helps keep the combat approachable for a wider audience. The characters are instead differentiated by unique traits and dedicated specials, which range from Doctor Doom creating barriers to Green Goblin summoning his glider.Arc System Works is known for over-the-top fighting and absolutely gorgeous visuals, and Marvel Tōkon sits alongside Guilty Gear Strive as one of the best-looking fighting games to date. Arc's signature 3D models that look like 2D drawings remain as impressive as they were back when we first saw Guilty Gear Xrd. Just as Dragon Ball FighterZ advanced on what Xrd did, Tōkon feels like another visual jump.A major part of the visual praise belongs to the character designs, which are all unique, fantastic, and tasteful. The anime-inspired style just works for this game; Iron Man, for example, has armor that looks ripped out of a Mecha anime, while Hulk’s exaggerated proportions and armor make him as huge and intimidating as he deserves to be. Story mode comes in the form of Episodes about teams in a worldwide tournament to take on a cosmic entity, The Champion, who has been travelling the universe and ending planets he deems unworthy. These narratives are told through visual novel scenes featuring the pre-set teams interspersed with fights. Each Episode takes around an hour to beat, and they’re all basic and predictable. Most stories follow the same format aside from a few differences in the Knights of Doom's path.  Episodes also don't serve as a tutorial in the same way story modes in fighting games sometimes do, with only very basic introductions to each character. Tōkon does have fairly in-depth training and tutorial options, but the character-specific tutorials are little more than some combo trials. They are useful, but don't really teach you the full mentality and gameplans behind each character. Online is standard for Arc games: lobbies, ranked and casual matches, plus a silly eight-player side mode called Arcadia Rumble. The netcode is great, with barely any noticeable issues when I've played, even over Wi-Fi. A fighting game's impact is difficult to determine at launch due to balancing updates and the community's approach to it. Given the package Arc has delivered here, though, Marvel Tōkon is a more than worthy successor to Marvel vs. Capcom and Dragon Ball FighterZ. I believe it has the potential to become a fighting game mainstay for years to come. Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
