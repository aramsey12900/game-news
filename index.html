However, Wizards of the Coast has decided to start the week of announcements early, as the company is making big changes to booster packs in 2027, starting with Nauctis: The Sunken Realm in February. The biggest changes involve one addition and one subtraction. The "addition" is God Packs, a type of pack that features only rare and mythic cards, as well as rare alternate art variants of those rares and mythics. God packs will be available in one out of every 1000 regular Booster Packs (which will also no longer be called "Play Boosters," just "Booster Packs) and one out of every 300 Collector Boosters. Nauctis: The Sunken Realm Those Collector Boosters, meanwhile, are the source of the major subtraction. Collector Boosters will now only contain 12 cards instead of 15, with each pack removing "two commons and one uncommon" according to WOTC's official blog post. "The common and uncommon cards were the least-exciting parts of these opening experiences and were often passed over," the blog post reads. "As booster printing becomes more complex, we need to make trade-offs in both complexity and capacity. We wanted to add God Packs and include Japan Showcase cards in Booster Packs, but to do so we needed to find room in our printing capacity to enable that." Normal Booster Packs will also see a change regarding foils, as a foil card will no longer be guaranteed in every pack. Any foils that are found in a Booster Pack, however, will either be a rare or mythic card, which means no more common or uncommon cards in foil in regular Booster Packs. So, if you're following this, there will no longer be a chance to receive a foil common or uncommon in a standard Booster Pack. There will also be less chances to find them in Collector packs, as they're going down from 15 to 12 cards, and all three removed cards are commons and uncommons. "Shrinkflation" concerns for Collector Boosters to one side, it will be interesting to see how the market for those foil commons and uncommons will be impacted. Every foil card in the Nauctis set will now see a new level of scarcity, since the only place you can get them will be Collector Boosters. That kind of defeats the purpose of "common" and "uncommon," but since they're foil, does that mean it's a different playing field? No matter what the greater Magic community's opinions are on these changes, I think there's one thing we can all agree on: Getting rid of the name "Play Boosters" is the right call. It was always awkward. The new pack format will debut when Nauctis: The Sunken Realm launches February 5 in local game stores and big-box retailers.

We Got A Deep-Dive On The Dungeons And Monsters Coming To D&D: World of WarcraftNEW
Dungeons & Dragons and World of Warcraft are two of the biggest fantasy games around, even though they exist in completely different mediums. As such, a crossover between the two is a nat...
Dungeons & Dragons and World of Warcraft are two of the biggest fantasy games around, even though they exist in completely different mediums. As such, a crossover between the two is a natural fit (I bet there are plenty of D&D groups who also run raids in World of Warcraft together), but the form of that collaboration is less certain. In a private presentation breaking down the upcoming Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft project, the designers made their approach clear: this book is a toybox. "This book is absolutely a toolbox or toybox that you can use to fill your D&D game with these sorts of adventures, and I think the dungeons represent that really well," senior game designer Joey Haeck said. "There are a lot of tools at your disposal that I think make it really easy for a DM to explore the world of Azeroth with their players."This isn't a linear, pre-written campaign, but a set of curated crossover locations, character options, magic items, and monsters for players to build their own version of events. Of course, as Haeck mentions, the book also includes some of the most iconic dungeons from World of Warcraft's history, and if your DM isn't interested in coming up with a narrative framework to tie everything together, you're welcome to just storm some castles together. The book also includes advice on building campaigns around World of Warcraft villains, many of which have stat blocks included.Each dungeon is designed for parties of a certain level, defined in D&D as tiers of play. You can venture into the Deadmines and Shadowfang Keep at levels 1-4; Blackfathom Deeps at levels 5-10; Dire Maul at levels 11-16; and Azjol-Nerub at levels 17-20. There's also the Scarlet Monastery, which is designed for levels 5-10 or 11-16 depending on which wing you're exploring, and Onyxia's Lair, designed for characters level 17-20 and centered around a boss fight with the dragon who dwells within. Yes, this is a dungeon with a dragon; as I said, the crossover is a natural fit. Dungeon layouts are pretty loyal to their MMO counterparts, though Haeck mentions they've added new secrets and shortcuts to some, which will be a surprise even to players intimately familiar with the maps. And while most problems in World of Warcraft are solved with combat, the D&D adaptation allows for alternate solutions, like stealth and negotiation, where it makes sense. Still, if you want to go in guns blazing, you're more than welcome to. Some dungeons include random combat encounter tables for that very purpose.The foes on those tables are a mixture of newly designed Warcraft monsters and existing D&D monsters, some of which are re-skinned to fit the Warcraft universe. For example, there's already a stat block for a water elemental in the 2025 Monster Manual, but if you want players to face off against a Murloc, the book includes rules to convert a table of humanoid stat blocks by adding the Amphibious trait and a swim speed. Meanwhile, characters like Jaina Proudmoore, Sylvanas Windrunner, and the Lich King Arthas each get their own unique stats, rated at a challenge rating of 22, 23, and 25, respectively. And while there are plenty of dragons in D&D, their abilities don't line up one-to-one with those in the Warcraft universe, so the crossover will include Green, Blue, Red, Black, and Bronze Dragonflight Dragons to square up against.While the presentation was more DM-focused, it ended with some player-centric mechanics. Tool proficiencies (which can fall to the wayside in many D&D games) are repurposed to correspond to World of Warcraft's professions. End of Abyss Review - Samus-Inspired Survival SuccessNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC Publisher: Epic Games Developer: Section 9 Release: October 1, 2026 Rating: Teen I first played End of Abyss at a Summer Game Fest demo in 2024, and I haven't stopped thinking about it since. The full release is about as good as I hoped it would be, blending Metroid's familiar formula with a survival horror flair, making it stand out even amongst an oversaturated crowd of indies. My playthrough was unfortunately infested with some major bugs, but I enjoyed the rest of the experience enough to largely brush those aside. In End of Abyss, you play as a technician who ventures into an abandoned facility alongside soldiers and their captain. The setting is liminal and mysterious – fitting for a survival horror game – where you must seek out old notes and item descriptions if you want narrative context. The atmosphere is cold and lonely, and even your armed companions seem disinterested in helping you complete your tasks. For a game so light on plot, I found myself instantly sucked into its worldbuilding, cautiously curious to know what was around every corner.It helps that the complex is a joy to explore. End of Abyss is clearly inspired by the Metroid series and borrows some of its best traits. The map constantly winds in on itself, and you're just as likely to discover a shortcut back to a previous area as you are to find a rare resource. Even in the sea of similar indie games, I was consistently impressed by End of Abyss' large-scale level design. There's also a scanner (à la Metroid Prime) that allows you to analyze enemies and search the environment for ways to progress. I don't know what the facility is called, and even after rolling credits, I'm not quite sure what purpose it served, but the setting is one of End of Abyss' most memorable highlights. While End of Abyss is structurally similar to Metroid, it's also a survival horror game with twin-stick shooting. Your pistol has infinite ammo, but you need to craft bullets for other weapons you find (most of which are optional). Every conflict is tense, making my narrow victories satisfying, and the game has just enough enemy variety to keep things interesting, including zombies, bugs, and robots. Different enemy types also have their own weaknesses, so you're encouraged to use your full arsenal of weapons.Bosses, on the other hand, weren't always as fun to encounter. These tough showdowns are balanced well from a combat perspective, but repeated attempts drain your valuable ammo and grenades quickly. On more than one occasion, I had to grind for bullets and bombs before returning. The alternative is to rely on your starting pistol, but most health bars are so large that it's too tedious to rely on the handgun alone. This became less of an issue in the late game, when I had more weapons and therefore more types of ammo in stock. Finally, there's the elephant in the room; my time with End of Abyss was rife with bugs. I got stuck in walls, encountered invisible walls, and even fell out of bounds at one point. The map would also have glitches, marking unlocked doors as locked or omitting certain landmarks, making backtracking confusing. And perhaps most frustrating of all, some doors would fail to prompt me with the "open" button, leaving me unable to enter until I quit and reloaded. The bright side is you save frequently to heal, so resets don't erase much progress. End of Abyss has also already received multiple patches during my playtime, and some issues have been resolved, but it affected my experience nonetheless.Despite the glitches, I loved snooping through the cold, industrial hallways of End of Abyss, and the bugs did surprisingly little to affect my overall opinion. I've been looking forward to End of Abyss for quite some time, and I'm pleased that it mostly lived up to those expectations.  Score: 8 About Game Informer's review system
