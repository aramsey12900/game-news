Advertising and the video game industry have been intertwined for decades. Growing up, I almost exclusively played licensed games that promoted movies, like Lego Star Wars or The Incredibles for my or...
Advertising and the video game industry have been intertwined for decades. Growing up, I almost exclusively played licensed games that promoted movies, like Lego Star Wars or The Incredibles for my original Xbox. I enjoy the occasional round of Fortnite and can see everyone from Halo’s Master Chief to Family Guy’s Peter Griffin while doing so. Still, Xbox’s ad-supported games announcement crosses a line the company never should have.  If you missed it, “ad-supported game streaming” is currently in testing with Xbox Insiders. Those not subscribed to Game Pass Ultimate can watch ads before they play in exchange for one hour of a game they own via cloud gaming. The Xbox Wire post about it stresses that it’s optional, that “ads should create value,” and that ads will be held “to the same quality bar as our content.”  After all the confusing messaging companies like Xbox have fed players recently, it’s hard to see this as anything more than a loss for the game industry. While video games have flirted with advertising for decades, we’re now at a point of no return – there’s no clean way for the industry to come back from it.  The Xbox console UI for streaming games for free with ads Advertising takes many different forms across all forms of media. We watch trailers before seeing a movie in the theater. Ad breaks have been baked into network television and broadcast radio for decades. Waiting and watching an ad has even been ubiquitous on creator-driven platforms like YouTube for a long time. Those kinds of ads aren’t as interactive, though. For the most part, you’re just watching a commercial while waiting to watch the thing you actually want. In comparison, advertising for non-gaming products within video games has historically worked a bit differently, and more interactively. Licensed games were popular for decades, as everything from 7-Up’s Spot (he’s so cool) to the Duck Tales crew got their own video games. When game development got more expensive and licensed games couldn’t just be a rounding error on a marketing budget’s bottom line, the way advertising worked changed. In-game ads appeared in some cases. While Steam’s policy around this is restrictive, sports games in particular have had no problem shilling brands since the 2000s. More recently, Fortnite cracked the code and found that brand collaborations draw eyes to games. Now, you can’t go a week without hearing about Top Gun in Battlefield 6 or The Simpsons coming back to Fortnite.  To promote Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey, outfits based on characters from that movie were added to Fortnite. Licensed games also upped their budgets, so something like Marvel’s Wolverine is the product worth promoting, not the vessel for advertisement. In all of these cases, the ads were integrated into the games we played or interactive in some form. That’s much more fitting for an interactive medium like gaming, which is possibly why there hasn't been a tremendous amount of pushback with these types of experiences. To be clear, this isn’t a defense of advertising within games. I take a bit of psychic damage every time I boot up a game like Fortnite and see the theme song for Phineas and Ferb available for purchase next to a swimsuit Batman outfit. The fact that we generally accept something like The Odyssey outfits in Fortnite makes Xbox’s ad-supported games strategy sting even more. I’m already used to being flooded with in-game advertisements and microtransactions every time I boot up a game like Fortnite or Call of Duty: Black Ops 7. Now, even if I’ve already spent money on a game, you’re telling me that I have to sit there and watch a noninteractive, boring ad trying to sell me something in the middle of what feels like a recession? No thanks.  DC Comics recently promoted itself within Fortnite with swimsuit outfits Sure, Xbox is putting limits on how obtrusive its ad-supported gaming strategy is right now, but I don’t trust that this approach will last. No amount of in-game advertising will ever be good enough for publicly traded corporations that are solely concerned with the bottom line and increasing shareholder value year over year. Microsoft’s CEO has made it clear that’s the goal with Xbox. While this is an issue that plagues all publicly traded companies, not just Xbox, it is the first game console manufacturer to embrace pre-game ads.    We’ve seen subscription services like Netflix and Prime Video integrate ads, only to subsequently charge their subscribers more without clearly improving what they offer. Right now, Xbox is promising that “the core game experience is unchanged,” but its multitude of broken promises over the past several years prove that you can’t take Xbox at its word. For years, the video game industry has been standing near the line of embracing ad-supported gaming, licking its lips in anticipation of crossing. Now, companies like Xbox have little to lose and are gleefully leaping over that line. Ad-supported gaming is not good for us as players, no matter how the companies doing so try to spin it.

It also features one of Japan’s most celebrated samurai, Miyamoto Musashi, as the protagonist – a bold but exciting decision in a series with some beloved leads. While using Musashi, a legendary samurai in Japanese history, seems like an obvious choice for an Onimusha game, Capcom chose the historical figure because of his life (and the stories about it) and how it could connect to Way of the Sword mechanically.  “In terms of picking Musashi for the story, obviously we wanted to have a samurai as the lead, and when we were thinking about famous samurai and the masters of the sword, Musashi’s name came up,” director Satoru Nihei tells me. “But looking more into his background and the elements of his real history, where he would use any means necessary to claim victory or use the environment around him, these are things we thought would feed in very nicely to the gameplay to introduce new elements into it. Even so, Halo: Campaign Evolved does a phenomenal job of capturing what made the 2001 original so much fun while updating certain elements to better suit modern sensibilities. The core game shows its age in some notable ways, but there’s no mistaking the magic that made this formula work all those years ago is still very much intact.Behind the wall of clever sci-fi world-building, Halo’s triumph has always been its insistent fun at all times, sometimes at the cost of perfect balance or sense. Brightly colored weapon flashes trigger chains of plasma grenade explosions. Warthog vehicles careen over cliffs in improbable arcs. Rocket launchers appear in your path at suspiciously opportune moments for maximum destruction. Enemy grunts are almost gleefully eager to charge into your shooting gallery line-of-fire. The action is constant, the set-piece moments and cinematics are bombastic, and the power fantasy never relents. Halo Studios manages to capture that persistent exuberance, and it’s the highest compliment I can offer for this remake.I’m also a fan of the selective efforts to update gameplay to contemporary expectations, including the integration of features like sprinting, equipment, and additional aiming options that didn’t come until later installments. Enemies are more dynamic in their actions, especially as they reveal more nuanced and distinct behaviors on higher difficulty levels. Four-player online cooperative play joins the original fun of two-player local. Either way is ideal for sharing the experience with friends. On the gameplay side, the tough choice not to go further in rebuilding the game keeps the campaign's original flow mostly intact. It must be said, though, that it includes a degree of repetition, backtracking, and narrow-corridor navigation that feels at odds with modern shooters. While it’s no doubt a product of its time, that limitation might help the game feel true to its source material if it didn’t make it feel dated and frustrating to navigate today. I also appreciate the efforts put into offering customization of weapons and armor, and the huge number of unlockable skulls that let you tweak your play experience, including a remix option that offers major changes, like new enemy and weapon placements – a significant bonus to replayability.The original Halo presented an alluring and mysterious setting that combined alien ruins with Pacific Northwest-inspired natural grandeur, but the level of detail back then simply hadn’t yet caught up with the imagination. That’s resolved here, with a gorgeous visual update that makes the whole campaign pop with excitement. Unfortunately, I encountered several instances of framerate chugging and stuttering when the screen got especially crowded, even if the game otherwise ran smoothly throughout my playthroughs. The remastered audio is stellar, featuring excellently re-recorded dialogue and scoring of all those memorable musical tracks.For those who, like myself, find some aspects of the campaign to be feeling their age, the three new bonus missions are a welcome addition. This standalone story, set before the events of the main game, gives us more time with fan-favorite Sergeant Johnson, as well as some of the weapons and enemies that came along later in the series. I enjoy the new location aboard a unique Covenant ship, especially the expansive concluding event, though the scope of these three missions feels less impactful overall compared to many Halo adventures over the years.  Master Chief and Cortana’s journey across the ringworld of Halo was an integral experience in my gaming life, and I’m certainly not the only one. Campaign Evolved is a superb update and rebuild of what made the original great, but it stops short of a complete restructuring of that landmark game. Even constrained by some of the features that defined the 2001 classic, this new version nails the roiling current of chaos that made it so fun all those years ago.   Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
