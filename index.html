Colorado-based developer IllFonic has been making asymmetrical multiplayer games based on iconic movie IPs for most of its existence. Arguably its biggest hit remains its first stab at the genre, with 2017's Friday the 13th: The Game (a great game now infamous for its shutdown), but since then, the team has released Predator: Hunting Grounds, Ghostbusters: Spirits Unleashed, and most recently, Killer Klowns from Outer Space: The Game. Finally, though, IllFonic is making the biggest horror-movie-turned-asymmetrical-multiplayer layup game of all time with Halloween: The Game. If you preorder the more expensive fancy-smancy version, you can hop into the game early on September 4; otherwise, the game goes wide on September 8. I need more time to work through and digest IllFonic's multiplayer offering in Halloween, but I have completed the single-player campaign (the studio's first in one of these games) and wanted to bring you some initial thoughts. A Few Notches Above Glorified Tutorial If you aren't a multiplayer person and were curious about picking up Halloween for its single-player offering... I wouldn't. It lasts just a couple of hours – though you can extend it by replaying chapters to knock out optional objectives and unlock cosmetic rewards – and is just a few notches above a glorified tutorial for playing as Michael Myers. Each chapter introduces new mechanics in his kit that you will then hone and master in the multiplayer side of Halloween. There is a heaping dose of love from IllFonic, however, to the movies, primarily the 1978 Halloween from director/synth player/gamer John Carpenter that started it all, that elevates it just so.As you might expect, a single-player campaign where you play as Michael Myers based on the first Halloween film is, well, a video game version of that movie. If you've never played an IllFonice game before, this campaign will give you the rundown on how its games feel, including the at-this-point-must-be-a-feature-and-not-a-bug baked-in instability. I encountered nothing game-ruining, but like its predecessors, Halloween has a distinguishable jank to it that you will either come to love because it adds to the unpredictable nature of these multiplayer experiences or you will hate because... it's still jank. It runs fine, performance-wise, but I did encounter some noticeable bugs, like falling through floors or being unable to hide bodies in designated hiding areas; I would not categorize this as the acceptable jank I expect in an IllFonic game, and I hope the team is working on patching issues like that up.  Nonetheless, escaping Smith's Grove, killing a mechanic and donning Michael's trademark navy blue jumpsuit, and killing the clerk at a department store so you can steal and put on the William Shatner mask to complete the set is good fun. A filmic look overlaid on the game's true-to-movie visual style sells the aesthetics. Though if you aren't a fan of film grain, you won't like what you see. Performances in the campaign are fine, with Dr. Loomis being the only real standout/named character in the otherwise boilerplate cast who are there to be victims. Running around Haddonfield's suburbs as Michael is good, terrifying fun. In quiet moments, his pained breaths add a sinister air to every step, while screams from nearby houses or hushed breathing in closets sell the idea that you embody evil and everyone is afraid of you. So, too, does the wide array of kills at your disposal. You can stalk victims from afar, completing minigames to raise what is essentially their fear level, and then strike using your knife, your hands, other weapons you find out and about like pitchforks and fire pokers, or even the environment for special contextual kills (like piercing bodies onto telephone poles or throwing someone off a second-floor porch). It remains a good time throughout the short campaign, which ends with Michael escaping Haddonfield to presumably go kill a bunch of random players in online matchmaking lobbies, but it does little more than teach you the ropes within the context of some of Michael's most notorious kills.  I'm satisfied as this campaign is about what I expected, but I would have liked something bigger to dig my knife into for IllFonic's first go at a single-player experience like this. Admittedly, however, that's not what you're here for (or at least, not what you should be here for). IllFonic's games of this ilk live and die by the multiplayer, and though I can see how playing as Michael will be interesting, I still have no idea what it's like to play alongside real players or as a resident running from him. With the campaign behind me, I'll be starting my multiplayer Halloween journey properly.Look forward to my review for Game Informer at a later date.In the meantime, read Game Informer's reviews for Friday the 13th: The Game, Predator: Hunting Grounds, and Killer Klowns from Outer Space: The Game. After that, read our interview with IllFonic about what to expect in Halloween.

Enter it and activate Focus Mode to look for clues. You should find a sack with silver arrowheads and a burnt letter. Reading the letter kickstarts The Firebrand quest, which directs you to the home of a miller. Note: Ideally, you should complete The Firebrand quest during the day. Since Coen is a human, there should be more options to converse with other NPCs. In our playthrough, however, we tackled these objectives at night. The Firebrand: Renna and the mill To do this part of The Firebrand quest in The Blood of Dawnwalker, go to the mill due south of Laslea Glen. You can’t miss it since it’s along the main road that’s dotted with fast travel shrines. If you do this quest during the day, Mert’s wife, Renna, will greet you. She’ll tell you about her cart, which is a way for her to inform you that there are guards nearby and that Mert is already imprisoned inside the Stoneward Post. You can avoid fighting the guards at this stage. At night, though, the guards will be alerted. You can lie to them about your identity, but you should still pick the “On second thought, talk to my sword about it.” Kill the guards and talk to Renna afterward. Ask her about Mert and she’ll tell you about Stoneward Post. Ensure that Renna won't be hassled by the guards anymore. The Firebrand: How to infiltrate Stoneward Post You can save Mert in The Blood of Dawnwalker if you play your cards right at this stage of The Firebrand quest. You can pretend that Mert is “going to be sick,” which allows him to inform you about his manumit contact. This leads to another quest called Shadows in the Woods. Also, now that you’ve met with Mert, you’ll be presented with a quest called Under Watchful Eyes. It’s time-sensitive, which means you only have a few days to break him out of prison. Failure to do so will lead to Mert’s death. But if you do The Firebrand quest at night, you can utilize Coen’s vampiric abilities and traversal methods. Here’s what we did: We went to the back of the tower in Stoneward Post. We had enough stamina to Shadowstep and Planeshift to reach the top. We saved the game and gingerly dropped down the ledges, careful not to land on the floor. There’s a slight opening where you can activate Focus Mode and target the overseer with Voracious Bite. Because the overseer is alone in the tower, you can fully drain his blood and kill him. Doing so lets you loot all sorts of items, including a key. Final Fantasy Resonance Is A Little More Than Just NostalgiaNEW
...
Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: Square Enix Developer: Square Enix Release: October 22, 2026 Rating: Everyone 10+ Nostalgia for the olden days of Final Fantasy is on no shortage. Trading card games, merchandise, and even spin-off games have dipped into the well, including the 2016 mobile title, Final Fantasy: Brave Exvius, which is serving as the base for Final Fantasy Resonance.With Resonance, Square Enix and Lancarse are following in the footsteps of Octopath Traveler 0: taking a former mobile game and giving it a premium game treatment. And while it's certainly a little strange at times, the results have so far turned out well.The big draws for Final Fantasy Resonance are immediately apparent. For starters, it's a new HD-2D game, an aesthetic that's become a trademark of the retro side of Square Enix's portfolio. There are some new advancements, including some 3D mapping using pixels that makes for incredibly gorgeous story moments. And the Visions you can call upon unleash their biggest attacks in elaborate 3D CGI cutscenes, in ways that strangely reminded me of Square's movies like The Spirits Within and Advent Children, though not in a bad way. Those Visions are another draw. During my demo at Gamescom 2026, I had an established party of characters specific to the story of Resonance, but each of them could equip a spiritual Vision of famous Final Fantasy characters from throughout the series, augmenting their move sets. Final Fantasy XV's Noctis was invaluable in accessing more Blizzard attacks for my boss fight, for example, while the Warrior of Light from the original Final Fantasy served as a perfect companion for my party's vanguard, healing and casting protective magic.Combine that with a classic turn-based system, with a stagger bar setup that encourages you to press an enemy's weakness for extra turns, and you've got a pretty well-rounded RPG setup. It would have been easy to stop there: make an RPG, fill it with Final Fantasy references, slap a coat of HD-2D paint over the top, and you've got a decent entry for the fall release season. Oddly though, it wasn't the nostalgia that hooked me the most. I'm an avowed Final Fantasy enjoyer; I've collected the aforementioned trading cards, played numerous mainline and spin-off games, and spent likely hundreds of hours in Final Fantasy XIV. What I enjoyed the most in Resonance, though, was the promise of what will be fresh.Some additions are small but meaningful, like the conversations your party can have while wandering around a dungeon. I really like options like this – the Tales Of series' skits are a great example – and they naturally flesh out the lore and world of Resonance, as well as the relationships between my four party members. I didn't get a big intro to their backstories leading into the dungeon I played, but even just hearing them discuss the ethics of summoning a primal entity to play a bedtime lullaby for the party was a fun way to get to know this crew of adventurers.The battles, too, add a lot of interesting strategy to your conventional turn-based combat. Clever management of the stagger bar against tough enemies, especially the wind-wielding boss of the dungeon I faced, was the difference between a victory fanfare and a full party wipeout. Chipping away at the bar meant constantly balancing who was attacking, defending, healing, and using items to keep the magic flowing.Final Fantasy Resonance falls into an interesting spot, as Square Enix's trend of revitalizing former mobile games into standalone console releases continues. There are some upgrades and quality-of-life changes built up over the narrative framework and building blocks of Brave Exvius, with plenty of series iconography draped over the top; a towering sundae of RPG. The cherry on top, though, is a solid new RPG built on both new and old. I might have shown up because of the Final Fantasy moniker, but now I'm just interested in a good ol' fashioned RPG to dig into this October.
