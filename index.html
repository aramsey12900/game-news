Orbitals Is An Antidote For Hazelight’s Miserable CharactersNEW
Something core to Orbitals stuck out to me as I played the Switch 2 exclusive. Compared to the similar co-op games from Hazelight Studios, the major difference in Shapefarm’s two-player 3D platformer ...
Something core to Orbitals stuck out to me as I played the Switch 2 exclusive. Compared to the similar co-op games from Hazelight Studios, the major difference in Shapefarm’s two-player 3D platformer was that the two main characters, Maki and Omura, actually seemed to enjoy each other’s company. While that might seem like an inconsequential detail, it was actually a breath of fresh air after sitting through Hazelight's co-op hits A Way Out, It Takes Two, and Split Fiction, which all centered on troubled, angsty, and often-combative protagonists. Hazelight's games blazed a path for Orbitals, refining a template of required two-player cooperative play, forced split-screen, and puzzles and action sequences that emphasize teamwork. While the Hazelight inspiration was clear in Orbitals (the game director is even a Hazelight alumnus), Shapefarm’s contribution to the genre contains a warmth and affability that better represent the spirit of what a cooperative game can be. For better or worse, Orbitals doesn’t bog you down with the backstories of Maki and Omura. It’s clear from the game’s opening that they’ve known one another since they were children, living together on a space station ravaged by a cosmic storm. The dual protagonists are basically blank slates with little motivation outside of their drive to save themselves and everyone on the station. And while that left a little to be desired in terms of history and lore, that minimalist approach ultimately served the gameplay experience. Maki and Omura's positive dynamic proves that teamwork truly makes the dream work. Maki and Omura may appear shallow on the surface, compared to the likes of escaped prisoners Vincent and Leo from A Way Out or soon-to-be divorcees Cody and May in It Takes Two, but the magic of Orbitals shows in dialogue. Through the 6-7 hours of the game, the duo imbues the story with infectious positive energy, bantering the same way long-time childhood friends would. They trade jabs, and players can actively spray water or shoot a metal claw at one another, with the characters reacting with playful mock-threats. They go back and forth with cheesy puns (“Our teamwork is getting... rock solid,” Maki exclaims as they lift rocks to use as platforms. “Even though we had a... rocky start,” Omura responds.) and tease one another about the state of their bedrooms. Most importantly, they explicitly trust one another through the entire story. Hazelight’s story formula uses a markedly different approach, taking cues from buddy films in which the two protagonists are far apart in personality. Naturally, that leads to heads butting, constant bickering, and downright hostility--until the story takes them through an arc in which they’re able to gain respect for one another and solidify their teamwork. It’s a sound way of crafting a story for a movie or a book, but featuring characters who aren’t in sync is far less effective in a co-op game. If you’re going to invest hours in a game’s story, I imagine you would play something like It Takes Two with a close friend or a romantic partner. Chances are, your relationship with your gaming partner is healthier than the one in It Takes Two. The persistent negativity from a story like that starts to turn into background noise to skip when you’re just trying to play a fun video game together. The absence of artificial conflict makes Orbitals a lot more palatable for co-op partners. We take on many different roles when we play video games; while some games allow us to craft a character’s personality and background, more often than not, we play as characters who are different from who we are as people--sometimes drastically so. The escapism of living fantasies and inhabiting different worlds is a prime attraction of video gaming. The main goal of a cooperative game is to have a joyful and mentally stimulating experience, working together to gain fulfillment from successful teamwork--and when the game’s story has discord as a foundation for its characters’ relationship, that makes it hard to get invested in the game as a whole. That isn’t to say that Hazelight’s output lacks any positivity. As irritating as Mio and Zoe’s dynamic could get in Split Fiction, there were sequences with fun and whimsy, and seeing the two eventually put aside their (otherwise contrived) differences and bond together created some catharsis. Even though It Takes Two was fraught with conflict and arguments, the minigames allowed for a break from the drama and opportunities for playfulness. Even A Way Out has wholesome moments such as these, with minigames letting Vincent and Leo play Connect Four and competing to balance themselves on wheelchairs--activities that provide fun distractions before the pair brutally guns down the boss of a crime syndicate later in the game. Hazelight deserves a lot of credit for being a pioneer in modern cooperative game design. Yet, it’s other games that follow that studio’s gameplay structure, like the upbeat Orbitals, that get to the heart of what makes playing a game with someone important in your life so special. It would be absurd to suggest that every co-op game in this mold has to be exactly like Orbitals and replicate its approach to characterization. Stories traditionally need tension, conflict, and contrast to compel audiences, and any fictional relationship that completely lacks any of those elements would appear insincere. But one thing that I hope Hazelight and other storytellers making co-op games learn from Orbitals is that if you want players to feel good, consider having the characters they play experience that positivity as well.

The Frustrating Mystery Of Who Makes Nintendo GamesNEW
Whenever a Nintendo game gets a splashy reveal in a Direct, my immediate hype takes on a few forms. I’ll replay the trailer at least once, then I’ll start going frame by frame. I scour Nintendo’s webs...
Whenever a Nintendo game gets a splashy reveal in a Direct, my immediate hype takes on a few forms. I’ll replay the trailer at least once, then I’ll start going frame by frame. I scour Nintendo’s websites for concrete information, then I read up on Reddit comments and theories to see what other people noticed that I might have missed. And then, I watch the trailer yet again. But almost every single time, my brain lands on the same question: Who the hell made this thing? The most recent catalyst for this vicious hype cycle was Metroid Ravenous, a game that looks remarkably like 2021’s Metroid Dread. That game was made in collaboration with Spanish studio MercurySteam, but I saw no mention of that company anywhere in Nintendo’s promotional materials immediately after the reveal. Being the inquisitive and nosy neurodivergent person that I am, my mind subsequently went down a rabbit hole, contemplating why Nintendo is so averse to talking about this. Nintendo’s devoted fanbase is many things, but one constant is that fans will never stop being curious. They are always seeking out specifics, no matter how trivial the subject, from the nuances of the Zelda timeline to whatever Mario’s current occupation is. With Nintendo often being a secretive company, the lack of information it provides only intensifies fans’ thirst for knowledge. And for whatever reason, Nintendo is particularly tight-lipped--arguably now more than ever--about which specific studios are behind its titles. After a cryptic post from MercurySteam, we probably know who's making the new Metroid. This has long been the case for Nintendo, but discussions over who is making what in Nintendo’s portfolio have increased over time. In the lead-up to the release of Princess Peach: Showtime in 2024, talk amongst enthusiasts about the game was dominated by speculation on who was developing it, rather than the content of the game itself. Nintendo was oddly evasive about the subject, saying that players needed to look at the game’s credits to find that out. (It turns out, as datamines indicated, it was frequent Nintendo partner Good-Feel.) The same happened months prior, when it took some sleuthing to figure out who made the Super Mario RPG remake (ArtePiazza, in that case). While this is purely speculative, I can take a guess as to why Nintendo takes this approach. When Nintendo reveals a new game, the company wants you to see it as a Nintendo game–not an Intelligent Systems title, or a Grezzo production, or a HAL Laboratory original. By framing it as such, Nintendo is presumably hoping that you do away with preconceptions relating to the developers’ previous games and make a judgment on the game once you actually play the final product. That practice has the potential to save some consumers from certain pitfalls; for instance, it could avoid pre-release discourse centering on the studio’s track record instead of the game itself. That type of dialogue could lead to people unfairly evaluating a game before it’s even out. I know I would be mortified if someone prematurely judged Next Level Games’ titles solely based on its work on the ill-fated Metroid Prime: Federation Force. It’s also a way for Nintendo to strengthen its brand. Being a Nintendo-published game comes with some level of shine and prestige, no matter who made it. When a Nintendo game succeeds, no matter if it was a partner studio or one of the company’s in-house and generically named EPD studios, you can owe it to Nintendo’s special touch. And when one fails, then Nintendo can own the mistake instead of the studio. But as the years have passed, Nintendo appears to be deemphasizing its subsidiary studios and its development partners, and there’s a magic that’s lost as a result. When you know Next Level Games is behind a new title, you can expect excellence in character animation. There’s a sense of joy I got from some of Nintendo’s GameCube-era titles, which featured the developers behind those games loudly and at the very start. Whether it was Waluigi yelling “CAMELOT!” in Mario Power Tennis or Daisy screeching “NEXT LEVEL GAMES!” before Super Mario Strikers, the pride and passion from these studios was palpable. I always enjoyed how the original three Metroid Prime games opened with “Nintendo and Retro Studios present” before the title screen, highlighting how essential the talent from Retro was to these games. Fast forward to Metroid Prime 4 in 2025, which shortens that vanity card to “Nintendo Presents,” as if it’s ashamed to acknowledge its Austin-based affiliate studio. I can’t even find the Retro Studios logo on the back of the box. At most, you’ll see studio names like Intelligent Systems or Monolith Soft in the copyright notice at the bottom of the title screen. And while you don’t want potential customers to be stuck with preconceptions based on who’s making a game, more information is always better for making informed decisions. I want to know if Good-Feel is making a new Yoshi game, because that studio excels in crafting unique art styles. I want to know if Next Level is making a new Luigi’s Mansion, because the folks there are particularly great at making expressive character animations. I want to know if MercurySteam is behind a new Metroid (which appears to be the case for Ravenous), because while that studio makes a damn good Metroid, it also caught flak for not crediting certain developers, received accusations of crunch and intimidation, and recently underwent layoffs. Nintendo is always going to do things its way–it wouldn’t be Nintendo if it weren’t. But the least it can do is give its fans a better idea of what they’re getting.
