With Friday the 13th, Halloween, and other horror franchises getting video game adaptations in recent years, you might wonder when it'll be Freddy Krueger's turn, but according to Illfonic, the team behind those games, the task is harder than you might think. To do it right, one aspect of the films creates a big roadblock. "There are plenty of IPs that people are like, 'Why aren't you doing this?' and 'Why aren't you doing that?' As much as I love Elm Street, [those movies are] about these big over-the-top kills," Illfonic's chief creative officer Jared Gerritzen told me in a Zoom call. "Same with Terrifier, where it's like these big, over-the-top kills that you would expect that you would have to do [in order] to do it right. That's the most costly thing, in some cases. You'd have to do hundreds of those to build that basis. You'd have to do all these other layers, and then it just becomes like, 'Well, shit.' Like, there are all these quagmires that we can't go into, and so for us, we're very diligent about the IPs that we choose." With Freddy's dream world seeming like a great fit for a game, I'd long envisioned Elm Street as an obvious fit for an asymmetrical multiplayer horror game akin to Illfonic's other titles, but listening to Gerritzen speak on the pitfalls, it occurred to me that he's right. It's hard to imagine a game creating enough elaborate set-piece kills to keep them feeling fresh for players who may play for dozens or hundreds of hours, but neither does it make sense to rinse and repeat the same standout moments like Tina's nightmare that saw her crawling on the ceiling, or the way Glen's bed swallows him whole and erupts with a geyser of blood.  Freddy doesn't usually catch up to someone and finish them off with his iconic glove in a way that maps well onto multiplayer. He makes a meal out of them, and that's hard to translate into a game built on replayability. Gerritzen explained that his team has worked on so many games of this sort that, by now, it gives them something like a running start. "We've been through this so much that when we start off a game, we already know what the pitfalls are. [...] And so we start off with a pretty good skeleton, and then we add those layers of the project that we love. We turn down a lot of stuff because it's just like, 'Hey, to do this, it doesn't make sense in a video game form.'" In addition to those gameplay pitfalls, there seems to be a rights issue tying up the Elm Street franchise broadly, too. Though Gerritzen didn't comment on that aspect, it's recently been reported that competing Elm Street movies may soon be in the works--one for US audiences and one for international audiences--because the ownership of the intellectual property is currently split. That sort of rights quagmire probably scares off some creators, following what happened to Friday the 13th. Does this mean we'll never get a Freddy game? I don't think that's what Gerritzen meant, though it may mean a team other than Illfonic is the one to make it, or it could be that Illfonic finds a way to make it work in the future.  For now, the team is focused on supporting Halloween, which launched on September 8 and has surpassed a million copies sold, alongside its cabinet of other asymmetrical multiplayer games, Predator: Hunting Grounds, Ghostbusters: Spirits Unleashed, and Killer Klowns from Outer Space. Halloween is the latest in a long line of asymmetrical multiplayer horror games to release over the past decade, and like all the others, it's faced lots of comparisons to Dead by Daylight (DBD), the decade-long leader in the genre. It's an "easy" comparison to draw, Illfonic chief creative officer Jared Gerritzen said, but ultimately it's inherently flawed. "It's literally cricket versus baseball. It's so easy to see an image of cricket and say it's exactly like baseball; it's like, really, is it? No, it's not. Like, here are all these differences," Gerritzen told me in a Zoom call. Illfonic is no stranger to this space, having made Friday the 13th,  Predator: Hunting Grounds, Ghostbusters: Spirits Unleashed, and Killer Klowns from Outer Space all before Halloween.  "We make a game bespoke for that franchise, for that group of horror fans, and for the people who love that monster." Gerritzen said that "others" make something more like "mixtapes," which have their own merits, but the projects are too different to directly compare, as some players do. "And it's like, how could you not want something that is built purely for Halloween? Like, if you're a horror fan, if you're a Halloween fan, how would you just be okay with it being a secondary piece? We build things for that love of that movie, and I feel like they deserve it.  "I feel like when we choose IPs, we choose the things that we love, and that we feel could be something amazing, and it's cool that other games exist that put characters in as playable, but we don't want to make it [only] playable. We want to make it Haddonfield. We want to make it Michael [Myers]. We want to make Michael's abilities and put all these characters that were beloved into it." Gerritzen's words hit on something I've been grappling with recently, when I tried to explain how very different DBD is from the field of bespoke, licensed, multiplayer horror games. I love DBD, but I love Halloween, and The Texas Chain Saw Massacre, and most of the others that have filled this space, too. Though there's only so much time in the day, and sometimes one game of this sort does have to beat out another for my free time, I keep them all in my rotation.  "If you're a horror fan, you should consume everything, you know?" Gerritzen added, imagining a similar sentiment in horror movies to prove how silly he thinks it sounds. "'Oh, I'm just gonna watch only movies from this director.' It's like, 'Come on. No, you're not. You're gonna watch everything.'" As for the people who wrote Halloween's obituary before it had even been released, Gerritzen reminded players of Illfonic's track record.  "How many Illfonic-published games [have shut down]?" he asked rhetorically. The answer is none, as he confidently implied. Friday the 13th was shuttered due to a rights issue, but Illfonic didn't publish it, and it remains a unique case in this space anyway. Meanwhile, the team's other games in this genre are still running today.  Gerritzen explained how this talking point has become a refrain for players in this space, and how the team blocks out the noise. "As soon as we started doing Predator, people were asking about it," he recalled. "Same in Ghostbusters, same in Killer Klowns, same in [Halloween], and it's like, cool. All we have to do is prove you wrong. Predator has 30-some-odd predators right now. You know, we're talking about doing some really cool stuff with that. Klowns has all this great stuff. Like, we slow things down for sure. But it's not like Illfonic has ever shut a server down [or] closed down shop. Halloween has a lot of really long legs, and we've got a lot of stuff ready, you know, and it's exciting. So we can only prove them wrong. That's literally all we can do." He starts out chopping wood, checking traps for hares, playing tag with his energetic siblings, and haggling with the local herbalist over his mother’s desperately needed medicine.  I assumed this inauspicious beginning would become a distant memory once I, playing as Coen, imbibed the supernatural vampiric powers of a Dawnwalker. Surely I’m destined to become rich, powerful, and respected, I thought. I’ll become a willing participant to political machinations within gilded halls of renown. Maybe I’ll even get on the property ladder like in The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, and start my own IKEA collection. No such thing happened. Instead, I was delighted to find Dawnwalker rejects that played-out routine of upward mobility. I was never wooed and welcomed into the halls of the vrakhiri; the game's vampire ruling class reviled me to the last. I firmly remained a peasant, an upstart subject challenging their feudal vampiric hierarchy. Dawnwalker may take place in the 14th Century, but the sense of class solidarity I saw makes for a very modern fantasy. From Gareth the Arthurian knight to Oliver Twist, there’s a long line of fictional characters who treat poverty and precarity as a state to overcome and entirely forget once they’ve acquired  wealth and power. Games aren’t immune: My Dragon Age: Origins City Elf playthrough started in the slums but invariably ended in the throne room; And, for all Atlus’s nods to more democratic governance models, Metaphor: ReFantazio’s protagonist still sets aside his humble Elda roots for the Euchronia highlife. With Rebel Wolves being founded by several CD Projekt staff, including The Witcher 3 director Konrad Tomaszkiewicz, The White Wolf is perhaps the most interesting comparison of all. Witchers are treated lower than peasants, yet, despite his claim to eke out a living from patchy contracts, Geralt still gets swept into the ruling class’s orbit. He treats with royals and enjoys their hospitality--even their bathtubs. Remember Geralt’s famous scrubdown? It comes right before his audience with the Emperor of Nilfgaard. Put it this way: If you toss a coin to Geralt, he likely spends it at the tavern or smithy. If you toss a coin to Coen, that poor lad desperately needs it to feed his impoverished family. That defiant attitude toward those who think they’re Coen’s betters shapes Dawnwalker’s worldview of power, class, and politics. It extends far beyond Coen; even accounting for branching decisions, it’s etched across its worldbuilding and systems.  It’s seen in its vampires, who aren’t the kind to inspire class envy like the stylish, seductive, and sophisticated modern vampire. Brutish, cruel, and repulsively ugly, the vrakhiri hew closer to the original Eastern European folk tales that first inspired Bram Stoker’s Dracula. For Brencis and his boyars, eternal life doesn’t come with eternal beauty. But it doesn’t matter when power comes first. Wealth is a welcome, but secondary, consideration. And it’s equally visible across the extent of the vrakhiri's blood-tithe regime, which imposes industrialized cruelty across the valley. Peasants wade into fetid swamps as they farm for leeches, fed on their blood and stored for vrakhiri snacks. Handpicked maidens are trafficked under guard across Vale Sangora for unknown sinister ends. And boyar Bakir runs bloodthirsty battle royales where volunteers fight for his favor. That feudal system of extortion is mapped out across the open world and through the game’s reputation system. You accrue notoriety, not political influence. That metasystem places Coen as a destructive interruption to an exploitative machine, well-oiled with peasant blood. And yet, for all the vrakhiri's monstrousness, Rebel Wolves draws a complicated and unresolvable comparison between the vrakhiri and the valley’s former ruler. For many peasants, the new vampiric rulers are little different to the previous knyaz. Their new lords at least offer protection from the plague that’s taken the lives of millions across the rest of Europe, in exchange for serving as a living factory farm prone to ruthless displays of violence.  After upending the social order and reducing nobles and knights to peasants, the vrakhiri can genuinely be seen as an equalizing force. Life was already poor, nasty, brutish, and short for the peasantfolk. Now that the underclass has expanded to everyone else, peasants rejoice in seeing one-time nobles now play jesters or serve as a moveable feast for the vrakhiri court.  When commoners now play key roles in boyar courts, their loyalties are divided to the point many don’t need to be coerced by fear or propaganda to fight Coen to preserve the new status quo. For the peasants working towards Bakir’s inexhaustible taste for alcohol, or filling barrels full of (former peasant) Ambrus’s blood-fattened leeches, consistent pay beats the hardship of subsistence farming. And when either variation on society treats humanity as livestock to exploit, your choice of farmer can still make a difference. That choice between familiar or supernatural oppression is the moral conflict behind many of Coen’s decisions.  Nevertheless, everyday life continues under the nobles’ shadow. And when you spend time in the communities you encounter, rather than chasing mystical swords, sabotaging boyars, or marshalling allies, it holds weight due to the ongoing countdown to your family’s slaughter. When the game invites you to help your father’s friend Volk--an aged, blind, and disconcertingly horny man--there’s a tangible cost: the lost time. Yet still, I fix his windows and slay the wolves roaming nearby, all for scant reward. When I ask Volk if he needs anything else, the ungrateful codger cheekily tries his luck for a footrub!  The pattern repeats when I pursue a quest in Svartrau City, wherein the only way I can proceed is by spending hours helping nuns tend to the sick and dressing their wounds. These unwanted duties could feel like a waste of time, but I do them gladly. These acts feel earned, like they're more than just RPG busywork; these scenarios replicate communities surviving atrocity through mutual care and favours.  Coen isn’t the only peasant who dreams of taking matters into his own hands. The careless, reckless Manumits share the same intention of taking down the vrakhiri. Drawing their support from the populace, or at least those their rash actions haven’t angered, Crake and his forces don’t easily draw my sympathies or allegiance. For him, peasants are collateral damage. Crake’s win-at-all-costs attitude even tempts him to consider calling for aid from nobles outside the valley. When that act risks recreating the same tyranny that made Brencis the more palatable despot, his ending felt the worst option to me by far--only slightly better than the one in which Coen simply abandons Vale Sangora by leaving the valley forever through a forgotten mountain pass. Dawnwalker’s vampiric metaphor for the ruling classes resonates all the more today, when working-class precarity has heightened to the stage it’s been compared to neo-feudalism: Hypercapitalism extracts labor from our bodies for ever-dwindling rewards, while big tech extracts the expression of our emotions and intellect, just to sell it back to us. Pitted against such overwhelming forces, those old fantasies of joining the elite or effecting change as a benevolent ruler don’t cut it anymore. I’m reminded each time I defeat one of Brencis’s boyars, plunging my fangs into a defeated adversary’s neck, that peasant power is where my class allegiances truly lie. When the monsters and their insatiable greed are real, The Blood of Dawnwalker knows the solution isn’t to join them: It’s to eat the rich.

Silent Hill: Townfall Review - Addressing The TraumaNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: Konami, Annapurna Interactive Developer: Screen Burn Release: September 24, 2026 Rating: Mature Much like the franchise itself, for years Silent Hill existed as an opaque figure embedded in fog, with only crackling radio static confirming its existence. Konami wasn’t sure what to do with it beyond Pyramid Head cameos in kart racers and Bomberman party games. But, like a repressed memory that suddenly manifests as a monster compelling you to address your unresolved trauma, Silent Hill is back, and Townfall, the third Silent Hill game in as many years, continues the impressive return-to-form streak. Simon Ordell’s journey through the Scottish town of St. Amelia in 1996 is not without its shortcomings, but the experience is an affecting one buoyed by the first-person perspective. Silent Hill has dabbled in first-person before with Silent Hill 4: The Room, 2024's experimental The Short Message, and the whole P.T. debacle. We also, of course, all definitely played and remember the on-rails shooter, Silent Hill: The Arcade. Townfall, however, is the first time the franchise has fully embraced the perspective. The view makes the game unique within the Silent Hill oeuvre. Through Simon’s eyes, St. Amelia is overbearing and imposing in all the intended ways. That the game looks great also helps. The claustrophobic interiors are full of details that reward inspection, and you can practically taste the fog while exploring outdoors. The peeking mechanic is also expertly integrated and makes it feel like you truly are cowering and exposing as little of yourself as possible while staying quiet and keeping an eye on monsters.The broad story, at least structurally, will be familiar to Silent Hill players, which I offer as a compliment. Townfall may take place in St. Amelia, but there is no question that this is a Silent Hill game. Simon and others are confronting their inner demons in a foggy town, and what exactly is happening or why the streets are littered with monsters is never truly clear. You do know, however, that something is off and the only way out is through. Uncharacteristically, compared to past Silent Hills, the individual plots that have led to the emotional distress of Townfall’s cast are more transparent. There are fewer questions about why characters feel what they’re feeling. It eliminates some of the dreamlike qualities that make these games ethereal and mysterious, but I am not opposed to more clarity, as sometimes information is withheld unnecessarily. I like the narrative balance Townfall strikes.Simon has an era-appropriate portable television that lets him track monsters through the screen’s static, and it does a good job of letting you monitor creatures’ whereabouts while maintaining intensity. Combat is heavily discouraged, with perhaps the fewest number of bullets in any Silent Hill game (that feature guns, anyway) and melee weapons that expire quickly. As a result, Townfall is a stealth game, but I struggled with understanding what alerted enemies to my presence. Sometimes crouching and moving slowly behind cover was enough. Sometimes enemies saw me anyway. The inconsistency made me reload saves often, which diminished the mood of the encounters. I got particularly frustrated with one invincible, instantly-kill-you-if-caught monster who never seemed to walk away no matter how long I tracked him on my TV while hiding in a closet. In these moments, I was bored and annoyed instead of scared.What I have enjoyed so much about Konami’s renewed efforts with Silent Hill is how it is finding developers like Screen Burn Interactive that understand what makes Silent Hill so intriguing and are managing to produce unique experiences within an admittedly undefined structure. The franchise is about loneliness and trauma and how that affects your emotional state of mind. Townfall presents these emotions well, and my only frustrations arose when I was forced to grapple with some of its unbalanced video game mechanics. Score: 8.25 About Game Informer's review system

In 2006, I eagerly attended the first showing of Christophe Gans’ Silent Hill. I loved Silent Hill 2, but was ready to mock the film, as video game adaptations to that point had been underwhelming, to say the least. In one scene, protagonist Rose checked her map, and I leaned over to my friend and said, “Now she just needs to check the map every few seconds to make it really feel like Silent Hill.” To my surprise, Rose proceeded to check the map on every block as she made it to her destination. The movie leading up to that scene and the rest following it is generally forgettable, but that moment made me pause and realize that Gans had in fact played Silent Hill, and was probably even a fan.Zach Cregger’s Resident Evil made me recall that moment from Silent Hill. A moment that expressed the feeling of playing Silent Hill more than just being a direct adaptation of the property. As promised by Cregger in multiple interviews promoting the film, Resident Evil contains very few overt references to the larger Resident Evil franchise. It primarily takes place in Raccoon City, the Umbrella Corporation is acknowledged, there is a typewriter, and protagonist Bryan uses Heavy Field brand shotgun shells. That’s about it. But the whole film expertly expresses the feeling of playing a Resident Evil game. The intense, terrifying highs, the cathartic moments of catching your breath, the frantic pace, the moments of failure as a player, the reward of simply figuring things out, and even building your skill over the course of the game finally paying off in the end.The movie follows Bryan on the worst night of his life. He is a normal guy (who doesn’t know how to use a gun) trying to make some money on a delivery job while managing his feelings during an emotionally tense event currently affecting his girlfriend and himself. And then he gets stuck in a Resident Evil game – and there is no Leon to whisk him away on a jet ski.From that point forward, Bryan reacts to everything not as a Resident Evil protagonist, but as a player of the games. He stares wide-eyed at unbelievable biological horrors; cannot stop comically swearing as he sprints full speed away from danger; screams at embarrassing pitches when monsters leap from the shadows; makes myriad mistakes; searches rooms for anything that could help him; knows he shouldn’t open that scary door even though he has to; is constantly wasting ammo; and during one of my favorite moments, accidentally gets rid of an item he absolutely needed. The whole debacle (which lasts a sublime 90 minutes) is intense, scary, fun, and most surprisingly, often hilarious.Director Zach Creggor and Austin Abrams, who plays Bryan, nail a very important element of Resident Evil that I believe no other Resident Evil movie has been able to figure out: you can’t take this too seriously. Resident Evil is absurd. The Resident Evil characters are the Leslie Nielsons of this Naked Gun horror universe, and we as the players are the ones that get to scream and laugh and have a good time. Bryan is our stand-in in Creggor’s adaptation, and I love it for that. I don’t need all video game adaptations to be like this. There is undeniable joy in seeing specific elements of a property you love getting the Hollywood investment and being projected on a big screen. I have no idea if it will be good or bad, but I would be lying if I said I wasn’t excited for the Zelda movie next year. But I love that the emotional experience of playing a Resident Evil game has been so expertly translated into a film here. It officially unseats my previous favorite video game film adaptation, which was, of course, Josh Ruben’s 2021 take on everyone’s favorite Ubisoft game, Werewolves Within.
