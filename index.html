Would I still love working from home if I was a worm? Turns out the answer is a resounding yes, and I never would have made this crucial discovery if it hadn't been for Worming From Home. What's Worming From Home? Oh I am so glad you asked. In the midst of September's glut of new releases and the constant influx of new games coming to Steam, Worming From Home stands as an unassuming addition to this line-up, releasing earlier today on Steam. Developed by two people, the physics-based simulation game tasks players with working from home albeit with a slight twist: you're a worm. https://www.gamespot.com/videos/worming-from-home-opening-gamplay/ Yet being a worm doesn't stop you from having big dreams and the ambition to see them brought to fruition. After getting a call from your boss, during which he tells you that after six years you're finally in line for a promotion to senior financial analyst, your worm-self must embark on a mission to up your work output and convince him you're ready for the added responsibility.  Tasks will populate your task board, asking you to do things such as respond to emails from your colleagues or fill out spreadsheets. Some of these will require a bit of environmental exploration, such as a task that asked me to insert my height into an excel sheet, while some might force you to brush up on your math skills and pattern recognition; others might have strict deadlines, requiring you to navigate your environment with purpose and precision. You have to keep finding ways to accomplish your tasks no matter what and keep anyone from thinking you're incompetent--or discovering that you're a worm. And yet, you also have to maintain "worm-life balance." How do you do this? Well, when not working, you can exercise, read, shop, earn new, wormy cosmetics (hats, mainly), and use inside information to illegally game the stock market. You know. All normal, everyday things.  You can also flirt with your co-workers, though, I don't know--that seems like a bad move when you're gunning for a management position, right? But hey, it's a game. Might as well have fun with it and break out your best wormy pick-up lines. Most importantly, and unlike many physics-based simulation games, Worming From Home is shockingly pleasant to control. Yes, it's silly and unruly, but not in a way that inspires aggravation. It's also consistently funny, with a mascot meant to emulate Microsoft Word's long lost icon, Clippy, serving as a commentator and voice of guidance throughout your adventure. Though it still has that endearing "low-effort" (though probably not actually low-effort) feel to it, Worming From Home has a shocking amount of polish and feels like far more than a gimmicky game with a wild concept that you might play for five minutes. Though I haven't finished the game, after 30 minutes it already feels like something really special and well worth the $6 I spent on it. Being in this business, my calendar and non-worm task board is often filled with events and assignments related to the games everyone is talking about: the hot new AAA open-world adventure game, or the friendslop title that somehow sold a bajillion copies in 12 hours, for example. As much as I love finding new games and breaking out of the zeitgeist, it's hard to find the time to do so--and perhaps even harder to find these small, unassuming games that lie tucked in the recesses of Steam. Stumbling upon Worming From Home has been an utterly delightful experience, so now I am here, shouting from the mountaintops (aka, typing ferociously from behind my desk) about how fun this game about being a worm is.

The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of the popular genre. This is Metroidvania Week. When we talk about something, we should talk about it in ways people can understand. You have to be deep in the "gamer" weeds to have any idea what "Metroidvania" means, though. You have to be familiar with not one but two different decades-old game series, and not just familiar with their names or generally aware of their existence, but knowledgeable about how they're designed and how they're actually played.  And if you're that well-versed in Metroid and Castlevania, you know enough to know that neither truly invented this type of action game. And you know that the Castlevania games, the first of which didn't have any of the distinctive traits that define the genre that bears their name, didn't even popularize it, as Metroid did. (Yes, Vampire Killer and Simon's Quest both had Metroid-y elements, but the former wasn't open-ended enough and the latter was too open-ended and obtuse; it wasn't until Castlevania: Symphony of the Night, over a decade later, that Castlevania really jumped on board the 'Troid train.)  Castlevania didn't adopt search-action mechanics until Symphony of the Night So either you have no idea what "Metroidvania" means, or you know that it's not even that accurate a term, and in either case, the solution is simple: Stop using it. I didn't have a lot of hard-and-fast rules during my 15 years editing the games section of a second-tier entertainment site that used to be a third-tier music magazine, but any of the dozens of writers who wrote for me knew of one big one: We tried as hard as possible to not use the word "metroidvania." It was never an absolute ban, and I'm sure if you go search whatever URL those articles currently live at, you'll find many pieces I edited that included it. But I definitely discouraged its use, because this kind of impenetrable jargon guarantees that games criticism will never be read by anybody except hardcore "gamers." And although there's definitely a place for games writing that's directed exclusively at passionate experts who are deeply embedded in the scene, your typical consumer-facing game review isn't that.  Games as a medium have a broader problem involving genre tags, one that isn't as prevalent in other disciplines. It's difficult to succinctly explain a game's aesthetic and interactive elements with a short, pithy descriptor. Even other sub-optimal examples are far better than "metroidvania," though. If you're brand new to games and hear the word "platformer," you might not know why these platforms exist and how you use them, but you can be pretty sure that there are some platforms in that game. "Shooter" and its various offshoots are a little clearer, although you need to add extra language to give a sense of how you'll be shooting. (You still won't know what you're shooting or what you're shooting at, or why, but hey, that's what playing the game is for.) "Horror" might be the most coherent to non-"gamers," but it's so expansive that it doesn't give any idea how you'll experience or interact with that horror. There are no good answers here, but there are a few brain-meltingly bad ones, and "metroidvania" is at the top of that list. Super Metroid is widely seen as a milestone in the genre Some try to justify the second half of the portmanteau by saying that a true "metroidvania" includes RPG-style stats, with experience points and leveling up underpinning the character's progression, as found in 1997's Symphony of the Night. That could be a marginally useful distinction if the term was used exclusively in that context. It isn't. It's used indiscriminately for any game where you have to backtrack to visit previously inaccessible areas, and where your character gradually discovers or unlocks new abilities and power-ups. And even though the first-person Metroid Prime is almost 25 years old at this point, "metroidvania" still immediately draws forth images of two-dimensional platformers. The genre that shares part of its name remains tightly pegged to the flat side-scrolling found in Metroid and Super Metroid.  Even if the original Castlevania were structured exactly like Metroid, though, one simple fact remains: If you're writing for the public, you should want the public to understand you. "Metroidvania" is an impediment to that understanding--and not particularly accurate to the history of these games, either.  Let's call 'em something else. And no, I don't have any suggestions.

The original Metroid and Castlevania both turn 40 years old this fall. As the two series most responsible for creating the “metroidvania,” we’re taking this occasion to take a comprehensive view of the popular genre. This is Metroidvania Week. Metroidvanias are very popular among indie developers, and small studios have produced some of the genre's finest examples. Just one look at Steam presents a dizzying array of search-action games, many of which you probably didn't know existed.  Among all of these titles, however, there are a few that are special: They're made by Team Ladybug, a Japanese development group whose expertise in the genre is outstanding. While they've made games in other genres--like the excellent arcade shooter Drainus--Team Ladybug specializes in the metroidvania, and they are undisputed masters of their craft. Yet when I hear others wax on about great games in this genre, their works are rarely mentioned.  I'd like to do something about that. I'm going to recommend you three of Team Ladybug's metroidvania masterpieces: Touhou Luna Nights, Record of Lodoss War: Deedlit in Wonder Labyrinth, and Blade Chimera. Touhou Luna Nights, first released in 2019 on PC and since released on Switch, PlayStation 4, and Xbox One, is a strong example of what makes Team Ladybug's games unique and enjoyable. Luna Nights is based on the mythos of the Touhou Project--a popular semi-collaborative setting inspired heavily by Japanese mythology that would require an article of its own to fully explain. Leading lady Sakuya Izayoi is a knife-wielding maid who serves the vampire sisters who reside in the Scarlet Devil Mansion. She's sent to a bizarre parallel dimension for reasons unknown--one that strips her of her unique time-manipulation powers. Sakuya needs to get her powers back, get out of this strange world, and figure out which of the Touhou universe's numerous troublemakers is responsible for everything.  Touhou Luna Nights borrows bullet hell elements What sets Luna Nights apart is the integration of distinct game mechanics. While Sakuya starts out devoid of her time-manipulation powers, she gains them back as she progresses, allowing her the ability to slow and freeze time. Different objects, enemies, and hazards react to time manipulation in different ways, and using them well is crucial to both succeeding in combat and getting through some of the more treacherous stretches of the castle.  "Grazing" enemies and projectile attacks--as in, getting extremely close but not close enough to register damage--is also an important skill to learn, since it restores Sakuya's magic meter. This element serves as a nice little nod to Touhou's foundations as a series of bullet-hell shooters. Add in some gorgeously animated visuals, fun upgrade pickups, and a fantastic soundtrack--Touhou's community is well-known for creating killer tunes--and you've got a very satisfying and challenging outing. If you, like me, grew up as an anime-watching teenager of the '90s, you likely remember the fantasy OVA series Record of the Lodoss War--and the most popular of its adventurers' party, the high elf Deedlit. After seeing their development prowess, an editor of the Lodoss War novels--the basis for the anime--approached Team Ladybug about making a game based on the series. The result is 2021's Deedlit in Wonder Labyrinth on PC, PS4, Switch, Xbox One, and Xbox Series.  Deedlit in Wonder Labyrinth is a visual homage to SOTN Making a metroidvania out of a series that has its roots in pen-and-paper role-playing fantasy might seem like an odd choice, but it absolutely works. Deedlit finds herself in a strange environment filled with past friends and foes, a maze built from her memories mixed with those of others, for purposes unknown, that she must explore to escape.  Visually, Wonder Labyrinth feels heavily inspired by Castlevania: Symphony of the Night--with Deedlit's walking animation in particular paying tribute to one of the most influential games in the genre. And while controls and combat also feel very SotN-adjacent, there's a little dash of Ikaruga sprinkled in as well, in the form of Deedlit's wind and fire spirits, Sylph and Salamander. Each spirit grants attack affinity and reduces damage received from their respective elements when at her side. They also give unique movement skills: Sylph allows Deedlit to hover, while Salamander gives her a slide-dodge. While knowledge of the Lodoss War series might be helpful to getting the most of the game's setting and story (and its surprisingly poignant ending), there's plenty to enjoy here for non-fans simply looking for a good SotN-inspired title. Deedlit in Wonder Labyrinth doesn't feel quite as bold in its gameplay choices as either Luna Nights or the next game we'll talk about, but it's packed with action, offers lots to discover, and puts up a solid challenge, while avoiding being overly frustrating. Team Ladybug's most recent offering--and, in my opinion, easily its best--is Blade Chimera, available on PC and Switch. Unlike Luna Nights or Wonder Labyrinth, Blade Chimera is a wholly original story and setting, taking players to a future in which Osaka is overrun by a plague of demons. Keeping them at bay are the forces of the Holy Order, of which hero Shin is a talented member. However, he soon finds himself attached to an unexpected companion: a light-based demon woman named Lux, who can take the form of a giant sword. Lux soon becomes an indispensable companion for Shin--but her demonic nature makes her a dangerous target for the other members of the Order. Between demonic attacks ramping up and a radical faction of humans who believe in coexistence with demons committing acts of sabotage, Shin's got plenty to deal with. Blade Chimera's blends a near-future setting with yokai-like demons Besides the usual high quality of the 2D visuals, Blade Chimera showcases how good Team Ladybug is at creating atmosphere and storytelling. Since the story and characters are their own creation, they're free to take them in whatever direction they please, and they successfully weave an engaging, intriguing saga with great characters and surprise twists. Backgrounds and enemy designs are packed with fun details that make exploration feel even more rewarding, too--I'm not sure why Team Ladybug painstakingly recreated actual movie posters in tiny sprite sizes as background elements, but the team did, and it's fantastic. But what really makes Blade Chimera special is Lux, and how Shin is able to make use of her abilities for movement, attack, and defense. Since Lux is usually in sword form, she can be launched to attack in conjunction with Shin's standard weapons, and enemies she defeats restore Shin's health. You can also use Lux defensively: Stick her in the ground to create a small barrier, or launch her ahead while holding down the button to have her spin in place and attack rapidly or deflect enemy attacks. Keep her in the ground as you move away, and you can call her back to you and deal huge damage to any enemies she hits as she flies to your side. Lux can even help scale walls: Embed her in the side of a structure and use her flat side as a platform for climbing.  Lux also has the ability to reverse the effects of time on certain objects, which lets her move heavy objects and restore broken structures, and--as a light demon--can partially illuminate dark areas. However, she's extremely vulnerable to UV rays, which are often shining in very inconvenient locations. Using Lux's and Shin's movement and combat abilities in tandem is tremendously enjoyable, and the two have fantastic chemistry, both in gameplay and story. Shin's companion demon Lux offers a wide array of special abilities If there's one big knock against Blade Chimera, it's that it's notably easier than Team Ladybug's other offerings--not a cakewalk by any means, but experienced metroidvania players will likely be able to figure out ability and equipment loadouts to make short work of many enemies and bosses. But that doesn't take away from the game at all--it's a fantastic ride from beginning to end, and among my favorite games in the genre. Hopefully I've managed to convince you that one (or all) of these games are worth checking out. But maybe you're still on the fence. While I certainly believe these games are more than worth their full asking price, they often feature in digital storefront sales, so try picking one up at a discount. Hopefully, you'll come right back and grab the rest as well.
