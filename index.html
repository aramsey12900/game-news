BioWare Did Something Very Rare For The Time: It Took Star Wars SeriouslyNEW
In what seemed to be an inevitability, Electronic Arts announced the closure of BioWare this week, including the cancellation of its current project, a future iteration in the Mass Effect series. The ...
In what seemed to be an inevitability, Electronic Arts announced the closure of BioWare this week, including the cancellation of its current project, a future iteration in the Mass Effect series. The writing has been on the wall for some time, and EA's recent sale to Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund (PIF) sealed its fate.  Much can (and should) be said about BioWare's ability to create fully realized, original worlds like those of Mass Effect and Dragon Age. But to write the developer's epitaph, we also have to recognize the incredible job it did as the steward of one of the most popular franchises in the world. BioWare truly understood Star Wars, down to its very bones, and that knowledge resulted in one of the best Star Wars games of all time. I barely even need to mention that I'm referring to Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic. The hit from BioWare was its breakout success, remembered for its rich RPG mechanics and a twist that felt mind-blowing at the time. Without it, we may not have ever gotten modern classics like The Witcher 3, to say nothing of BioWare's own Mass Effect and Dragon Age titles. Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic The creators of Knights of the Old Republic had the vision to set it in a new era of Star Wars, thousands of years before the events of the original movie, separating its story from the main continuity of films that has become a crutch for lots of modern Star Wars stories both in and outside of games. The Old Republic setting was a bold new direction, allowing fans to see the height of the Jedi Order decades before Disney introduced the High Republic era. The worldbuilding was so rich and seamless, in fact, that even though KOTOR itself has fallen out of official Star Wars canon, many parts have been borrowed and adapted into modern Star Wars lore. Memorable additions like deadly HK droids, the barren planet Malachor, the broad outlines of the Mandalorian War, and even the existence of Darth Revan have become canonized into modern Star Wars in recent years.  KOTOR understood, better than any other game of the time and many since, the fantasy of being a hero in the Star Wars universe. Adapting pieces from the world of pen-and-paper RPGs meant you could specialize as a lucky scoundrel or a clever slicer (aka hacker) wielding a blaster just as easily as a lightsaber. And once you did start down the Jedi path, the game provided a wide array of powers to experiment with, which were in part determined by your affinity to the Light Side or Dark Side of the Force. That itself was influenced by the choices you made. And your companion characters fit into the world, complementing and clashing with each other as they brought different ideologies and priorities to your mission. All of these disparate systems went hand-in-hand to make a game that truly felt like you were living within the world of Star Wars. Perhaps most impressive of all, Knights of the Old Republic took one of the elements Star Wars is most remembered for--the twist at the end of The Empire Strikes Back--and found a new angle on the concept. The question of identity and how it interconnects with fate is core to the Star Wars mythos. Luke Skywalker's struggle to accept Darth Vader as his father, and confronting his own darkness, is a key element that makes the original trilogy so meaningful. BioWare found a way to address similar questions with a reveal that packed extra punch because it was a video game. It tailored the moment to the medium.  Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic Since then, many other RPG developers have iterated on KOTOR. Most directly, Obsidian made a direct sequel that was hampered by a rushed production schedule, but has since been modded and patched by fans to restore it to something close to its intended glory. It now stands as a well-regarded story set in the Star Wars universe that poses some unique takes on the nature of the Force and its relationship to its wielders. Other developers have surely been similarly inspired by KOTOR's blend of TTRPG and old-school CRPG mechanics. That's all thanks to the strong foundation built by BioWare. When The Fate of the Old Republic releases, it will inevitably be compared to KOTOR. Studios are made of people, not branding. The talented developers who gave us Knights of the Old Republic have moved on, some of them years before BioWare was closed. But the name still has meaning regardless, and for those of us who fondly remember living out our dreams of heroic quests in a galaxy far far away, BioWare will always be synonymous with Star Wars.

In trying to spin the news positively and toward the future, the memo's attributed writers, chief studios officer Cam Weber and chief operating officer David Tinson, spoke about several of the publishing giant's upcoming games, including something EA is calling Project Gridiron. What is Project Gridiron? The memo's exact phrasing calls it a "social-first open-world experience for our American Football team." In other words, EA is getting closer to something I've been writing about for years: Madden (and possibly College Football) heavily cribbing from NBA 2K's The City mode and giving football fans a virtual social space of their own. The memo speaks of Project Gridiron as a glimmering future prospect, ignoring--or perhaps unaware of--the fact that such a mode requires EA to be a totally different company than it has proven to be. A mode like The City demands three things the EA Sports brand has historically lacked: patience, an authentic voice, and community goodwill. In NBA 2K, The City is a social hub that players can jump into with friends and strangers. In this open-world setting, players play pick-up streetball games and limited-time modes, enter tournaments for prizes, work out, practice, or simply cross the virtual hangout space to get to their next NBA game in their custom avatar's solo career.  Importantly, it's also dominated by spending opportunities, including in-game shops that sell sneakers from brands such as Nike and Adidas, streetwear from labels like Fried Rice and Washed Dads, fantasy items like magician and dragon costumes, as well as branded watches, arm sleeves, sunglasses, scooters, and much more. As rich as it is with things to do, it's also packed with ways to spend money.  EA's gamble with its marquee sports games demands it behaves like a company it's never shown itself to be. In essence, the mode blends live-service gameplay with in-game stores that players actually walk into and browse. It might sound corny or overtly cash-grabby to the uninitiated--and to some ever-present degree, it is--but it's grown into the star at the center of the series' solar system. It offers tons to do; there's always something new and interesting going on. Though I get annoyed when my avatar has to work out in the invasively green-and-orange Gatorade Gym, or when Jake from State Farm approaches me in a cutscene to give me new quests, on balance, it's a mode that's well worth its time. The City makes a tremendous amount of money for NBA 2K, and not just from in-game microtransactions. It's become such a must-play mode for so many basketball fans and friend groups that it's helped NBA 2K outsell EA's football games, despite this country's unique obsession with football. That's because the team behind NBA 2K, Visual Concepts, has spent the last decade and beyond building this mode up into something that could attract so many players. What is today, arguably, the game's main attraction actually has roots in NBA 2K14's mode called MyPark, a smaller, more modest hangout space that later grew into The Neighborhood and, eventually, The City. As mentioned, I've been waiting for EA to crib from this mode, so it wasn't surprising to see the company tease it. In fact, such a mode called The Grounds already debuted in EA's soccer game, EA Sports FC, just last month. The FC team is usually first out of the gate with shiny new tech or transferable features like this, so it makes sense that the brand's American football games will follow. That's the company's pecking order.  But EA Sports games rarely show the kind of patience the NBA 2K series is known for. Traditionally, Madden has lacked a long view. I've heard the team speak in terms like "three-year plan" for the series, so I understand the studio plots what's next in the near future, but nothing Madden has ever done feels like it's built on a longer time scale than that. Too often in Madden's history, a new feature would debut; maybe it would be good, or even not so good, but there would be obvious room for improvement, and then players would watch that part of the game stagnate over the next several years.  Even as the most recent entries in the Madden series have shown more interest in building on each successive game--a trait I credit to the braintrust that returned to the company when EA's College Football series was revived--some things still seem stuck in quicksand. Players eagerly waited an extra two months after the launch of Madden 22 for a draft-scouting overhaul, but when it finally arrived, it was lackluster, and five years later, the same system is in use. Often, EA's way of abandoning features leaves the better ones stuck in neutral, feeding the age-old criticism that sports games fail to innovate year over year, while the more poorly received features sometimes get totally removed after a couple of years. Visual Concepts sees a mixed response as constructive criticism. EA's two-headed football team at Tiburon too often sees a mixed response as good reason to drop an idea completely. In most cases, one of these is the right way to handle it, and the other is EA's way. When other game companies mess with players' money, players notice. When EA messes with players' money, players mobilize. But a social hub mode like The City demands patience. The earliest versions probably won't be great, and they might even be bad if FC's The Grounds and WWE 2K's The Island are any indication. In each case, developers tried to apply what The City does so well to their respective sports sims, but both games' communities reacted negatively, citing a lack of meaningful things to do. They've become painfully obvious signals that these modes were built and imagined first as cash registers rather than captivating modes. As overt as NBA 2K's shopping mall aesthetic can be, there's a lot to love outside the shops. Will EA commit to sticking with Project Gridiron when the initial response is likely to be tepid or worse? History suggests not. Maybe NBA 2K uniquely benefited from being the only sports series to try something like this over a decade ago, so Visual Concepts could build it up over time without any comparisons for players to make. That leaves EA's efforts to be judged in the uncomfortable shadow of The City's tremendous appeal. But it's also been nearly 15 years since NBA 2K first signaled that it was headed toward a social-first ecosystem. Where else in AAA game development does a great idea take a decade or more to be copied? EA was slow to compete.  To my eyes, the company didn't want to invest in something on that scale, and it paid for it. Instead, seven years after the earliest drafts of what would become The City, EA tried to extract similar value from a smaller-scope social mode. It introduced The Yard in Madden 21, a social "street" football mode with no shared social space, few events or attractions, and only shop menus to browse. That mode has since morphed into Superstar Showdown, so it does still exist, but the changes are largely cosmetic--swapping The Yard's grassy outdoor field for a confusing laser-tag look. You'd be hard-pressed to find anyone who cites it as the reason they buy the game. That neon-lit field is emblematic of another issue: EA's cosmetics feel disingenuous, with most paid offerings placed on an uninspiring spectrum from "how do you do, fellow kids?" to garish.  With a straight face, EA asks players to purchase cosmetics that look like this. Some players refuse to spend an extra dime in any game. I'm not such a person. I'll buy skins in Fortnite, outfits in Arc Raiders, and clothes in NBA 2K if I feel like it. But I've never seen anything I wanted to spend money on in Madden. Literally. Not once in a series I play 500 hours a year.  That's not like me, and it's easy to see why. You couldn't pay me to wear them, never mind ask me to fork over my real-life money for such gaudy options. Unlike NBA 2K's cosmetics, which feature some admittedly absurd stuff alongside styles rooted in authentic basketball culture, in Madden, everything carries the aesthetic of a mall kiosk selling meme tees to adolescent boys. How can EA sustain an ecosystem like The City if its players aren't buying lots of cosmetics? While The City offers dozens of brands at once, FC's The Grounds debuted with just a handful. Over time, the quantity of cosmetic options needs to increase significantly, but so does their quality. And if the mode itself is no good, even that won't be enough, because no one will be there to buy those cosmetics anyway. That brings me to the third self-inflicted wound EA will suffer as it builds Project Gridiron: an earned lack of community goodwill. One aspect of The City I've criticized a lot over the years is how it handles gameplay-altering microtransactions. It's not just clothing and mascot costumes you can buy in The City. The series' Virtual Currency can be spent to improve your player. In the NBA 2K community, it's essentially a baked-in tax that all dedicated players pay up front.  You have two options: Buy a bundle of VC once you get into The City, immediately propelling your avatar from a player rated 60 to something closer to 80, or you can buy one of the game's more expensive editions, which include a bunch of VC as a bonus. Either way, for new players in the game's community, it can feel like reading the line items on your phone bill and finding a hidden charge you didn't know you were responsible for. We can't see the exact numbers, but there's no doubt this tactic is a massive money-maker for Visual Concepts and parent company Take-Two Interactive. Walking around The City during the game's launch week every September, it's hard to find players who haven't already paid their way past the initial skill grind. Jump into The City on launch day and see if you can find five people in your lobby who haven't already spent a bunch of extra money. I've previously explained how much this part of the game sucks, despite how much fun the better parts are, and I'm far from the only one who feels that way. Still, the community seems to have accepted this reality. People like me make articles, videos, and memes about the "2K tax," but most of us still pay it year after year because the alternative is being the only low-rated player in your crew and getting left on the figurative bench. Players don't like the hidden fee, but it's been there for so long that most just pay it and move on. EA won't get that same mild response. It's EA. Players love to hate EA, and sometimes EA has given players good reason to feel that way. When EA tries a new money-making scheme, players notice and revolt loudly. Just look to this past summer, when College Football 27 was pissing everyone off due to slower XP gains that the publisher paired with new microtransactions that circumvented those slowdowns. Players protested, and within days, EA removed those paid options, though it said they'll be back. (Good luck to the EA Sports comms team with that one next summer.) Go back a few years and see how a similar rejection of pay-to-win elements in EA's Star Wars Battlefront 2 led DICE to basically rewrite the game's economy in just a few weeks, helping usher in the now-ubiquitous cosmetic-only battle-pass system. When other game companies mess with players' money, players notice. When EA messes with players' money, players mobilize. EA will certainly bring the same pay-to-win scheme we've seen in NBA 2K to Project Gridiron. I'm sure because EA has already brought that scheme to FC's The Grounds. It's even in Superstar Showdown, though, almost comically, it's made less noise there because no one cares about the mode anyway.  The company that gave us viral bad news like "Project $10," day-one DLC, and paid crafting materials in Dead Space 3 simply hasn't earned a tempered response from players, and so there won't be one. Every financial scheme will be forensically investigated by content creators seeking to lay out the greedy math of it all for their audiences.  EA wants to bounce back from the dramatic closure of BioWare and the elimination of 400 roles by pointing to the future. "Today's news is grim, but look at what we have cooking for you," the company hopes to imply. But in chasing NBA 2K's The City, Project Gridiron will need its developer-publisher to be patient, authentic, and generous--everything it's never been.

Tenebris Somnia Review – A Hair-Raising And Entertaining ThrowbackNEW
Reviewed on: PC ...
Reviewed on: PC Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC Publisher: New Blood Interactive Developer: Andrés Borghi, Saibot Studios Release: October 16, 2026 Tenebris Somnia channels the old-school appeal of classic point-and-click adventures, survival horror, and full-motion-video (FMV) experiences, then smartly modernizes them to create a creepy, entertaining experience. Surviving the twisted threats of a demonic dream world is a tightly packed adventure that rewards you with what’s essentially an impressive indie horror film peppered throughout. It’s a nightmare you may not want to wake up from.The blend of live-action and in-game storytelling works well in Tenebris Somina’s adventure, which begins when protagonist Julia visits the apartment of her filmmaker ex-boyfriend, Ivan, after suffering recurring nightmares of him being massacred by monsters. What begins as a well-meaning check-in on his status spirals down an unsettling rabbit hole when Julia’s worst fears are realized and then some. The dialogue-free tale presents a scary but fascinating exploration of obsession, both in art and in romance, that constantly delivers surprising, and at times upsetting, revelations during its eight-hour runtime. While Tenebris Somnia sometimes leans a little too far into “tell, don’t show” territory when explaining the cool lore of its supernatural threats, I never felt lost or confused about the wild things that regularly unfold on screen. FMV experiences are nothing new, but Tenebris Somnia’s live-action scenes are a highlight. They’re well shot, sport impressively gruesome practical effects, and even present visual clues that make them important to gameplay. These scenes are additive, never taking too much time away from the in-game action, and they feel well placed to elicit the strongest emotional response. I also appreciate how the live scenes fit thematically, as much of Tenebris Somnia’s tale centers on a horror film shot by Ivan and Julia that significantly impacted their former relationship; at times, it’s like watching a movie about the movie. While I enjoy smaller moments, like opening a door to trigger a live-action jump scare, the filmmaking succeeds in selling Julia's emotions and the cast's reactions. Seeing real fear, concern, and determination in Julia’s eyes as she navigates a dreamworld made up of Ivan’s memories helped endear me to what would otherwise be a featureless pixel sprite.Gameplay may look unimpressive due to its old-school adventure game presentation, but Tenebris Somnia’s spin on familiar survival-horror mechanics hits the right notes. Taking place entirely within a dreamlike version of a four-story apartment building, you’ll traverse floors collecting and combining objects and deciphering clues to solve well-crafted puzzles. I’m impressed by how well the game’s puzzles make the most of its limited graphical presentation, including clever scenarios involving mirror/screen reflections I won’t spoil, and how consistently creative they are. One devious exercise even utilizes the game’s limited save points as an unexpected resource in a way that changes how you approach your adventure going forward. No matter how strange puzzles become, they are never opaque, requiring just enough out-of-the-box thinking to make me feel smart when I connect the dots. Battling grotesque monsters (which, naturally, look even creepier in live-action) also feels good, with melee and gunplay sporting deliberate animations reminiscent of classic adventure games without feeling mechanically clunky. Taking on multiple enemies by swinging a pipe or blowing them away with a shotgun feels better than it looks, and the most intense encounters end just as they approach the line of becoming overwhelming.Boss battles, perhaps most unexpectedly for a largely puzzle-focused adventure, are fun and distinct from each other, be it wave-based bouts against small gremlin-like foes, protecting a fragile effigy of yourself from a parade of demented house servants, or battling literal mirror images of a tentacled flesh monster the Resident Evil series would be proud to claim. Managing limited ammo and health strikes a good balance of feeling tense but not oppressive. Playing conservatively is rewarding, as few non-boss threats need to be engaged if you can evade them well enough. But when I did find myself down to my last bullet or on the verge of death, Tenebris Somnia lets you feel the pinch just long enough before dropping a box of bullets or a health vial at the right moment.Tenebris Somnia mines the enjoyable nostalgia of playing the horror games of yesteryear without feeling dated in the worst way. Weaving a solid indie horror film adds to the experience rather than feeling like a gimmick. The result is a captivating, well-executed survival-horror romp that maintains a hair-raising atmosphere with a fraction of the pixels of other genre giants.  Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system

Dispatch, a superhero-themed workplace comedy from last year, celebrates its one-year anniversary today. While it was released episodically, the first batch dropped on October 22. It was received well, with strong performances from a star-studded cast (including Breaking Bad star Aaron Paul) and filled a niche left vacant by Telltale Games' absence from the gaming world in recent years. Today, developer AdHoc announced a batch of anniversary content, and some of it drops today. First, there's a new way to play Dispatch: storyboard mode. This option lets you play through the whole experience using the original animatics – rough placeholder sketches that the artists used to sync images up to the voice-over. It's a fun behind-the-scenes look at the game's development that we don't get very often. In that same vein, the cast and crew recorded DVD-style commentary tracks to play over the game, making jokes and sharing insight into the development process. A press release says there are over 16 hours of commentary from the game's development team (like art director Derek Stratton and creative directors Dennis Lenart and Nick Herman) as well as some of the cast, including Aaron Paul, Laura Bailey, and Erin Yvette.Storyboard and Commentary modes are included in the game's deluxe edition, which you can upgrade to for $10.99. Both modes are available today.If you haven't played the game yet, AdHoc has announced a new way to do so; Dispatch is coming to mobile platforms on December 9. It's been rebuilt to support touch controls and vertical play, and it will be available on iOS and Android. You can even play the first half of the game for free – the first four episodes are included, but you'll need to pay $19.99 to play the rest. Finally, AdHoc announced new merch, including a book created alongside publisher Lost in Cult called Dispatch: Design Works, which is both an art book and a "making-of" book. There's also a live game soundtrack concert in Los Angeles featuring Thot Squad, Bershy, Primer, Caroline Kingsbury, Fartbarf, and Dispatch composer Andrew Arcadi with the SDN Company Band. It will be held at Avalon Hollywood on December 3, but it won't be livestreamed, so you'll need to grab a ticket to see the event. The press release states that people who signed up for updates from adhocstudio.com will be the first in line to purchase tickets.We liked Dispatch quite a bit, with our review saying, "it delivers one of the most compelling interactive dramas in years, an adult animated superhero story with the emotional punch of prestige television and a script that truly shines." For more on the game's creation, subscribers can read our interview with the studio from the Ninja Gaiden 4 issue of Game Informer.
