WoW: Forever Is More Than Classic+, It’s WoW 2NEW
Going into BlizzCon 2026, World of Warcraft Classic fans, myself included, were hoping for what the community coined as Classic+. Instead, what we got is essentially WoW 2, an alternate vision for wha...
Going into BlizzCon 2026, World of Warcraft Classic fans, myself included, were hoping for what the community coined as Classic+. Instead, what we got is essentially WoW 2, an alternate vision for what Blizzard's legendary MMORPG called World Of Warcraft: Forever. It wasn't long after Blizzard re-released WoW Classic, the original version of its genre-defining MMO, in 2019 that players started to dream about what might come next. A community that was at one time dominated by calls for Blizzard to make "no changes" to WoW Classic and keep the game as it was two decades ago gave way to an entirely different desire: more.  Sure, it was fantastic and nostalgic to return to the original version of Azeroth and old-school WoW's slower and more methodical gameplay, but what if there were substantial changes and additions? What if there were major class balance changes to make more playstyles viable? What if Blizzard finished zones that were infamously half-baked in the original game? What if some of the zones long teased on the original WoW's map, like Mount Hyjal, were actually accessible? What if there were new dungeons and endgame content instead of the same Molten Core and Onyxia raids players have completed dozens upon dozens of times over the years? The list of possibilities felt endless, but the exact requests and no-nos differed depending on who you asked. As WoW: Forever senior game designer Josh Greenfield told me in an interview at BlizzCon, the "old adage" for Classic+ has always been "ask 10 different people what they want [and] you'll get 11 different answers." https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zerqLHQPN9Y Somehow, WoW: Forever is going above and beyond those community expectations, in often-surprising ways. After going hands-on with WoW: Forever for around two hours at BlizzCon ahead of the game's closed beta on September 17, I can safely say it delivers on many of the changes the community has wanted over the years. But it's also so much more--a fascinating blend of old and new that on the surface may simply look like WoW Classic with splashes of new content, but in many ways looks and feels like an all-new game. What's new? Well, plenty. A visual user-interface refresh that brings WoW: Forever more in line with the modern, retail version of WoW is added, as is the inclusion of transmog. Modern WoW quest UI features have made their way, too, giving players more guidance on where to go and what to do. A whole host of impressive visual changes to lighting, water, reflections, and fog make even old zones feel new, even if the actual lower-poly assets are not. Players even have the option of using HD character models ripped straight from the modern version of WoW, the biggest example of how WoW: Forever feels like a middle ground between the WoW Classic of old and its retail sibling.  WoW: Forever senior art director Jeff Parrott said the goal was for the visual changes to "make the world feel more alive" and to "feel like going home." Every one of WoW: Forever's four new zones are made to blend in seamlessly with existing areas from 22 years ago. "It feels like a place you've been before," Parrott said. "Like, 'Did this exist?' You know, if a player asks 'Did this exist in the game?' then I think we're in a good spot." But Forever is far more than just visual updates. There are around 1,000 new quests. That might not sound like a lot for a world as massive as WoW, but for context, Greenfield said the original WoW Classic has around 3,000-3,500. These new quests are spread across Azeroth rather than only in the game's four entirely new zones. There will be new quests in the game's classic starting zones as well as across the old world, with a particular focus on fleshing out notoriously content-lite zones like Azshara and Wetlands.  The Drowned City is just one of WoW: Forever's nine new dungeons at launch. Forever will include nine new dungeons spread across the game's level brackets and two raids. I got to play one of the new dungeons, The Drowned City, at the show. It's a massive, late-level-30s, early-40s open-air troll dungeon that feels true to the spirit of Classic but also stands as a showcase for what WoW: Forever can be. Right off the bat, I was struck by how much of an impact the game's visual updates could have, with rays of light peeking through a rocky cavern and bouncing off a colossal monster skeleton helping to invoke the feeling of mystery and adventure that WoW Classic does best. Moving further inside, a massive courtyard with a colossal pool in its center showcased Forever's impressive new water tech.  The Drowned Temple feels like a WoW Classic dungeon in the truest sense, with large packs of enemies players need to carefully navigate, lest they pull too many groups at once. For those worried about WoW: Forever's "retailification" of WoW Classic, this dungeon put any fears I had aside. You still need to use your class's full toolkit to crowd-control enemies--pull one extra pack and it's almost certain death. Positioning is still important. You'll still be taking breaks for mana and food after every few groups. WoW Classic's unique flavor of gameplay is alive and well. That being said, there are racial ability and class changes galore, with all-new abilities, class and race combinations (undead Paladins at long last), and reworked talent trees across the game's nine classes. The class changes are significant but aren't as radical as some of the entirely new class roles added in WoW Classic's 2023 Season of Discovery, an experimental season Blizzard and the community viewed as a testing ground for what an eventual Classic+ might look like. There aren't any Mage healers or Warlock and Shaman tanks in WoW: Forever--at least not yet. "I think in spirit, a lot of the lessons we learned are carried over, but some of the more out-there things that weren't originally in original WoW, we tone them back a little bit," Greenfield said. "Because it's one of those things…It's in the name: It's Forever. We can do a lot of things over years, but we wanted to kind of start from a place where the soul of the class is very coded and original-WoW-centric. But there's still meaningful changes and really exciting kinds of ways to play, the existing ways to play your class and new ways to play your class." A new camping system lets players share buffs and craft items while out adventuring. There are even entirely new systems. A Legacy perk tree system, where players essentially earn perk points for completing account-wide achievements, looks to be a real game-changer. By completing achievements related to leveling-up classes, mastering professions, or exploring the world, you can invest perk points to make it so you travel faster on flight paths, gather more profession resources, or even start acquiring talent points sooner. Of all WoW: Forever's big changes, this might be the one that I'm personally most excited about, with there finally being some level of account-wide progress in a game that has traditionally tied all progression to individual characters. A camp system, where players can gather around a campfire to get buffs and craft items, is also making its debut, and it looks to further encourage WoW Classic's more social nature. Somewhat controversially, Forever adds a new playable race that can join the Horde or the Alliance, the Skyborne elves. Their starting area of Zephras Isle makes up one of WoW Forever's entirely new zones, though players do have to pay for the privilege. In a first for WoW, Blizzard is charging $30 for the new race, though WoW: Forever as a whole is simply included in WoW's monthly $15 subscription.  The Skyborne are a new playable elf race coming to WoW: Forever. If there's one aspect of WoW Forever I'm not entirely sold on, it might be the Skyborne. They are cool enough--wind-elemental-loving blue elves who live in the sky. For those who desperately want to play as something close to a high or blood elf in the WoW Classic setting, perhaps $30 is a small price to pay. For me, they don't feel essential after playing through a good chunk of their starting zone, even if it does make great use of WoW: Forever's new visual tech to deliver spooky, fog-covered forests and impressive elven towers peaking above the clouds. The fact they can be members of either the Horde or the Alliance also dilutes some of the faction-specific flavor that defines so much of WoW Classic. Giving players options is clearly important for Blizzard. You don't have to buy the Skyborne. Toggling between HD character models and the game's old-school ones is done with a checkbox. Transmog, a beloved feature in modern WoW that allows players to create custom outfits and looks, is also entirely optional. If you don't want to see player-made outfits, you can simply turn them off to see the mishmash of random gear that has long defined how WoW Classic players look while out adventuring. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IEMrosrAVH0 In a first for WoW (officially at least) you can even play with a controller, which was available when playing through the Skyborne starting zone but not the dungeon demo. Forever's full-fledged controller support works surprisingly well. Cycling through friendly or enemy NPCs is done with the shoulder buttons. Interacting and jumping are done with the face buttons. Abilities and spells are bound to both the d-pad and the face buttons, with additional keybinds accessible by pressing one or both triggers. Each trigger provides access to eight keybinds, with another eight from holding down both triggers at the same time. Combined with the d-pad, you have 28 buttons at your fingertips.  That's a lot of buttons, and while it might not work for every player or be more suited to some classes than others, I was actually shocked how quickly it felt natural, despite having played WoW on a keyboard and mouse for 22 years. There have technically been player-made add-ons that allow full WoW controller support for years, building upon some barebone controller options Blizzard has added in recent years, but this felt easy and intuitive in a way most of those don't. It's currently unclear if controller support will be launching alongside Forever or if it will also be coming to modern WoW, but it certainly opens the door to some fascinating possibilities. Being able to potentially play WoW: Forever on Xbox or on a handheld PC could be revolutionary.  It's that clash of old and new, the adherence to much of what makes WoW Classic so beloved while still pushing the game forward in ways not all players will expect or agree with, that makes Forever such a bold vision for the future. I can't say I'm 100% on board with everything WoW: Forever is doing, but I am excited to experience it when it launches on November 4. I'm even more eager to see where it goes from there. WoW: Forever feels like a fresh starting point for the 22-year-old MMO, taking lessons learned from two entirely different versions of WoW and coalescing them into an almost entirely new one. "What I think we were trying to do is get somewhere, if we can get like 80% of what almost everyone is looking for, or, you know, pretty ubiquitous things that people are looking for, then I think we could call that a success," Greenfield said. He added, "We tried to make it so that it could be as much your Classic+ as possible. We're never going to be anyone's perfect, perfect, perfect game, but that doesn't mean we can't try to hit as many of those big things as possible."

Few movies can truly say they hit the ground running, but Zach Cregger’s Resident Evil is among them. And that’s a good thing, because after so many Resident Evil movies, every new one needs a running start to have a chance of making it. We can’t talk about a Resident Evil movie without doing a little bit of table-setting first, though. Resident Evil debuted on PlayStation in 1996 and coined the name for an emerging game genre: Survival Horror. Since then, Paul W.S. Anderson has directed six live-action Resident Evil movies starring his photogenic and acrobatic wife Milla Jovovich. 2021 brought us the Robbie Amell-led Resident Evil: Welcome to Raccoon City. That’s not counting the half-dozen CGI Resident Evil movies that more or less act as canonical extensions of the game series. Generally speaking, the Resident Evil movies have been on a downward trend since early into Anderson’s tenure. Now, in 2026, the unkillable movie franchise rises again, this time with Zach Cregger, director of Barbarian and Weapons, acting as the head of the monster. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mNd1gb19A-c Resident Evil follows medical courier Bryan (Austin Abrams) as he drops off a package and argues with his girlfriend over the phone. Instead of going home to talk to her about it, though, Bryan jumps at the chance to take a high-priority delivery to Raccoon City. It needs to get there intact, and without any detours. A perfect way to not talk about any personal problems Bryan might have. Bryan starts questioning his decision when he hits a woman in a nightgown while driving in the dark down a slick road in heavy snow. That moment marks the point in which Bryan is in over his head and finds himself in a race to survive. Bryan is not a hero. He is perhaps the least heroic character to lead a Resident Evil movie. And that’s a huge factor in why this disgusting, hilarious, and often frightening movie works. Bryan doesn’t have special forces training, and like so many of us, he’s only ever wielded a gun using a video game controller.  As he curses and fumbles his way through this horrifying night, he’s easy to sympathize with. He’s a sort of self-insert character for the audience, but not in the usual way we think of. What if you sat down to play Resident Evil for the first time but woke up in that world? The vast majority of us would be stuck in a stumbling run, trying to load the last rounds we have with shaking hands. That’s Bryan–always breathless and on the edge of a panic attack. But Bryan never stops, and the movie doesn’t, either. Resident Evil runs at a breakneck pace through the film's entire 95-minute runtime. There are no quiet moments to breathe and reflect: It’s a horrible night where one disaster pushes Bryan into the next one. Each time he’s found something resembling safety, he’s yanked right back into the endless nightmare. It’s a marathon movie in the vein of movies like Mad Max: Fury Road and Run Lola Run. As such, Resident Evil is more of a tense movie than a scary one. There are a handful of great jump scares, of course, and tons of viscera just everywhere. It’s also a very funny movie. Cregger cut his teeth on the sketch comedy show Whitest Kids U Know (he’s in good company with Jordan Peele and Curry Barker as sketch comedy alums making some of the best horror flicks around), and he’s said in interviews that he had to cut tons of jokes. Our protagonist copes with his nightmarish surroundings with humor. The jokes land, but they don’t destroy the tension and terror by any means. In fact, some of the best laughs come when we know something very bad is about to happen, and I still found myself backing into my seat when the bad thing came to pass. Resident Evil fans will find plenty to enjoy here. There are no cameos from any Raccoon Police Department officers or members of STARS, but there are references all over the place. These are things that the average moviegoer might notice, but that only fans will understand. And even the monsters feel like they were created by and for Resident Evil fans. There aren’t actually that many zombies in this movie, though they certainly do help propel it along. Instead of relying on classic Resident Evil monsters, Cregger treats us to mostly original monsters, but ones that feel like they’re in the lineage of the T-Virus. Masses of arms and legs, tentacles that end in teeth, you name it. I felt right at home watching the movie, but it still surprised me with awesome, gnarly, and nauseating monsters around every corner. By backing away from the stories and characters of the video games, Cregger dodges the giant bullet of “that’s not my Leon Kennedy” and instead lets us finally experience Resident Evil ourselves, through our own eyes. The artifice of a campy action movie is gone, and we’re left with a terrified guy who runs from one set of problems, only to encounter something far worse.

Reviewed on: Switch 2 Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Intelligent Systems Release: September 17, 2026 Fire Emblem’s scale has been escalating for some time. From three Fates, to Three Houses, to numerous rings and now, a massive three-act entry for the Switch 2. Those ever-escalating aspirations have led to impressive new heights and ballooning scope. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave is easily one of my favorite settings and stories in the series' history, with memorable battles and characters, even when repetition and overabundance weigh it down.Fortune's Weave starts at the end of all things. You could go challenge the big bad at any moment, but the more lords you assemble, the easier time you'll have. Showing up ready for the finale probably means bringing at least two lords, and their respective recruits, to the final campaign, and in my case, I wanted to get as many through as I could.Fortune's Weave takes on a three-part structure, with each lord getting an individual story through the Heroic Games. They each have their own reasons for fighting: the young Cai wishes to rescue his father from prison and imminent execution; Saraminian queen Theodora wants to reclaim land important to her people; songstress Leda has murder and revenge against her father's killer on her mind; and Dietrich is just in it for the love of battle.It's a little overwrought to explain, but it lends itself well to the size of the tale Fortune's Weave is telling. All four face trials and strife throughout the course of the games. Faustian bargains, shadowy organizations plotting in the background, and old secrets brought to light by the influx of every interesting and notable person in Dagda to its capital. It's not required to play any of the routes, as you could go fight Balor on your own if you wish. But each lord's story adds more context, nuance, and character to this huge tale. I loved moments where I'd see Dietrich and his spell-casting pal Fabio mention something during Cai's path, and then get to see the events from their perspective when I played through them in Dietrich's.Playing through each route is a big task, though. They're made up of chapters, which each have a big battle or two, with free time baked in for you to spend as you'd like in and outside of the capital. The scope starts small, with shared meals to build rapport within your unit, recruiting others to your team, or training skills at the arena. During some periods I'd be stressed out, trying to build up support levels for a new Archer recruit while finishing enough side quests to get the renown level I'd need to get them on my team for a big battle with flying foes coming up. After leading a lord to the end of their path in the Heroic Games, a second section unlocks, exploring their story in a war that breaks out not long after the Games' conclusion. See them through that, and the lord – and any units they recruited – can join you for the final campaign against Balor, which has its own cycle of days, activities, and an extra layer of grand strategy over the top. Pull it all together, and Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave is weighty to see through to the end; it took me over 130 hours to see credits, and I still missed some scenes and events. The payoffs are excellent, and certain story beats getting set up in Part One and paid off in Part Three were incredible. But doing so requires a lot of playtime, investment, and repetition. Dungeons never get more interesting or challenging, collecting specific resources can feel cumbersome, and even exploring the map and managing units starts to slow Fortune's Weave down in its final hours.What kept me going was the world and its characters. Packing them all into a single game might have made this the most intimidating entry to jump into since Fates, but putting it all in a single package with some excellent quality-of-life options, like cross-path progression boosts and side quest clears, helps smooth out the road for those who want to see everything. I've no doubt that those who crave a slow-burn epic and can stomach the repetition for the payoffs will love this world and its cast of characters. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave will easily eat up all your time for months to come, and it's worthwhile to see these Heroic Games through to their conclusion.
