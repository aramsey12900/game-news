Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave Preview - There And Back And There AgainNEW
...
Platform: Publisher: Nintendo Developer: Intelligent Systems Release: September 17, 2026 I went into the opening chapters of Fire Emblem: Fortune’s Weave with close to zero expectations. I knew there was a colosseum and that fights would happen there… and that’s about it. Now, hours into the game, I never could have expected what’s played out thus far. Fortune’s Weave looks back at the series, bringing forward systems from the past like Fire Emblem Gaiden’s dungeon crawling (which Japanese players experienced in 1992 before it was remade in 2017’s Fire Emblem Echoes: Shadows of Valentia); but it also looks forward, literally, with a sci-fi future where magic can be shot out of guns and flashback plotlines make up the primary narrative. It hasn’t quite coalesced into the gripping marriage of strategy gameplay and war and political intrigue typical of the series, but its early hooks have me searching for my Switch 2 any second of game time I can carve into my daily schedule.  Fortune’s Weave wastes no time, throwing you right into a scenario I’m not sure I can entirely explain. But the hero you create using simple, to-the-point character customization options is playable only for a moment. Eventually, after seeing medieval-dressed characters interact with advanced computer systems and magic guns ripped from the future, you will reach an Obelisk Chamber, and a bright-colored goddess (I think?) named Fortuna who speaks on behalf of Sothis, the young goddess from Fire Emblem: Three Houses. Sothis urges you to dive back into the past and rewrite the histories of fabled heroes so that they might help you in the seemingly apocalyptic present. Again, I think. A lot is going on, and what I’ve played in this preview-designated section is clearly just the placing of Fortune’s Weave’s first few chess pieces on the board. There are four obelisks to choose from, each with an associated hero-turned-fallen-warrior-who-has-now-become-an-evil-flame-lord from the past. After selecting one of the four, you’re thrust into a narrative that leads to Dagsion, the Dagdan Empire’s capital. The Heroic Games, Fire Emblem’s spin on ancient Roman colosseum fights and gladiator tournaments, take place here and it is where each obelisk hero plays a special role. Though I’m not privy to those roles yet, it’s been fun seeing how Leda, a magical bard with a bloodlust for her father’s murderer, arrives there.  Leda’s life is ripped away from her as a girl – with her eyes set on revenge, she travels the world in search of clues and her next coin, wowing alehouse patrons with song and dance. I suspect each hero’s story flows into combat the way I’ve experienced it with Leda, who uses melodies from her mystical vihuela to buff nearby allies on the battlefield. That’s exactly what her Blaze Art does, a character-specific move that turns the tide when used correctly. But because it saps HP to do so, when and how you use it in the series’ trademark strategy grid-based battles matters. The same can be said for Combat Arts, which power up specific weapons but chip away at their durability. These two systems create new strings of tension on familiar grids, which is where the classic rock-paper-scissors style of turn-based strategy gameplay returns. It remains satisfying to create forward pushes, flanks, and the like with intentional advances, and the accompanying battle animations are the best they’ve ever looked; the same can be said for all of Fortune’s Weave, which highlights Intelligent Systems' commanding artistic vision with the boon of power that comes from developing exclusively for Switch 2. While I’m happy to see dungeon crawling return, the associated turn-based combat (notably not grid-based battles) is so far underwhelming – a button to fast forward through these fights or skip to the conclusion screen entirely suggests a lack of confidence in these systems.  Game Informer video editor Eric Van Allen deliberately chose a different obelisk when prompted, instead peeking into the history of Cai, a teenager in search of a better life.Cai is an optimistic, Shonen-esque hero who's stumbled into a greater lot in life than he realizes. It seems clear that he and his childhood pals' path through the Heroic Games will probably not be as lighthearted as they hope. Going on excursions outside Dagsion to build up their renown is enjoyable. Quests, hunts for useful resources, or just a quick trip out to see more of the outlying country net some good returns and spur on the idea of Cai slowly becoming as recognizable and formidable as the other lords.There is still so much Fortune’s Weave left to go for both of us, but the hooks are in – it’s time to see where these obelisks take us, and I’m happy to say I have no idea where that will be.

So it's going to be on by default," he said. "Almost every content service in the world is on by default. I think the thing that we are doing here that is unique and different is respecting your wishes to opt-out of model training. I know this is not a fan-favorite. I know it's very upsetting to the community. But this is where we are." On the Twitch website, the company said the new model-training feature uses channel content to train generative AI content models, including streams, VODs, clips, stream chats, and pictures and text broadcast to the channel. Opting-out stops Twitch from doing this on your channel, but as mentioned above, chatting on someone else's stream will still leverage the model-training if that person has not opted out. Additionally, anyone who opts-out to this new feature will still be part of model-training, as Twitch uses AI and machine learning for its AutoMod community-safety service and for AI-supported features like automatically generated captions. It is not possible to opt-out of these offerings, though Twitch said it does not retain user data for other types of model training. You can't opt-out of all of Twitch's AI uses because some AI-supported features are not individual settings, but platform offerings, Twitch said. "Some of it runs the service itself, the same way our servers and our code do. Some of it keeps the service secure. And some of it, like AutoMod, protects the whole community. Turning those off for one person would weaken safety and security for everyone else on the platform, so they are not individual settings," Twitch explained. Twitch's own head of community, GameSpot alum Mary Kish, said she is opting-out of the new AI-training features in part because some of her community members would not join her streams if she opted-in. People are raising other issues, like what happens if a creator streams your game and has opted-in. Or what happens for union-backed voice actors who lobbied for protections against AI usage. The push for further AI training on Twitch is not a big surprise, as its parent company, Amazon, is heavily invested in various pillars of AI. Fire Emblem has always been known for its mechanical density and complexity, but Fortune's Weave applies a force multiplier. With an array of four differentiated but intertwining story campaigns, it feels complex in a way that could prove overwhelming. Having played the first several chapters of two of the campaigns, each had its own wrinkles and elements that could have supported their own standalone campaigns--all composing a sprawling narrative that appears to reward exploring it in full to see every scene and interaction.  Fortune's Weave looks to be a conscious effort to restore some of the magic of Three Houses, following the more divisive Fire Emblem Engage. That means a tale that is more grand and sweeping across multiple factions, including a time-jump element that Nintendo revealed during its recent Direct presentation. At its core, though, this is a story that focuses on four main protagonists, the so-called Flame Lords, with the neat hook that they can be explored in any order, and you can jump between them with mostly unfettered freedom. For the purposes of this preview I started with just two of the four main characters: the dancer Leda and the queen Theodora. And even in the early stages, it was easy to see how this four-quadrant approach holds incredible promise for storytelling, with just a few potential pitfalls. For one, the stories feel well differentiated, even when covering similar sword-and-sandals epic subject matter. My first choice, Leda, begins by witnessing her father's coldblooded execution in the street, thus beginning a revenge quest that completely consumes her. She is obsessed to a fault and often seems to have trouble even relating to people who aren't similarly single-minded and driven. That creates an interpersonal dynamic with her attendants and friends, who understand that this obsession isn't healthy for her, but that on some level she needs to see it through to get closure. Theodora likewise has a tragic past involving the death of her father, but the conditions and her reaction to it are radically different. As the heir to a throne, she's ambushed by her wicked brother, and his loyalists cut off her dominant arm in the process. It's only later, after finding a magical prosthesis--more on that in a bit--that she learns her brother's betrayal included killing her father as well, but it's offscreen and treated as part of his larger regicide plot. She isn't obsessed with vengeance at all. Instead, her story dispatches the wayward brother quickly and then transitions into her goal of proving herself worthy of the crown. Leda, one of the four Flame Lords in Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave Both of them, and ultimately all four of the main protagonists, are drawn toward the Heroic Games, and that's where you start to see factionalism and clashing interests start to arise. They all have their own reasons to be there--Leda gets a lead that her father's killer is competing in the games and sees an opportunity, while Theodora wishes to prove herself against a possible succession battle. Each team competing in the Heroic Games has its own little cadre of warriors with their own relationships to each other. You can even see the other protagonists and their own bands of warriors milling around the grounds as you explore the capital city of Dagsion.  I was able to give gifts to start winning over recruits, which act as small, contained pools of characters to befriend and add to your growing forces. I didn't get far enough to try poaching warriors from the other Flame Lords, so I don't know if that would work or how it would impact their own paths. But the recruitment system looks flexible enough that it could support such a thing.  As you may expect, Leda and Theodora play very different roles in combat as well. Theodora is a fierce queen who uses a spear--in fact, her missing arm was a major issue for using her weapon of choice before she received her magical substitute. Leda is more of a back-line combatant, most proficient in bows. They were both rounded out by a supporting cast to make up for their weaknesses, like Leda's loyal attendant, Buccar; the flirtatious Sirocco; or Theodora's stalwart quartermaster, Bonaventure. The cast is very likable right from the start.  The warriors make camp in Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave They also both have their own unique abilities not shared by any other regular characters, and this is where Fortune's Weave starts to show the real depths of its complexity. Leda is a dancer, while Theodora is a warrior queen, so there's a real contrast between their abilities both on and off the battlefield.  First are their unique Blaze Arts. As a dancer, Leda can cast Spellsongs, providing buffs to her allies in combat, while Theodora has an Earthen Throw attack that can reach across the battlefield in a straight line. Both have powerful effects at the expense of some life. Theodora can also assign Battalions to strengthen her units.  Outside of combat, Leda can learn new Blaze Arts by accepting performance requests at local taverns, and she can travel around the map picking up gigs to perform at local taverns in smaller towns. Sometimes giving a performance will prompt a barfly to give her a tip about a secret spot nearby where she can acquire special materials. Theodora's abilities, meanwhile, focus on her role as a leader of her people, letting her deploy battalions to search for supplies and send supplies back home to help her people. The two are also linked, indirectly, by the presence of cursed objects. Leda has an instrument that is said to be teeming with dark energy, while Theodora's arm itself--taken from the resting place of a legendary hero of her people--is said to be cursed. In both cases these work thematically to emphasize their stories, symbolizing Leda's willingness to use any dangerous tool at her disposal to enact her vengeance, and showing Theodora's need to prove herself through physical strength. But early as I am, I suspect there will be more to both of these, and other similarly cursed objects across the other two protagonist stories.  A meeting at the Heroic Games in Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave Once you reach Dagsion and begin the Heroic Games, the structure of the game reveals itself. You're scheduled to take part in qualifier rounds on particular days, which gives you a certain amount of time to explore the town and surrounding cities taking part in sidequests, skirmishes, and to give gifts to potential recruits, between major story events.  In the thick of combat, this is through and through recognizable as a Fire Emblem game, with all of the density of gameplay the strategy RPG series name has come to suggest. You'll be carefully positioning units, drawing enemies in to be flanked, and experimenting with different combinations of job classes as you buy licenses to unlock more advanced units. The unique powers of each protagonist gives them their own sense of combat identity and makes them feel special.  Aside from the traditional strategy battles, there are also sporadic dungeon-crawling segments with more traditional RPG combat, which appear randomly as you explore the map, and as occasional story events. I found these to be fairly simplistic for now, but they were an okay diversion and another way to gather resources aside from simply using the search function while exploring the map. And as the game continues those may blossom into a more engaging diversion, though if they remain too simple I could foresee them becoming an annoyance, or just skipping them altogether. Training units in the Auto-Advance menu in Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave It altogether feels a little overwhelming. But that feeling actually worked to soften one of the rougher edges of this game's structure for me. Since you have a choice of four stories that all run in parallel, and you can hop between them freely, you're essentially doing multiple different versions of early-game missions. That could risk making the game feel fairly flat at the beginning, when the challenge level is still at its most gentle. I haven't done this, but in theory you could play Leda's path to completion and then start all over with Theodora's, which would probably feel like a hard reset. But instead I played the first few chapters of each back to back, and I plan to keep swapping between them. That means that, at first, I was staying at a steady, very easy challenge. It also had the odd side-effect of repeating a handful of optional introductory quests from a quest board, like finding the item and weapon shop vendors in town. But that gentle introduction with some elements repeated actually worked in favor of Fortune's Weave in an unexpected way. Since the world is so dense and complex and with so much to do, and so many factions with their own agendas, the presence of some repetition helped reinforce the aspects that are the same between the campaigns, and gave me a better sense of what was different between them as well. I'll have to see how my patience holds out for doing it twice more, if I choose to play all four at all, but at least at first, this was a welcome way to learn the ropes. The grand city of Dagsion in Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave Another way that Fortune's Weave seems likely to soften the edges of its multiple campaigns is through the use of Boons of Salvation. By completing Milestones, you unlock karma shards that can be spent on Boons that apply universally across all four paths. Those include things like an XP Boost, increased damage output with particular weapon types, or discounted items. So in theory you could use your progress in some chapters to create a glide path for the next one. That wouldn't skip over the more simplistic early-game elements but it would likely make them quicker to breeze through and get to the more substantial tactical combat. Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave feels absolutely massive. It's dense in a way that few other RPGs are, and the story being structured around four separate heroes with their own stories and abilities sets makes it even more complex still. The four parallel stories also feel reminiscent of the various schools in Three Houses. That game is widely seen as the recent high-water mark of the series, and while Fortune's Weave doesn't seem to have quite the same Persona-like social hooks, the array of specialized abilities make the four main characters feel distinct, both in and out of combat. It's so vast and sprawling that it will take many more hours to reach a final evaluation, but what I've played so far is a very promising start that could put it among the best the series has to offer.

Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls Review - The Fighting-Game Comeback Marvel DeservesNEW
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 ...
Reviewed on: PlayStation 5 Platform: PlayStation 5, PC Publisher: PlayStation Studios Developer: Arc System Works Release: August 6, 2026 Despite its origins as a comic book publisher, Marvel is fighting game royalty on par with the likes of Mortal Kombat and The King of Fighters. But after the disappointing Marvel vs. Capcom Infinite fell massively short of expectations, and with the release of Arc System Works' Dragon Ball FighterZ giving hyper tag-team fighting game fans a new home, Marvel's reign in the fighting game world felt at its end. Just shy of a decade since Infinite, however, Arc System Works delivers Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls, a fighter that is as much of an advancement to the genre as it is an homage to the Marvel vs. Capcom era.Marvel Tōkon is a 4v4 fighting game, but it’s no ordinary tag fighter. Each team shares a single health bar, but you can effectively play this as a 1v1 fighting game where your team's only purpose is to come in for assists. This helps retain the high-octane fighting fans are accustomed to without the need to memorize four entire movesets. Super and special moves are also assigned to simple button presses alongside more standard fighting game inputs, making it easier to pick up any fighter.Matches have a unique rhythm as they start with your main character and one assist, then you gradually gain access to your additional teammates. Not only does this style allow drastic momentum shifts, but it encourages you to be on the attack to gain control of your entire arsenal as soon as possible, making matches exhilarating at times.  While the hard-fought matches I played online aptly demonstrate the combo system’s depth, it’s still approachable. Despite my typical struggles with longer combos, Marvel Tōkon’s character-agnostic control uniformity helps keep the combat approachable for a wider audience. The characters are instead differentiated by unique traits and dedicated specials, which range from Doctor Doom creating barriers to Green Goblin summoning his glider.Arc System Works is known for over-the-top fighting and absolutely gorgeous visuals, and Marvel Tōkon sits alongside Guilty Gear Strive as one of the best-looking fighting games to date. Arc's signature 3D models that look like 2D drawings remain as impressive as they were back when we first saw Guilty Gear Xrd. Just as Dragon Ball FighterZ advanced on what Xrd did, Tōkon feels like another visual jump.A major part of the visual praise belongs to the character designs, which are all unique, fantastic, and tasteful. The anime-inspired style just works for this game; Iron Man, for example, has armor that looks ripped out of a Mecha anime, while Hulk’s exaggerated proportions and armor make him as huge and intimidating as he deserves to be. Story mode comes in the form of Episodes about teams in a worldwide tournament to take on a cosmic entity, The Champion, who has been travelling the universe and ending planets he deems unworthy. These narratives are told through visual novel scenes featuring the pre-set teams interspersed with fights. Each Episode takes around an hour to beat, and they’re all basic and predictable. Most stories follow the same format aside from a few differences in the Knights of Doom's path.  Episodes also don't serve as a tutorial in the same way story modes in fighting games sometimes do, with only very basic introductions to each character. Tōkon does have fairly in-depth training and tutorial options, but the character-specific tutorials are little more than some combo trials. They are useful, but don't really teach you the full mentality and gameplans behind each character. Online is standard for Arc games: lobbies, ranked and casual matches, plus a silly eight-player side mode called Arcadia Rumble. The netcode is great, with barely any noticeable issues when I've played, even over Wi-Fi. A fighting game's impact is difficult to determine at launch due to balancing updates and the community's approach to it. Given the package Arc has delivered here, though, Marvel Tōkon is a more than worthy successor to Marvel vs. Capcom and Dragon Ball FighterZ. I believe it has the potential to become a fighting game mainstay for years to come. Score: 8.5 About Game Informer's review system
